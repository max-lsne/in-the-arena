In the arena · 2026-09-01 · one of three on preload

Shroud · noun · /ʃraʊd/ — one of the standing wires that stay a mast athwartships, set up under tension against its fellow so the mast is held from both sides at once; from Old English scrūd, clothing, that which is drawn tight about a thing

Shroud

A mast does not stand because a wire holds it up. It stands because two wires hold it from both sides at once — port and starboard — each drawn taut against the other before a breath of wind arrives. Cast off one and the mast does not lean gently; it whips to the other side and jumps in its step. The rig is not a thing that resists the wind. It is a thing set into tension against itself beforehand, so the wind has nothing left to slacken.

The rigger sets up the shrouds: hardens the bottlescrews until both wires sing, putting a heavy tension into each and, through them, a heavy compression down the mast into its step. That set-up tension is the preload. Now a gust heels the boat: it adds to the windward shroud and takes from the lee. And here is the whole discipline — the lee shroud may lose tension, but it must never reach zero. A shroud gone dead-slack is a mast let go on one side: it snaps to leeward, the wire fetches up with a bang, and the masthead pumps and works until something lets go. Set the rigging taut enough that even the hardest gust only draws the lee shroud toward slack and never through it, and the mast is held, both sides, all the way to the rail.

The problem the set-up solves

A stay that must never go dead under a load that gusts

A wire is strong in tension and worth nothing in compression — it cannot push, only pull. A single shroud could hold the mast against a wind from one side, but the wind changes side with every tack and every gust, and a wire that has gone slack cannot catch the mast until it has already fallen away and gathered speed. The load on a rig is not steady; it comes in blows, and it reverses. What the rig must never do is present the mast a moment with no wire holding it to windward — a free instant in which the mast starts to move before anything stops it.

So the rigger never lets a shroud go dead. He sets both up so taut that each begins its life pulling hard, and arranges that the worst a gust can do is let the lee shroud pull a little less. There is always tension in the wire; the mast is always caught. What would have been a spar leaning on whichever wire happened to be tight becomes a spar clamped upright between two wires that are never both allowed to rest.

The mast is stayed not by the wire that takes the gust but by the wire on the other side that was tightened before the gust, and never quite lets go.

The one relation it embodies

The gust takes from the lee; the shroud holds until it is gone

Take the two shrouds as springs pulling against each other across the mast, both set up to the preload T₀ when the bottlescrews are hardened. A gust puts a heeling load P on the rig. How it divides depends on the base — how far outboard the chainplates are led: a wide base shares the load kindly, a narrow one throws it all on the lee. Write φ for the windward share; then only (1−φ) comes off the lee:

  • The windward shroud hardens — T_wind = T₀ + φ·P. It takes up the gust, and if the rigging is set up well it does so without ever coming near its breaking strain.
  • The lee shroud goes soft — T_lee = T₀ − (1−φ)·P. The gust bleeds straight off the tension that was holding the mast from that side. This is the number that must never reach zero.
  • The lee shroud dies when its tension reaches zero — at P_sep = T₀ /(1−φ). Below it, both wires alive and the mast held both sides. At it, the lee goes dead, and the next roll snaps the mast to leeward against a slack wire.

Two lessons in three lines. The preload T₀ sets how hard a gust the rig can take before the lee dies — set up harder, stand more. And the base φ sets how much of the gust the lee has to shed: chainplates led well outboard keep φ large and (1−φ) small, so the lee loses its tension slowly and stays alive far into the blow.

Set T₀ so P_sep sits past the hardest gust the boat will ever carry sail in, and the lee shroud never dies, and the mast is never once let go.

Why keep it

Held by the wire that was tightened first

The shroud is worth a page because it makes a spar stand up out of two things that can only pull and never push. It never asks a wire to catch a mast that has begun to move; it spends a heavy tension into both wires beforehand, at the bottlescrews, so that the gust arrives to find the mast already clamped upright between them and can do nothing but let one wire off a little. The rig is sound not because any wire is strong but because its tension was paid up front — and every pound the gust brings is a pound coming off a margin already set.

It is the same trick the cooper plays when driven hoops squeeze a cask watertight before a drop is poured, and the wheelwright plays when the shrunk tyre jams every spoke home before the cart rolls. All three are the one idea — preload — that the way to keep a join from ever failing is to load it hard, on purpose, before the world gets to. And it keeps company with the purchase that sets up a tackle before the strain comes on, the ballast that answers the same wind by lowering the weight it acts on, and the holdfast that grips by the load it is put under: more ways of settling the dangerous moment in advance.
