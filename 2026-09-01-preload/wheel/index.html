In the arena · 2026-09-01 · one of three on preload

Wheel · noun · /wiːl/ — a load-bearing ring of felloes and spokes bound by an iron tyre shrunk on hot, so the whole assembly stands in permanent compression; here in the wheelwright's sense, a wheel made sound by its bond, not its timber

Wheel

A wheel carries nothing by the strength of its spokes. Cut the tyre and the spokes stand loose in their sockets, and the first pothole shakes the whole thing to a heap of sticks. What carries the cart is a thing done to the wheel before it ever turns: the wheelwright shrinks the tyre on.

The iron tyre is forged a shade too small — a full turn of the wheel too short. Heated to a dull red it swells just enough to drop over the felloes; quenched with water it snaps back, and shrinking, it crushes the whole wheel inward, driving every spoke tight into its mortise and every felloe hard against its neighbour. The wheel now stands in a great ring of compression before a single pound of cart has come onto it. That is the preload. Now load the axle: the weight comes down through the hub and out along the spokes to the ground — and at the bottom of the wheel it comes straight off the preload. A spoke there is squeezed a little less; it is unloaded, never pulled slack. And a spoke that never goes slack never rattles in its socket, never works, never splits the felloe. Shrink the tyre hard enough and the wheel stays sound whatever the cart weighs.

The problem the tyre solves

A joint that must never come loose under a load that pounds

A cartwheel lives a violent life. Every turn brings the load onto a fresh spoke and takes it off again; every stone jars the rim; the timber swells in the wet and shrinks in the dry. A spoke is only pushed into a mortise, and a mortise that is ever allowed to open — to let its spoke go loose, even for an instant — begins to hammer. The loose spoke knocks on every turn, wears its shoulders round, splits the felloe, and the wheel unravels from that one joint. The failure is never the strength of the spoke; it is the moment a joint goes slack.

So the wheelwright never lets a joint go slack. He shrinks the tyre so hard that every spoke and every felloe begins its life clamped, and arranges that the worst a rolling load can do is clamp it a little less. The joints stay shut; the spokes stay home; nothing knocks. What would have been a wheel held together by the fit of its joints becomes a wheel held together by a preload no load can undo.

The wheel's whole life of pounding is answered once, at the fire, in the shrinking of one iron ring — and after that the road can only relax what the smith already drove tight.

The one relation it embodies

The load comes off the preload; the spoke holds until it is gone

Take the loaded spoke at the bottom of the wheel and its felloe as two springs pulling against each other: the ring of iron and rim above (in tension) and the spoke and its joint (in compression), both set to the same preload F₀ when the tyre shrinks on. The cart adds a load P pressing down onto that spoke. Rim and spoke share the give, so only a fraction φ = k_rim /(k_rim + k_spoke) of each added pound goes to the rim; the rest comes off the spoke's clamp:

  • The rim tightens a little — F_rim = F₀ + φ·P. The iron feels only the small share φ of the cart, so a tyre shrunk on taut barely notices the load and barely fatigues over a life of turns.
  • The spoke's clamp bleeds away — F_spoke = F₀ − (1−φ)·P. Most of the load comes off the compression that jams the spoke home. This is the number that must never reach zero.
  • The spoke goes slack when the clamp reaches zero — at P_sep = F₀ /(1−φ). Below it, the spoke is jammed home and never sees a loose instant. At it, the joint opens, and one turn later it knocks.

Two lessons in three lines. The preload F₀ sets how heavy a cart the wheel can carry before a spoke goes slack — shrink it harder, carry more. And the split φ sets how much of the road's endless pounding the iron itself has to feel: a stiff rim against a spoke that gives keeps φ small, so the clamp takes the beating, which timber in compression can shrug off forever.

It is the same trick the wire wheel plays upside down: there the rim is squeezed and the spokes are strung in tension, and a bicycle stands not on its bottom spokes but hung from the top — because the load at the bottom does nothing but let the lower spokes off their tension, never adding to it. Preload, either way round.

The clamp the smith shrinks into every joint before the cart rolls. It is the whole margin: the wheel can carry a load right up to P_sep = F₀/(1−φ) before a spoke goes slack. A tyre cut shorter shrinks harder and pushes the separation load past the heaviest cart; a slack tyre and a half-load rattles a spoke.

How the load divides between stretching the iron rim and relieving the spoke's clamp. A stiff rim against a giving spoke keeps φ small, so the load comes almost entirely off the clamp and the iron barely flutters — kind to a wheel that pounds down a road for years. A limp rim makes the iron feel every stone.

Why keep it

The road can only relax what the fire drove tight

The wheel is worth a page because it answers a whole life of pounding with a single act at the fire. It never asks the fit of its joints to survive the road; it spends a great compression into the timber beforehand, in the shrinking of one iron ring, so that the road arrives to find every joint already clamped and can do nothing but relax it a little. The wheel is sound not because its spokes are strong but because its stress was paid up front — and every pound the cart brings is a pound coming off a debt already settled.

It is the same trick the cooper plays when driven hoops squeeze a cask's staves watertight before a drop is poured, and the rigger plays when the shrouds are set up so taut the mast is held from both sides at once. All three are the one idea — preload — that the way to keep a join from ever failing is to load it hard, on purpose, before the world gets to. And it keeps company with the seam that carries load across a long lap not a line, and the involute tooth that obeys the law of gearing by its shape alone: more ways of settling the hard question in advance, in the geometry, before the load is ever felt.
