The heap grows on its own. Every day another spadeful goes on — the favour you said yes to, the idea you couldn't bear to drop, the meeting that might matter, the someday-project you can't quite bury. Nothing here is bad. It is all just soil and grit tipped together, and because you can't tell by looking which clods will hold, you keep the lot. So you try to carry the whole barrow at once, and what you carry is mostly fine stuff that should have gone to the bed, mud that slows the spade and hides the few things worth the saving.

The riddle is the other way. You don't sort the heap by staring at it; you put it on the mesh and you shake, and the sieve does the deciding for you. The fine falls through and is let go — no guilt, no eulogy, it was always going to the bed. What won't pass rides to the top, and that is your answer: the deliberate few, knocked off into the keep-pile, clean of all the loose stuff that was never going to hold. You cannot keep every clod. How it works

  1. 01

    Tip the whole heap on the mesh

    Shovel everything onto the riddle at once — every ask, idea and half-commitment, the good with the grit, no pre-sorting. You can't tell what holds by looking, so don't try. Name the heap, get it all up on the wires, and let the sieve be the thing that decides instead of you.

  2. 02

    Shake it and let the fine fall through

    Work the riddle side to side and let gravity do the triage. The fine soil finds the mesh and drops away in a haze to the bed below — the small stuff that was never going to hold. Each shake is a count. You let it go without a second look; it was always headed there.

  3. 03

    See what rides up and won't pass

    Keep shaking and the few that matter climb to the top — the stones, the roots, the clods too big for the mesh. These are the deliberate ones, the things that wouldn't fall through however hard you shook. The heap is suddenly readable: not a hundred maybes, just the handful that held.

  4. 04

    Knock the kept few into the pile

    Tip the caught few off the mesh into the keep-pile, clean of all the loose stuff. That small, deliberate heap is what you actually carry — the two meetings that hold, the one idea worth building, the root saved for replanting. Riddled clean, the work that mattered fits the barrow.

The four stages

  • Heap. Straight off the spade, loose on the barrow, not yet on the mesh. The dozen maybes tipped in together, fine and coarse all at once. You can't tell what holds by looking; it has to be shaken first.
  • Riddled. Up on the wires and being worked, the fine soil starting to fall through. The ask you've begun to shake down rather than swallow whole. In the sieve at last, but nowhere near sorted.
  • Caught. Shaken a good while, riding up on top, plainly too big for the mesh. A commitment that has proven it won't pass — the loose stuff around it gone to the bed, the thing itself standing clear.
  • Kept. Knocked off into the keep-pile, the deliberate few you carry. The stone for the rockery, the root saved to replant, the one project that held. Riddled clean and worth the barrow.

Who sorts for real

The gardener clearing a new bed

Spadeful by spadeful she throws the turned earth onto the riddle propped over the barrow and shakes. The fine tilth drops through to dress the bed; the flints and bindweed root ride up to be flung in the bucket. She never picks stones by hand any more. The mesh does the sorting, and her hands only carry what the heap could not let through.

The mason screening sand for mortar

Coarse pit-sand goes on the riddle and the clean grade falls through to the board; the pebbles and clay lumps stay on top and go back to the heap. He has a frame for every grade, and he trusts the wire, not his eye. A mix is only as good as what you let fall through and what you had the sense to keep out.

The grower saving seed from the threshed

The threshed heads go over the sieve and she works it in the wind — the chaff and dust sift away on the draught, the heavy true grain rides and is kept back for next year's drills. Everything light enough to lose, she lets the riddle lose. What stays on the mesh is the only part worth carrying to the store.

The lead with too many maybes

Every quarter the asks pile up — calls, ideas, favours, someday-projects, all tipped on the same barrow. He stopped grading them one by one. Now he shakes the whole heap against one plain question — will this still hold in a month? — and lets the rest fall through. Three things ride up. Those three he keeps, and the barrow is light.
