A rookery is what comes back.

A rookery is a colony of rooks — a dozen pairs, sometimes three hundred — built in the high forks of the same beeches or elms, year on year, generation on generation. In March they patch the old cup with new twigs. In April they raise a clutch. By August the trees stand quiet. Next spring the same birds, or their daughters, come back to the same fork in the same elm. The pile grows another inch deeper.

Most days the work is a single bird in a wide sky, trying to remember which elm is the right elm and which fork is the right fork, and arriving at a different one every evening, or no fork at all, or one already taken by a stranger. Each morning's flight is a fresh question. The sky has no memory.

A rookery is the other thing. A rookery is what keeps coming back. Each year the same bird returns to the same fork in the same elm and patches last year's cup with new twigs from the wood below. The pile grows another inch deeper. The colony grows another nest broader. A century of returning has made a parish landmark out of birds you can't tell apart.

How it works

  1. 01

    Pick the fork

    The fork is the one fork in the one elm you mean to come back to every year. Not all your trees. One. The corner of the desk where the half-page note is started. The same chair at the same hour. The fork you are going to patch year on year, patient and unhurried.

  2. 02

    Wedge the first twig

    Today, wedge the first twig. Not a nest. Not a cup. One twig. A line of the morning entry, a single open-string scale, the empty notebook with one entry on the first page. The wind could still take it. That is fine. There is no nest yet.

  3. 03

    Come back next March

    And the March after. Each year, patch the cup with new twigs from the wood below. The first patch is a finished cup — one season, one pair. The fifth patch is several nests in the bough. The fiftieth is a colony, a parish landmark, audible at first light from half a mile.

  4. 04

    Roost in the rookery

    One spring the rookery has banks of nests and a parliament of returning birds. You come back to the same fork. The cup remembers you. The work is held by something larger than your one season. The colony walks the year for you.

The four perches

  • Twig. A single twig wedged in the fork — the first half-page, the empty notebook with one entry, the line of code that is the only line of code. Could blow down in a wind.
  • Cup. A finished nest, one season's work — the first complete draft, the first finished chapter, the first piece of practice carried end to end. Holds a clutch.
  • Bough. Several nests in one tree — a body of work, a small archive, a corpus the same hands have built up patiently over a few years. Visibly inhabited.
  • Rookery. The full colony, twenty trees, three hundred nests — a lifework, a tradition, a school of practice, a parish landmark visible from a mile off.

Who comes back to the same elm

The poet at the morning notebook

Two lines of the morning entry every March for twenty-eight years. Twig at first, cup by year three, bough by year ten. Three notebooks in the bough now. The fork remembers her.

The cellist at the open-string scale

A single open-string scale, forty seconds, every dawn since 2019. The first cup is complete. A second is being patched on the bough above. The sentry calls from a low branch most mornings.

The gardener at the long bed

Forty seasons of the same long bed of perennials. Cathedral elm, twelve nests, three hundred clutches raised. Patching the cup with new twigs each March. The parish walks past and looks up.

The translator at the same desk

Two pages of the slow Italian every morning since 2012. A bough now, three cups, the topmost patched fourteen years running. Sounds at first light. The wood below provides the twigs.
