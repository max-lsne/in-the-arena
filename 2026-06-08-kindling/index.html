A kindling pile is what the morning of a working life needs more than any other tool. Not the big log. Not the bellows. The single dry stick small enough to catch when nothing else will. Kindling is a small page for the practice of keeping one — a one-sentence draft, a five-minute warm-up, an opened file — ready in arm's reach for the morning that comes cold.

Most mornings

Most productivity asks what the big thing is. The big thing is rarely the hard thing. The hard thing is the first thing. At six in the dark, in the cold, nothing wants to catch. Whether or not a working day begins is not decided by your discipline; it is decided by what is within arm's reach when you sit down. Big logs do not catch from a match.

A kindling pile is a small dry thing you put aside on the warm afternoons of last week, against the cold morning of this one. A one-sentence draft kept at the bottom of the notebook. A five-minute version of the work, already begun. The file closed at a place that opens easily again. The pile is patience put into small dry sticks.

How it works

  1. 01

    Cut a small stick

    One smallest possible start — a single sentence, a five-minute version, a file opened where the work already is. Small enough to catch when nothing else will. The smaller the stick, the more days it works for.

  2. 02

    Choose a pile

    Tinder for the smallest stuff. Twigs for the small first acts. Splits for the half-built pieces. Logs for the substantial work. The pile is sorted by what it takes to catch.

  3. 03

    Let it dry

    A stick fresh-cut still has the wet of the day. A week or two on the pile and it loses the wet of haste. Bone-dry sticks take a spark at a breath. The dryness is the practice.

  4. 04

    Use the smallest one first

    The cold morning is not the morning to chop a cord. It is the morning for the tinder you kept dry. Reach for the smallest dry thing first; let the small fire take the next one up. The pile catches itself.

The four piles

  • Tinder. The smallest possible start — a single sentence already in the notebook, a single push-up, the laptop opened the night before at the work. Catches at a breath. The pile under everything else.
  • Twigs. Small first acts — a five-minute draft, a rough outline, the walk to the desk. Will not warm the room alone; carries the spark to the splits.
  • Splits. Half-built work waiting to be picked up — drafts under their last line, sketches with one corner finished, the file open at the place you left it on a good day.
  • Logs. The substantial work that catches once the small fires are going — the long projects, the deep hours. Needs the rest of the pile already in flame.

Who keeps a kindling pile

The novelist at the door of a cold morning

A one-sentence draft laid every Sunday night, on a card pinned to the desk. Eleven years now, five hundred and seventy-two cards. The cold Monday is not a morning for drafting; it is a morning for the card that was already drafted on Sunday. The morning catches from the card.

The runner who keeps the first eighty steps

The shoes laid by the door the night before. The first eighty steps practised so often they are a single piece of kindling, dry and ready. The hard run begins after step eighty-one. The first eighty are the match.

The composer at the piano in the dark

A single scale laid on the keyboard each Sunday, untimed, before the first piece of any week. A tiny dry thing that has nothing to do with the music. It is the spark the music catches from. The scale is not the work; the scale is the kindling.

The researcher at the start of an afternoon

The next paragraph already marked in the manuscript before lunch. The terminal already at the file. The browser already at the tab. Three tiny dry sticks placed so the afternoon does not have to find a match. The afternoon catches.
