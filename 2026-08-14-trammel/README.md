# Trammel

**Hold one point of a rigid rod so it can only slide along a horizontal groove,
and another point so it can only slide along a vertical one. The rod is now
trapped between two rules, free to lean but not to wander. Mark any third point
on it and turn the rod: that mark cannot help but draw a perfect ellipse. There
is no string, no loop around two pins, no arithmetic in the hand — only two
grooves crossed at a right angle and one stiff rod laid across them. The reason
is short. Put the grooves on the axes; then the pin on the upright groove keeps
its x at zero and the pin on the flat groove keeps its y at zero, and a mark set
a from the first pin and b from the second sits, when the rod leans at angle t,
at exactly (a·cos t, b·sin t) — which is the ellipse x²/a² + y²/b² = 1. The reach
to the upright pin is the half-width; the reach to the flat pin is the
half-height. This is the trammel of Archimedes, the ellipsograph a draughtsman
kept beside the compasses, and its rule is exact, not stepped off by eye.**

The word *trammel* is a worker's, worn to several jobs: a beam-compass for
scribing curves too big for dividers, a three-walled fishing net, an adjustable
pot-hook swung over a fire, a hobble strapped to a horse's legs. What they share
is a thing held to a fixed path — which is exactly what two grooves do to a rod.
To be *trammelled* is to be held to a track; the ellipsograph holds a point to
the one track that two square constraints allow.

## The one shape

A point in the plane has two freedoms. Take one away with each groove and it has
none left to spare — except that the two constraints are *sliding*, not fixed, so
what they remove is not the point's position but its wandering. One freedom
survives, bent into a closed loop, and the loop is an ellipse because the grooves
pull in directions square to each other.

- The **two half-axes are the two reaches.** The mark's distance to the pin on the
  upright groove is the ellipse's half-width a; its distance to the pin on the
  flat groove is the half-height b. You can point at both numbers on the rod
  before it turns. Nothing is fitted; the geometry hands you the curve.
- **Equal reaches make a circle.** Slide the mark to where a = b and both pins fold
  onto the origin; the rod just spins about the centre with the mark swung out at
  a fixed radius. It is the simplest circle-drawer there is — a stick turning
  between two grooves, no centre pin driven into the paper.
- **A zero reach makes a straight line.** Slide the mark right onto one pin and one
  half-axis vanishes; the mark, now sharing that pin's groove, runs dead straight
  out and back. A machine built from two circles' worth of constraint, drawing a
  true straight line for free — the same line the Tusi couple draws, which is the
  trammel read from its centre.

The bargain is that you buy the whole family of ellipses with a single slide.
Between the circle at one limit and the straight line at the other lies every
ellipse, and you move among them by walking the mark along the rod — no new
mechanism, no template swapped, only the reach changing. The cost is only this:
the rod must be rigid and the grooves truly square, or the curve skews; a
constraint that slips is a curve that lies.

The page is one trammel, turned the way the hand turns it: two grooves crossed on
the bench, a rod held by a pin in each, and a green mark that lays down its trace
as the rod goes round. Set the **reach to each pin** — the half-width and the
half-height — and **turn** the rod by hand or let it run; it reads back the axes,
the eccentricity, the area, and which of the three shapes you have made, lighting
the mark when it arrives at a circle or a straight line. The numbers are the real
ones: the mark is at (a·cos t, b·sin t), the locus is x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, and the
whole family lies along the rod, after Archimedes.
