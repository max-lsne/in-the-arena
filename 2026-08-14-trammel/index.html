trammel · noun · /ˈtramɪl/ — a rod held by two sliding pins, one running a horizontal groove and one a vertical, so a marked point on the rod is compelled to draw an ellipse; also a beam-compass, a net, a pot-hook — anything that holds a thing to a fixed path

Trammel

Hold one point of a rod so it can only slide left and right, and another so it can only slide up and down. Now mark any third point on the rod and turn it. That point has no choice: it draws a perfect ellipse.

There is no string, no pair of pins with a loop around them, no arithmetic in the hand — only two grooves crossed at a right angle and one stiff rod laid across them. Each pin is a rule the rod must obey: this end stays on the flat line, that end stays on the upright one. Between two such rules a marked point is left exactly one freedom, and the shape of that freedom is an ellipse whose half-width is the marked point's distance to the pin on the upright groove and whose half-height is its distance to the pin on the flat one. This is the trammel of Archimedes, the ellipsograph a draughtsman kept in the drawer beside the compasses; and its rule is exact, not drawn by eye. Slide the marked point until the two distances are equal and the ellipse rounds into a true circle. Slide it onto one of the pins and the ellipse flattens to a straight line the point runs back and forth along — a rod that draws a straight line out of nothing but two that cross.

Where the ellipse comes from

Two constraints, one freedom, and the freedom is an ellipse

Lay the flat groove along the x-axis and the upright groove along the y-axis, crossing at the origin. Put a pin of the rod on each: pin A is trapped on the upright groove, so its x is always zero; pin B is trapped on the flat groove, so its y is always zero. The rod is rigid, so as it leans over at an angle t the two pins slide to keep their grooves — A rides up and down the y-axis, B slides in and out along the x-axis, and the rod pivots between them like the hand of a clock that has lost its centre.

Mark a point P on the rod at distance a from pin A and distance b from pin B, both measured along the rod. A line of plain trigonometry — no more than resolving that distance into its across and up parts — puts P at exactly (a·cos t, b·sin t). And that pair, as t runs round, is the ellipse itself: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1. The reach to the upright pin sets the half-width; the reach to the flat pin sets the half-height. The rod has done the drawing.

So the two numbers you can point at on the rod — how far the mark sits from each pin — are the two half-axes of the ellipse. Nothing is fitted or guessed; the geometry hands you the curve, exact, from the moment the pins are in their grooves.

It is the same bargain as any linkage that trades freedoms for a path. Two constraints on a plane point leave it one degree of freedom, and here that one degree is bent into a closed loop because the two grooves pull in directions square to each other. Tilt the grooves off square and the loop tilts and skews with them; keep them square and it stays an upright ellipse, waiting only for the reach of the mark to say how tall and how wide.

The two ends of the one rule

Equal reaches make a circle; a zero reach makes a straight line

Because the half-axes are just the two reaches, the whole family of ellipses is laid out along the rod, and you walk between them by sliding the mark. Bring the mark to where its distance to each pin is the same — a = b — and the ellipse rounds up into a circle, both pins folding onto the origin so the rod simply spins about the centre with the mark swung out at a fixed radius. This is the plainest circle-drawer there is: no centre pin driven into the paper, just a stick turning between two grooves.

Slide the mark the other way, right onto one of the pins, and one reach goes to zero. The ellipse has no height left, or no width left, and collapses to a straight line — the marked point, now sharing a pin's groove, simply runs the length of the other rod out and back. A machine of two circles' worth of constraint, drawing a dead-straight line.

That last case is the quiet marvel. Turning rotary motion into true straight-line motion was a real problem for the early engine-builders, solved with clever chains of links; the trammel does it for free, as a corner of the same rule that draws every ellipse between. The Tusi couple — a small circle rolling inside one twice its size — draws the very same straight line, and is the trammel seen from the centre. One mechanism, read three ways: an ellipse in the middle, a circle at one limit, a line at the other, and only the reach of the mark to choose between them.

The bench

One rod, two grooves, and a mark that cannot help but trace

Turn the rod and watch the marked point lay down its curve. Set how far the mark sits from each pin — its reach to the upright groove is the ellipse's half-width, its reach to the flat groove the half-height — and read back the axes, the eccentricity, the area, and which of the three shapes you have made. Bring the two reaches level for a circle; run one to zero for a straight line. The numbers are the real ones: P is at (a·cos t, b·sin t), and the locus is x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, after Archimedes.

Why keep it

An exact curve, argued from two straight rules

The trammel is worth a page because it makes a hard thing — a curve of two different radii, changing its bend everywhere along its length — out of the two simplest constraints there are: stay on this line, stay on that one. No template, no string with its stretch and slip, no table of points to join. Just a rigid rod and the fact that two square constraints leave a point one freedom shaped like an ellipse. Draughtsmen cut them from brass; machinists still meet the same geometry in the elliptic chuck and the Scotch yoke; and every student who has been shown the (a·cos t, b·sin t) parametrisation has, without always knowing it, been handed the trammel in symbols.

Turn the bench until the reasoning is in the hand and not just the eye: the mark cannot wander, cannot be coaxed, cannot draw anything but the one ellipse its reaches allow — and slides between circle and straight line as smoothly as you move the mark along the rod.
