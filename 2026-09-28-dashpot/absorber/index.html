absorber · noun · /əbˈzɔːbə/ — the oil-filled damper set across a vehicle's spring to take the energy out of a bump; not the spring that holds the car up, but the thing that stops it bouncing

Absorber

The spring holds the car up. The absorber decides whether a bump settles or bobs away.

People call the whole strut a "shock absorber," but the spring absorbs nothing — it stores the bump and hands it straight back, and a car on springs alone would pogo down the road for a hundred yards after every pothole. The absorber is the other part: a dashpot, a piston forced through oil, that resists the body's motion in proportion to how fast it moves and turns that motion into heat. Fit too little and the body floats — it wallows on the spring and, worse, the tyre skips off the road on the rebound and grip goes with it. Fit too much and the damper locks the body to the wheel, so every sharp bump comes straight up through the seat. Between them is one setting where a bump dies in a single motion.

Where the ride comes from

The spring stores, the dashpot spends

Hit a bump and the wheel drives up into the body; the spring compresses, storing the blow, and then pushes back — and, left alone, pushes the body up past level, whereupon gravity and the spring pull it back down past level, and so on. A spring is a bank that never charges a fee: it returns all it takes, so the bounce, once started, has no reason to stop. Something must lose the energy, and lose it fastest when the body moves fastest. That is the dashpot.

A spring gives the bump back. A dashpot takes the bump out — into heat, and never back. Comfort is not softness; it is loss, timed right.

Because the dashpot fights speed, not position — F = c·v — it does nothing to hold the car at ride height and everything to kill a fast bob. Too little and it cannot catch the bounce before it repeats; the ride floats and the contact patch chatters. Too much and the oil will not let the piston move at all on a quick input, so the damper behaves like a solid strut and passes the road straight through. The whole engineering is choosing how much, against the mass it must settle.

The one relation, read three ways

Natural bob, damping, recovery

Take the sprung mass m, the spring k, and the damper c. Idealise the tyre and road as one sprung mass, and the recovery from a bump is a single second-order system, read three ways off the damping ratio:

  • It has a natural bob. ωn = √(k ⁄ m) — the rate the body would bounce on its spring, undamped. A heavier load or a softer spring bobs slower; this is the frequency the damper has to tame.
  • Damping is the one dial. ζ = c ⁄ (2·√(k·m)) — the damper measured against that bob. Below one the body floats, bobbing past level again and again; above one it drags, sinking back slow and stiff; at exactly one — critical — it returns to level in one motion, in the least time, with no bob at all. Ride engineers often pick a little under one on purpose, trading a touch of float for a softer ride — but past that the float owns the car.
  • The dashpot stores nothing. Every joule the damper takes out of the bounce leaves as heat in the oil, P = c·v² — which is why a hard-worked damper runs hot, and why a worn one that has boiled its oil stops damping at all. It can only subtract the bob, never store or return it.

Those three are one fact. The bob is set by the load and the spring; the taming by the damper against that bob; and the taming only ever spends. Float loses grip; harshness loses comfort; and the honest middle — one clean motion — is bought with exactly enough loss and no more.

Under-damped, the tyre leaves the road. Over-damped, the road comes into the cabin. The absorber lives in the narrow band where neither happens.

Four recoveries, from a pogo to a jarring strut

Around the one bump

  1. Floats. Almost no damper. The body bobs on the spring, up and down, many times before it rests — and on each rebound the tyre unloads and skips off the road, so the car loses grip exactly when it is moving most. Seasick and unsafe at once.
  2. Wallows. Some damper, not enough. ζ < 1: the body overshoots level once or twice and rolls before it settles — soft, comfortable on a smooth road, vague and slow to recover on a rough one.
  3. Settles. The damper sized to the load, ζ ≈ 1. The body takes the bump and comes back to level in a single motion, no bob, in the least time — the fastest clean recovery, and the reference every ride is tuned around.
  4. Harsh. Far too much damper. ζ ≫ 1: the oil will not let the piston move on a quick input, so the strut behaves like a solid link — the bump comes straight through to the body, and what motion there is drags back slow. A jarring, tiring ride, and hard on the car.

Who tunes it, and for what

The same damper, three ways

The ride engineer

wants it to settle

Sizes the damper to the sprung mass so a bump dies in one motion — near ζ ≈ 1, or a little under for comfort. Re-tunes every time the load changes, because ζ falls as the car is loaded up: a damper right for one is soft for four.

The racer

wants the tyre down

Fears float above all: a tyre that skips on the rebound is a tyre not gripping. Runs the damping firm to keep the contact patch pressed to the road through every ripple, and accepts the harsher ride as the price of grip.

The mechanic

wants to read wear

Bounces a corner by hand: one clean return is a good damper; two or three bobs is oil worn out or boiled. The damper's whole life is spent turning motion to heat, and heat is what finally kills it.

The bench

Set the load against the damper

A wheel takes a bump; the body responds through a spring and a damper. Set the load — the mass the corner carries — and the damper — how hard the dashpot resists. The discipline in the word

The part that actually absorbs

Of the two parts on every wheel, only one absorbs, and it is not the one that carries the weight. The spring is a memory — it holds the bump and gives it back. The absorber is a forgetting: it meets the body's motion in proportion to its speed, hardest where it moves fastest, and spends that motion into heat that never returns, so the bump has somewhere to go besides back into the ride. That is the whole of damping, and it is the same discipline as a door landing at its latch or a needle coming to rest on its reading: take just enough to kill the overshoot, no more, and know that what you take is gone. Too little and it floats; too much and it jars; and the best ride is not the softest. It is the least loss that settles the bump in one clean motion.
