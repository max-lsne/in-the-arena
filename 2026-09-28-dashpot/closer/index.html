Closer

A spring that shuts the door, and a dashpot that decides whether it lands or slams.

Take the spring off a closer and the door bangs the frame; take the door off the spring and it hangs open in the draught. The closer holds both, at odds: a spring that wants the leaf shut now, and a dashpot — a piston in a cylinder of oil — that fights the leaf in proportion to how fast it moves, and turns that motion into heat. Get the check too light and the spring wins the argument and drives the leaf into the jamb. Too heavy and the leaf crawls and is caught ajar. Somewhere between is a single setting where the leaf arrives at the latch as quickly as it possibly can without overshooting — the fastest clean close there is. That setting has a name.

Where the manners come from

A spring for the will, a dashpot for the arrival

The spring is easy: wind it, and it stores a closing force it pays back the moment you let go, always pulling the leaf toward shut. Left alone with only the spring, the leaf would swing shut and keep going — into the frame — and rebound, over and over, because a spring gives back all it takes. It needs something that loses energy, and loses it fastest when the leaf moves fastest. That is the dashpot.

A spring cares where the leaf is. A dashpot cares only how fast it is going. One stores; the other spends — into heat, and never back.

A dashpot is a piston forced through oil: shove it quickly and the oil fights hard; ease it and the oil barely notices. Its resistance rises with speed, F = c·v, so it does nothing to hold the leaf in place and everything to slow a fast swing. That is exactly the manners a close wants — free early, when the leaf is far out and moving slow; firm late, checking the leaf hard as the spring whips it toward the frame. The whole design is choosing how hard: too little and the check cannot catch the swing; too much and it smothers it.

The one relation, read three ways

Natural swing, damping, arrival

Take the leaf's inertia m, the spring k, and the check c. Idealise the hinge as frictionless, and the whole close is a single second-order system, read three ways off one number — the damping ratio:

  • It has a natural swing. ωn = √(k ⁄ m) — the rate the spring and inertia alone would rock the leaf, undamped. A stiffer spring or a lighter leaf swings faster; this sets the clock every other quantity is measured against.
  • Damping is the one dial. ζ = c ⁄ (2·√(k·m)) — the check measured against that swing. Below one the leaf rings: it overshoots the latch and rebounds. Above one it crawls: it creeps home with no overshoot but takes its time. At exactly one — critical — it arrives in the least time with no overshoot at all. Every closer is trying to sit near one.
  • The dashpot stores nothing. The spring's energy that is not left in the shut door is burned in the oil, P = c·v² — all of it to heat, none given back. That is why a dashpot can only ever subtract a swing, never start or hold one: its single product is the death of motion.

Those three are one fact. The swing you must tame is set by the spring and the leaf; the taming is set by the check against that swing; and the taming can only spend, never store — so there is exactly one best answer, and it is not "as much check as possible." It is just enough.

Under-check and the door bangs. Over-check and it hangs. The art of the closer is the narrow band between, where it merely lands.

Four arrivals, from a bang to a door left ajar

Around the one latch

  1. Slams. Almost no check. The spring drives the leaf into the frame at full speed and it rebounds — the loud failure, and the one that splits jambs and pinches fingers. The spring was never the problem; the missing dashpot was.
  2. Bounces. Some check, not enough. ζ < 1: the leaf overshoots the latch, rebounds a little, and settles after a wobble or two. It closes, but not cleanly, and not on the first try.
  3. Lands. The check sized to the spring, ζ ≈ 1. The leaf sweeps home and stops at the latch with no overshoot, in the least time any setting can manage. This is the whole aim — the fastest clean close.
  4. Hangs. Far too much check. ζ ≫ 1: the oil smothers the swing so thoroughly the leaf crawls and never reaches the latch before it is caught ajar — a failure as real as a slam, only silent. A fire door that hangs open is a fire door that failed.

Who sets it, and for what

The same check, three ways

The fitter

wants a clean latch

Turns the sweep and latch valves until the leaf comes home firm and quiet, catching every time without a bang. Reads the arrival by ear and hand — no overshoot, no crawl — which is ζ ≈ 1 felt rather than calculated.

The fire officer

wants it never ajar

Cares that the leaf always reaches the latch, in weather and draught, so the door can hold smoke. Fears the over-checked door more than the brisk one: a slam is a nuisance, a door hung open is a breach.

The access designer

wants it openable

Balances the spring against the people who must push it — light enough for a child or a wheelchair, strong enough to shut. A heavier spring needs a heavier check to stay clean, so ζ must be re-found every time the spring moves.

The discipline in the word

A thing that shuts, and knows how

A closer is not the spring — anyone can make a door shut, by hanging a weight on it. A closer is the manners: the knowledge, built into a valve of oil, that a swing let loose must be met and spent, hardest where it is fastest, and spent down to nothing exactly at the latch. That is the whole of damping, and it is the same discipline whether the thing arriving is a door, a car over a bump, or an instrument's needle: meet the motion in proportion to its speed, take just enough to kill the overshoot and no more, and remember that what you take is gone to heat and will not come back. Too little and it rings; too much and it crawls; and the best is never the most. It is the least that lands it clean.
