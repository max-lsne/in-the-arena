differential · noun · /ˌdɪfəˈrɛnʃ(ə)l/ — the gear train that divides one drive between two shafts and lets them turn at different speeds, the carrier turning always at the mean of the two; from Latin differre, to carry apart — for the two speeds it carries apart while holding their sum

Differential

A gear that lets two wheels turn at different speeds while driving them both — and can push them only equally hard.

Round a corner the outer wheel travels further than the inner and must turn faster; a solid axle would force them to agree, and one tyre would scrub while the other dragged. A differential dissolves the fight. Its carrier — the cage the drive turns — carries a small pinion free to spin on its own pin, meshing with a gear on each half-shaft, and that pinion shares the drive out: run one wheel faster and the other runs slower by exactly as much. So the carrier turns always at the average of the two wheels, and their sum is fixed — their difference is the corner, their sum is the drive. But the pinion that averages the speeds sits like a beam on a fulcrum, an equal arm each side, and so it can only ever push the two wheels equally hard. That equal push is the same symmetry as the free speeds — and it is why one wheel on ice will sit and spin while the other, with grip to spare, does nothing.

Where the freedom comes from

A pinion is a beam on a fulcrum, wrapped into a gear

Picture the carrier as a spinning cage. Bridged across it, on a pin fixed to the cage, sits a small bevel pinion — the spider — free to roll on that pin. It meshes with a bevel gear on the end of each half-shaft, one to the left wheel, one to the right. Turn the cage and it drags the pinion round with it; the pinion, meshing both side gears, drives both wheels. That is the drive.

Now hold the cage still and turn one wheel by hand: the pinion rolls on its pin and turns the other wheel backward by exactly as much. The cage sees the average; the pinion carries the difference. Sum pinned, difference free — the whole gear in one sentence.

So the two wheels are not locked to each other, only to their mean. Whatever the road asks of their difference — a gentle bend, a tight hairpin, one wheel in a rut — the pinion supplies it by rolling on its pin, and the carrier turns on, indifferent, at the average. The half-shafts may run at any two speeds at all, provided they sum to twice the carrier: ω_L + ω_R = 2·ω_c. Read straight off the gear.

And here is the catch, hidden in the same pinion. A gear on a fulcrum with equal arms can only push its two ends with equal force. The pinion pushes each side gear with the same tooth load, so the torque to each wheel is equal — T_L = T_R — no matter how the speeds differ. The differential cannot favour a wheel. The freedom in the speeds and the equality in the torque are one fact, seen from the two ends of the same pin.

The one relation it embodies

Free in speed, equal in torque

Take the two wheel speeds ω_L, ω_R, the carrier speed ω_c the driveshaft sets, and the torque T the engine feeds the carrier. Idealise the gears as lossless and the whole of it is two lines that never let go of each other:

  • The speeds split freely. ω_L + ω_R = 2·ω_c. The carrier turns at the average of the two wheels; their sum is fixed by the drive, their difference is whatever the road asks. Straight, they run together; in a corner the outer gains exactly what the inner loses. This freedom is the entire point of the gear.
  • The torque splits equally. T_L = T_R = T ⁄ 2, always, whatever the speeds. The pinion is a beam with equal arms; it pushes both sides with the same force and cannot favour one. Not a fault to tune out — the same symmetry that frees the speeds.
  • The sting. A wheel passes to the road no more torque than its grip allows. So the equal split caps the axle at twice the grip of its worst wheel: put one wheel on ice and the good wheel is held down to the same near-nothing, the extra throttle only spinning the wheel that slips — ω_ice climbing by exactly what ω_grip falls, sum still pinned, car still still.

So one pin does two opposite-seeming jobs, and they are the same job. A differential meant to pull — a tractor, a rally car, an off-roader — is given a way to break the symmetry when it hurts: a limited-slip that leaks torque toward the wheel that still grips, or a lock that welds the shafts into one solid axle while you hold it. Each buys traction back by surrendering, for a moment, the very freedom the differential was for.

The differential gives the wheels their freedom in speed and charges for it in torque: it will drive them both, around any corner, but only ever equally hard — and so it is only ever as strong as its weakest wheel, twice over.

Four states, from an even drive to one wheel spinning

Along the one axle

  1. Even. Straight and gripping. Both wheels turn together at the carrier speed, the torque split evenly between them, and every bit of it reaches the road. The plain, happy case the gear runs in almost all the time.
  2. Cornering. The road asks a difference. The outer wheel gains exactly what the inner loses — their sum unchanged, pinned to twice the carrier — while the torque stays even and the axle still pulls. No scrub, no fight: the freedom doing its work.
  3. At the edge. The inner wheel — on the poorer ground — nears its grip. The torque each wheel carries has risen to almost all that ground will hold; a touch more throttle and it lets go. The cliff's edge, where a careful foot keeps traction.
  4. Spinning. The inner wheel breaks free and spins, up toward twice the carrier; the good wheel gets only the same small torque and barely turns. The axle delivers twice a tiny grip and no more, the extra throttle poured into a blur of one wheel. The freedom, turned against you.

Who reads it, and for what

The same pin, three ways

The road-car engineer

wants the freedom

Fits an open differential so the tyres never scrub or fight through the thousand small turns of a car-park and a commute. Accepts the ice failing as rare, and leaves the symmetry whole — the ride smoother for it, every day it does not snow.

The rally driver

wants the freedom gone when it hurts

Runs a limited-slip or a lockable differential, to break the equal split the instant one wheel loses grip and shove torque to the wheel that still bites. Trades a little scrub and a heavier feel for a car that pulls out of the mud instead of digging in.

The instrument-maker

wants the average

Reads the differential not as a drive but as an adder: feed the two shafts and the carrier returns their mean. Built into odometers, fire-control computers and the mechanical differential analyser, the same gear sums and averages inputs — the corner-turner turned calculator.

The discipline in the word

A gear that carries apart, and holds the sum

The differential is one of the few machines named for what it lets happen rather than what it does: it does not make a difference, it permits one — carries the two speeds apart while keeping their sum in hand. That permission is its genius and its whole limitation at once. A drive that forced the wheels to agree could never round a corner cleanly; a drive that freed them entirely could send no torque at all. The differential threads the two, and pays for the thread in a coin it cannot help spending: it can push the wheels only equally, and so is only ever as strong as its weakest. The discipline is to see that the freedom and the equality are the same pin seen twice, and to decide — before the ice, not on it — whether this axle should be left free to corner or given the means to lock and pull.
