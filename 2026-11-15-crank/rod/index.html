connecting rod · noun · the rigid link joining the crank pin to the slider; it both carries the force between them and, having a length of its own, tilts as the crank turns — and so quietly rewrites the motion it is only meant to transmit

Rod

The clean cosine was a promise the rod can only half keep.

The throw page's tidy x = r·cos θ assumes a rod so long it never tilts — always parallel to the stroke. A real rod has a length L, and to keep hold of a pin that is swinging off to the side it must lean, by an angle β with sin β = (r/L)·sin θ. That lean feeds back into the slider, so its travel is really r·cos θ + √(L² − r²sin²θ) — a cosine with a smaller wave at twice the crank angle riding on it, a second harmonic. Everything odd about a real piston follows: it hits top speed before the crank is square, dwells a touch longer at the near end, and is flung harder at the far dead centre than the near, in the ratio (n+1):(n−1) with n = L/r. Shorten the rod and the bend grows; lengthen it and the motion creeps back toward the ideal sine.

What the lean costs

Why a real piston is not a sine

  • Expand the root for a long rod and the travel is ≈ r·cos θ + (r²/4L)·cos 2θ plus a constant. The first term is the ideal; the cos 2θ is the second harmonic, with amplitude r/(4n) — a wave at twice the crank frequency that no amount of balancing the crank can remove.
  • Because of it the piston reaches peak speed before 90° — the crank and rod are square to each other, not the crank to the stroke — and the asymmetry grows as the rod shortens.
  • The acceleration, and so the inertia force, is r·ω²·(cos θ + cos 2θ/n): at the far dead centre the two terms add to (1 + 1/n), at the near they subtract to (1 − 1/n). The far end is thrown harder — which is where the gudgeon pin and small end take their beating.
  • The lean also pushes the piston sideways against the cylinder wall — piston slap and thrust-face wear — with a force proportional to tan β, largest at mid-stroke where the rod leans most.
  • Engines cancel the second harmonic with balance shafts geared to turn at twice crank speed, carrying weights timed to oppose it. A longer rod needs less of this; it is the cheapest balancer there is, paid for in height and weight.

The rod is asked only to carry the force between a turn and a line. But it has a length, and length has opinions — so the humblest link in the engine is also the one that decides the shape of every stroke.
