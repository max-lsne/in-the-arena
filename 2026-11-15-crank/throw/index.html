throw · noun · /θrəʊ/ — the radius of a crank, the distance from the shaft's axis to the crank pin; and so the reach of the turn, half the stroke it drives, from Old English thrāwan, to twist or turn

Throw

A pin carried round a circle, seen from the side, is a thing sliding back and forth.

A crank is the part that turns a circle into a straight line, and back. A pin is fixed a distance r — the throw — from the shaft's axis, so as the shaft turns the pin rides round a circle of radius r. Tie that pin by a rod to a block that is held to a single straight guide, and the block can only do one thing: slide along the guide. The pin's sideways wandering is taken up by the rod swinging; what reaches the block is only the pin's motion along the line of stroke. Make the rod very long and that is exactly the pin's shadow on the guide — x = r·cos θ — so a steady turn comes out as a clean back-and-forth, out to one end, still for an instant, back to the other. The throw sets the whole swing: the block travels a full stroke of 2r, and no more.

The bench

One turn, read as a line

Let the crank turn and watch the slider. The pin walks round its circle at a steady rate, but the slider it drives is fastest crossing the middle — where the pin is moving straight along the guide — and dead still for an instant at each end, where the pin is reaching its farthest along the line and is, just then, moving only sideways. The lower panel draws the slider's position as the crank turns: a clean cosine, the pin's shadow unrolled in time. Widen the throw and the same turn swings a longer stroke.

What the shadow says

Why a steady turn comes out as a cosine

  • The pin sits at (r·cos θ, r·sin θ). The slider is held to the line of stroke, so only the first coordinate reaches it: x = r·cos θ. That is the projection of circular motion onto a line, and it is the definition of simple harmonic motion.
  • The stroke is 2r — out to +r at θ = 0, back to −r at θ = 180°. Nothing but the throw sets it; the speed only sets how often.
  • The slider's speed is −r·ω·sin θ: greatest through the middle (θ = 90°, 270°), where the pin crosses the guide square-on, and zero at each end, where the pin's motion is purely across the guide and none of it reaches the block.
  • The ends are turning points, not stops — the slider is still for only an instant while its direction reverses. The crank never pauses; it is the geometry that makes the line hesitate.
  • This is the ideal: a rod so long it stays parallel to the stroke. A real rod is finite and tilts, bending the clean cosine into something lopsided — the whole subject of rod.

The crank does not make the back-and-forth. The back-and-forth was always there, hidden in the circle; the crank only holds the slider to the one line on which you can see it.
