flywheel · noun · /ˈflʌɪwiːl/ — a heavy wheel added to a shaft to store rotational energy and even out its speed, giving back what a gusty drive leaves short; from fly, to run free, and wheel — the wheel that keeps running when the drive does not

Flywheel

A wheel made heavy on purpose, so the shaft keeps turning evenly through a drive that comes in gusts.

A single piston fires once every second turn and coasts the rest; a press bites metal for an instant and idles the minute round it. Left alone, such a shaft would surge and stall, surge and stall. A flywheel dissolves the lurch. It is nothing but a store: while the drive gives more than the load takes, the surplus goes into spinning the wheel faster; while the drive falls short, the wheel gives that energy back and slows a little to do it. The speed still rises and falls — it must, to bank and to spend — but only by a whisker, and the whisker is set entirely by how much the wheel weighs and how fast it runs. That same hoarded energy is the wheel's danger: it is slow to bring up to speed, slow to stop, and — because the store grows as the square of the speed — a wheel spun too fast holds more than it can contain and flies apart. The limit is not its size. It is the speed of its rim.

Where the steadiness comes from

Energy banked in the rim, paid back on demand

A turning wheel holds kinetic energy the way a moving mass does, but counted around the axis: E = ½·I·ω², where ω is the spin rate and I the moment of inertia — the wheel's resistance to being spun up or slowed, mass weighted by the square of how far it sits from the axle. That square is the whole design. A kilogram at the rim, twice the radius of a kilogram near the hub, stores four times the energy for the same weight and the same speed. So a flywheel is made thin at the centre and thick at the rim: a plain disc has I = ½·m·r², a wheel with its mass in the rim reaches I ≈ m·r² — twice the store for the same mass, bought by moving the metal outward.

The rim is a bank. When the drive is generous the wheel takes the surplus in and speeds up; when the drive is mean the wheel pays it out and slows. The account is the speed itself.

Watch a shaft over one cycle of a lumpy drive. During the power stroke the drive torque exceeds the load, and the excess has only one place to go — into the wheel, which speeds up. During the idle strokes the drive falls below the load, and the shortfall is made up by the wheel, which slows to cover it. The energy the wheel must hold and release inside one cycle — the fluctuation ΔE — is fixed by the drive's lumpiness and the load, not by the wheel. The wheel's only say is in how big a speed swing that fixed energy costs. And there it is decisive.

The one relation, read three ways

Store, smooth, and the ceiling

Take the moment of inertia I, the mean spin ω, the energy the drive makes the wheel bank and spend each cycle ΔE, and the rim's speed v = ω·r. Idealise the bearings as frictionless and the whole of the flywheel is three lines off the one store:

  • It stores as the square of speed. E = ½·I·ω². Doubling the speed quadruples the hoard; a flywheel at speed carries far more than intuition allows, which is both its usefulness and its hazard. The moment of inertia I is set by the mass and, squared, by how far out it sits — so the rim is where the metal is spent.
  • It smooths in proportion to that store. The speed wobble left over is Cs = ΔE ⁄ (I·ω²) — the fraction the speed swings across a cycle. The drive fixes ΔE; the wheel fixes I·ω². So to halve the surge you double the inertia, or spin it faster — a heavier rim buys steadiness directly, and there are steeply diminishing returns to piling on more.
  • The ceiling is rim speed, not size. A spinning rim is held together against flying apart only by its own strength, and the hoop stress works out to σ = ρ·v² — density times the square of the rim speed, with no radius in it. So a big wheel and a small one of the same material burst at the same rim speed. You cannot make a flywheel safe by making it bigger; past the material's limit, v_max, any flywheel lets go.

Those three are one fact turned three ways. The store that steadies the drive is the store that resists your hand and the store that can burst. A flywheel is generous with the speed it lends and severe about the speed it is spun: it will iron the lurch out of the roughest engine, provided you feed it a load steady enough to keep its speed sane, and never, ever spin its rim past what the rim can hold.

Inertia is patience with a price: the wheel that will not let the shaft surge is the same wheel that will not let it start, stop, or be spun a hair too fast.

Four states, from an even shaft to a burst rim

Around the one wheel

  1. Steady. A wheel with inertia to spare against this drive. The surplus of each power stroke barely nudges the speed up, the shortfall barely lets it down, and the shaft turns as if the drive were smooth. The whole point of the wheel, doing its quiet work.
  2. Surging. Too little wheel for a lumpy drive. The same fixed ΔE now costs a wide speed swing — the shaft races on each firing and lags between, and every belt and gear downstream feels the throb. Not a fault in the engine; a flywheel undersized for it.
  3. Labouring. The load drags the mean speed low. With ω small the store ½·I·ω² is small too, so the same energy fluctuation swings the speed hard — a heavy wheel run slow surges as badly as a light one run fast. Steadiness lives in I·ω², and both terms matter.
  4. Bursting. Too little load lets the wheel run away, its rim speed climbing past what the material will hold — σ = ρ·v² beyond the limit. The store that steadied the shaft is now a bomb, and no amount of extra mass helps: the ceiling was the rim's speed all along.

Who reads it, and for what

The same wheel, three ways

The engine-builder

wants the shaft even

Sizes the flywheel to a coefficient of fluctuation the machinery downstream can bear — small for a mill of fine gears, looser for a pump. Reads Cs = ΔE ⁄ (I·ω²) straight off the firing pattern and the running speed, and adds just enough rim to hit it, no more.

The press operator

wants the banked punch

Runs a small motor that could never shear the plate on its own, and lets the flywheel hoard its energy through the idle seconds, then spend it all in the instant of the bite. The wheel turns a weak, steady supply into a brief, enormous demand — a store, read as a hammer.

The safety engineer

wants the rim contained

Cares only for σ = ρ·v² and the burst speed it sets. Fits an overspeed trip, a containment ring, a rim of a material with a high strength-to-density — because a flywheel that fails does not slow, it explodes, and its size gave no warning at all.

The discipline in the word

A wheel that runs free of the drive

The flywheel is named for what it does when the drive stops giving: it flies — runs on, free of the stroke that made it turn. That freedom is a stored thing, and storage is the whole idea, seen from three sides at once. The wheel that carries the shaft smoothly over a dead stroke is carrying energy; the wheel that will not let the shaft surge is refusing to change its own speed; the wheel that shears a plate in an instant is emptying, all at once, a store it filled slowly. Each of these is ½·I·ω², and each has the same two dangers folded inside it — that the store is too small to do the job, and that it is too large to control. The discipline is to size the inertia to the surge you must smooth and no larger, to keep the mean speed high enough that the store is real and low enough that the rim is safe, and to remember that the ceiling was never the wheel's size. It was always how fast its rim was allowed to run.
