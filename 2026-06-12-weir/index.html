A river is too low.
A weir lifts it two feet.

A weir is the lowest possible dam — a course of dressed stone laid flat across a stream so that the water, gently held, rises behind it and spills cleanly over the crest. It does not stop the river. It only lifts the water a couple of feet, so that some of it can be drawn off to the level — patient, steady, exactly enough to do the slow work without the rest of the river noticing.

Most days the work is a river running shallow at its own pace, going wherever the country slopes, never quite at the level. You dip a cup in and you get half a cup and two pebbles. You build a wheel and it spins twice a week and stops. The problem is not the water. The problem is that the river has no level.

A weir is a long shallow stone. It does not stop the river. It just lifts the water two feet behind it, and holds it there, and lets the rest of the river spill cleanly over the top in a thin even sheet. You can dip a cup. You can turn a wheel. You can leave it for a week and come back to the same level. The work is in laying the stone, once, and in keeping the crest level.

How it works

  1. 01

    Find the stream

    The stream is the wide unfocused water of the day — what you have before you have lifted any of it. Whatever the country slopes toward. It does not know what it is for. That is the stream's job: to keep going.

  2. 02

    Lay the stone

    The stone is a small straight course laid across the stream, just two feet tall, level and dressed. You lay it once. It is not the river, not the cup, not the wheel — it is the long shallow thing that holds the water at the level long enough to be useful.

  3. 03

    Let the water rise

    Behind the stone, the water rises. It does not flood. It is held to the level of the crest, and once it reaches the crest, the rest spills cleanly over and goes downstream as it always did. The pool is full. The weir is quiet.

  4. 04

    Draw what you need

    From the side of the pool, behind the weir, you draw off a steady measured thread — to a wheel, a field, a cup, a working day. The river goes on past the stone. The held water comes off level. Tomorrow the pool is still full to the crest.

The four cuts

  • Notch. A V-cut in the crest, finger-width, for the lowest flow — the daily entry, the half-page note, the warm-up scale. Always there. Always trickling.
  • Sill. A flat broad cut, thumb-deep — the weekly practice, the Saturday workshop, the chapter under second revision. Spills steady most weeks.
  • Crest. The full dressed length of the weir, a season patient and quiet — the long course taken evening by evening, the book in second draft, the slow recovery.
  • Compound. A stepped weir of two or three falls down the hillside — a decade work, a rebuilt practice. Built one course a year.

Who lays a weir

The historian at the slow chapter

Two paragraphs an evening in a quarto notebook, four years in. The river is wide — teaching, the supervisor, the long winter. The pool is the chapter, brimming each Sunday, drawn off into the next month's draft. The wheel turns one paragraph a day.

The cellist at the warm-up

A single open-string scale, forty seconds, every morning since 2019. Notch in the crest, thinnest cut. Has never been drawn off, has never quite stopped spilling, holds the level so the rest of the practice can come off clean.

The gardener at the long bed

A bed turned slowly across four years, one square yard at a time. Compound weir, three steps down the hill: the bed nearest the house planted in vegetables, the middle in cut flowers, the upper still bare stone, holding the year's water.

The runner training for the long race

A single-mile addition each fortnight, layered across an eight-month build. The river is the city's traffic and weather. The pool is the held base mileage. The compound steps lead, eventually, to a clean spill at mile twenty-six.
