Chebyshev linkage · noun · /ˈtʃɛbɪʃɒf/ — a four-bar linkage proportioned so its coupler midpoint traces a path with a long near-straight segment; turned under and phased, the basis of an early walking machine

Chebyshev

The best near-miss — and it learned to walk.

Watt tuned his four-bar until the bow was small enough. Pafnuty Chebyshev asked the sharper question: of all near-straight four-bars, which bows least? Chasing that best possible approximation, he helped found a branch of mathematics — and found a four-bar, in the proportion 2 : 5 : 5 : 4, whose coupler midpoint traces a closed curve with a long, flat top. Then he saw what the flat was for: turn it downward to make the sole of a foot, set several linkages on one frame a quarter-turn apart, and at every instant some foot is pressing the flat — so the frame rides dead level while the feet pace a straight floor. His Plantigrade machine, 1878, one of the first walkers. Trace the flat; then turn it under and watch it walk.

The bench

A flat-topped curve, and the best you can hold

The four-bar runs and the pen at the coupler's midpoint traces its closed curve; the top is the near-straight part, and the strip magnifies how little it bows across the working run you ask for. Two things to try. Slide the tracing point off the midpoint and the flat spoils — the midpoint is the best point, as Chebyshev proved. Then stretch the working run: the longer a straight stroke you demand, the worse the straightness you can keep — the honest trade every approximate linkage makes, and the reason Chebyshev's whole question was how little bow is possible. That flat, held at constant height and nearly constant speed, is also what a foot needs — the seed of his walking machine below.

Why the flat, and why it walks

The best approximation, turned into a stride

  • Chebyshev's question was not "is it straight enough?" but "is it the straightest possible?" The answer — the curve that minimises the largest deviation over the run — is the heart of minimax approximation, and the polynomials and the whole theory that carry his name grew in part from chasing it through linkages like this one.
  • The proportion 2 : 5 : 5 : 4 — coupler, two equal links, base — puts the midpoint of the coupler on a curve with a long flat top: it bows only a part in forty or so across a run near a third of the base, and straighter still across any shorter stretch. Move the pen off the midpoint and the symmetry breaks; the flat tilts and bows, worst of all right where it was best.
  • Here is the gift hiding in the flat: along it the foot moves not only at constant height but at nearly constant horizontal speed. So a body rolling forward at a steady pace can keep a foot planted there without slip — the straight line is exactly the condition for a level, slip-free stride.
  • Turn the flat under to make a sole, and phase several legs a fraction of a cycle apart. The stance phases overlap: before one foot lifts, the next has landed, so the count of feet on the floor never falls to zero and the body never drops. The whole folder's straight line becomes, here, the floor a machine can walk — Chebyshev's Plantigrade machine, 1878.
  • It is the same bargain as Watt's — four bars, a bow you make small rather than zero — but pushed to its mathematical limit, and then put to work. Where Peaucellier spent three more bars to make the bow vanish by proof, Chebyshev kept the bow, made it the smallest it could be, and discovered it was small enough to carry a walking weight.

Watt's line was good enough to guide a piston; Chebyshev's was good enough to stand on — and then to walk.
