Peaucellier–Lipkin cell · noun · /poʊsɛlˈjeɪ ˈlɪpkɪn/ — a seven-bar linkage that converts a point's circular motion into exactly straight-line motion, by physically performing a geometric inversion

Peaucellier

A straight line, drawn true — by bars and pins alone.

Every pin swings in a circle; that is all a pivot can do. So for eighty years after Watt it was an open question whether a linkage could draw a dead-straight line at all, or only ever a clever near-miss. In 1864 Charles-Nicolas Peaucellier, an officer of the French engineers, answered it with seven bars: a rhombus of four equal links, two long equal arms pinning it to a fixed point O, and a crank. The machine is an inversor — it holds OP · OP′ constant — and because the inverse of a circle through O is a straight line, constraining the inner point P to such a circle forces the pen P′ onto a line exact to the last decimal. Turn the crank and watch it run true; then slide the crank pin off its circle and watch the straight line bow.

The bench

Seven bars, one true line

The crank on the right pivot swings the inner point P around a circle. Two long arms of length d run from the fixed pin O to the sides of a rhombus of four equal bars of length b; the pen P′ sits at the rhombus's far corner, always on the ray O→P, at the distance that keeps OP · OP′ = d² − b². When the crank's circle passes exactly through O, the pen draws a perfect vertical line. The thin trail is the path the pen has swept; the magnified strip beneath shows how far it strays from true — flat on the knife-edge, bowing the instant the crank pin leaves its circle.

Why it is exact, not nearly

Inversion, built out of bars

  • The four equal bars of the rhombus keep P and the pen P′ symmetric about the line through their two side corners — so O, P and P′ are always collinear, the pen forever on the ray out of O through P.
  • A short piece of Pythagoras on the rhombus and the two arms gives the key identity: OP · OP′ = d² − b², a constant fixed only by the bar lengths. The pen is the geometric inverse of P in a circle of radius √(d² − b²) centred at O. The machine does not approximate the inversion; it is one.
  • Inversion's one magic fact: the image of a circle that passes through the centre O is a straight line (and nothing else maps to a line). So the moment you pin P to a circle through O — one extra crank does it — the pen P′ is thrown onto an exact line, perpendicular to the line of centres.
  • That is the whole trick, and why it is exact: there is no small-angle fudge, no stretch of the curve that merely looks straight. Move the crank pin so its circle misses O, and the image is no longer a line but an arc — the pen bows, and the magnified strip shows it at once. Least error is not nearly-zero here; on the knife-edge it is zero.
  • It settled an eighty-year question. Watt believed an exact straight-line linkage was impossible and built the best approximation he could; Chebyshev, hunting the best possible near-miss, grew a theory of approximation out of the failure. Peaucellier's rhombus proved them both wrong by proving the thing could be done at all — "the most beautiful theorem in elementary kinematics," Sylvester called the family it opened.

Watt drew a line that was nearly straight. Peaucellier drew one that was straight, and could prove it from the lengths of his bars.
