parallel motion · noun · a four-bar linkage whose coupler-midpoint traces a path with a long near-straight central stretch, used to guide a point along an approximate straight line

Watt

Nearly straight — and good enough to build an empire on.

The double-acting engine of 1784 pushed as well as pulled, so the piston rod had to be held to a straight path — and no chain could push, no slide of that decade could be cut true enough. Watt's answer was a four-bar linkage: two equal rockers on two fixed pins, a coupler across their tips, and a pen at the coupler's midpoint. That midpoint traces a long thin figure-eight, and through the crossing in the middle the curve hugs a straight line closely enough to guide the rod. It is a near-miss — the line bows, and hooks into the lobes at the ends of the stroke — but a bow finer than the iron it moved. "I am more proud of the parallel motion," he wrote, "than of any other invention I have ever made." Rock the beam and watch it draw; magnify the strip and watch it bow.

The bench

Two rockers, a coupler, a near-straight stroke

The two fixed pins carry equal rockers; the coupler joins their tips, and the pen rides its midpoint. As the beam rocks, the pen runs across the top of a faint figure-eight — nearly straight through the middle, then curling down into a hook at each end as it falls into a lobe. The pale curve is the whole eight the midpoint could trace; the bright stroke is the working part the mechanism actually runs. The strip beneath magnifies how far the stroke departs from a true straight line — flat across the centre, flaring where the hooks begin. Watt used only the straight centre, and sized his engine so the rod never reached the hooks.

Length of the bar across the rocker tips. It fattens or thins the eight: a shorter coupler makes a leaner eight with a longer, flatter straight run; too long and the hooks crowd in and the straight part shrinks.

Length of the two equal swinging arms. Longer rockers flatten the stroke — the farther the pivot, the straighter the swing near the top — at the cost of a taller, lankier machine.

Distance between the two fixed pins. With the coupler and rockers, it tunes where the straight run falls and how long it is — the three lengths together are the whole design.

Why it is nearly, not exactly

A straight line borrowed from a figure-eight

  • Two equal rockers swing on fixed pins; a coupler joins their tips. By symmetry the coupler's midpoint is the one point whose sideways wander from the two rockers cancels to first and second order near the centre — so its path is flat there, where any other point on the coupler would swing in a plain arc.
  • The full path is a lemniscate — a figure-eight — and only its central crossing is near-straight. The cancellation is not perfect: a third-order term survives, so the line bows, gently at the middle and sharply into the hooks where the stroke falls into the lobes. There is no setting that makes the bow vanish; it can only be made small.
  • You buy straightness with length. Longer rockers flatten the swing near the top; a leaner coupler stretches the straight run. Watt tuned the three lengths until the residual bow over his working stroke was finer than the bore and bearings could feel — then stopped, because finer than it matters is the engineer's definition of straight.
  • It is the opposite bargain to Peaucellier's. Watt spends four bars and accepts a bow; Peaucellier spends seven and a theorem and gets a line that is straight by proof. For eighty years everyone paid Watt's price, because four bars are cheap, "nearly" was always enough, and no one yet knew the exact price could be paid at all.
  • The same four-bar, re-proportioned, is Chebyshev's linkage — and Chebyshev asked the question Watt did not: of all the near-straight four-bars, which bows least? The hunt for that best approximation grew into a whole branch of mathematics, and taught a linkage to walk.

Watt did not draw a straight line. He drew a figure-eight so patiently that its middle could be mistaken for one — and ran an engine down the mistake.
