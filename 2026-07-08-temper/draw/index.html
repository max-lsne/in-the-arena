What comes of drawing the temper slow

Throw it back hot,
and you draw the temper out.

A blade comes off the quench glass-hard — hard enough to hold an edge, and brittle enough to snap like a carrot. It is not finished; it is dangerous. To bring it right the smith draws the temper: he polishes it bright, sets it back over a low heat, and watches the colour crawl up the clean steel — pale straw, gold, bronze, peacock, and at last a spring blue — pulling it from the fire at the exact shade that trades a sliver of that hardness for the toughness that lets an edge bend a little instead of breaking. Get greedy and throw it back hot and the colours race past blue in a breath and the temper draws clean out — dead soft, a whole day's forging gone. So you draw a few, over a low heat, and stand over each one.

How it works

Stop throwing the whole forge on the fire. Draw a few, and read the colour.

01

Quench it hard, and clean it bright

A thing you mean to bring right by drawing starts hard — quenched, glass-hard, and brittle — and then polished bright, because you cannot read a temper colour on scaled steel. Bright and cold, it is ready for the heat, not yet touched by it.

02

Draw it slow, and read the colour

Drawing is minding a colour. You move it along — hard, bright, drawing, let — and you read how it is running right now: cold, running, blued, tough. How far along it has come and how hot it is running this minute are not the same question.

03

Pull it at its colour, before it runs past blue

A temper is caught, not forced. Draw more irons than you can watch and the far ones run past blue unseen, drawn out soft and useless. A few, held over a low heat and pulled each at its own shade, come off tough — an edge that bends and springs back instead of chipping.

The forge

A smith draws only as many irons as he can watch run.

A temper colour runs in seconds, and once it has run past its shade it will not come back — you would have to harden and start again. So drawing is standing over the heat, eyes on the steel, pulling each iron at the moment its colour is right. That is close, unhurried, undivided work, and a smith can only watch so many irons at once. Throw the whole forge on the fire and the far irons run past blue while you are pulling the near one — the more you set on at once, the more you draw out soft. The cure is never more irons. It is fewer, laid where your eye falls, and each pulled in its own moment.

So the forge has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point. Draw holds six irons. When the forge is full the next blade waits hard and bright on the bench, out of the fire, until one iron has been drawn and pulled and set aside. A forge charged beyond what one smith can watch would run past blue, iron by iron, before a single edge was ever brought right.

For people who set more on the fire than they can watch.

The whole-forge heater

A dozen irons all thrown on the fire at once, running their colours unwatched while you pull the wrong one. Draw asks which six you are actually standing over, and lets the rest wait hard and bright on the bench.

The slow drawer

A skill, an argument, a habit — things that only come tough when they are drawn slow over a low heat and pulled at the right shade. Draw is built for the minutes with your eye on the steel.

The one who ran it past blue
