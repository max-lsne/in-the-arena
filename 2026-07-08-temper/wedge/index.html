What comes of wedging the body slow

Throw it unwedged,
and the air bursts it in the fire.

Before a potter can throw a single pot, the clay has to be wedged. Fresh or reclaimed clay is never even — it holds hard patches and soft, dry seams and wet ones, and, worst, pockets of trapped air. So the potter wedges it: cuts the lump on a wire, stacks it, and presses it down and forward in a steady, whole-body rhythm — ram's-head, or a spiral — over and over, until the body is one smooth homogeneous thing and the air is gone. It is dull, heavy, unglamorous work, and there is a great temptation to skip it: slam a barrow of clay together, wet, and throw it in a hurry. But the hidden air fights the wheel and pulls the pot off true — and if it survives to the kiln, the trapped air expands in the heat and bursts the pot, often taking its neighbours with it. So you wedge a modest ball, patiently, and drive the air out before the wheel.

01

Reclaim it wet, and know it is uneven

A thing you mean to make sound by wedging starts wet — fresh or reclaimed, full of hard patches, soft seams, and trapped air. Formless and uneven, it is ready for the board, not yet fit for the wheel.

02

Wedge it slow, and read the body

Wedging is minding a body. You move it along — wet, wedging, evened, thrown — and you read how the clay is right now: short, airy, slack, plastic. How far along it has come and how it is working under your hands are not the same question.

03

Even the body, before the kiln finds the air

A pot is made on the board, not the wheel. Wedge more than your arms can truly even and the air stays hidden in the far balls, throws them off true, and bursts them in the fire. A few, wedged patient and whole, centre sweet and come up true.

The board

A potter wedges only as many balls as his arms can truly even.

Wedging is real bodily work — cutting, lifting, stacking, and pressing a heavy ball in a rhythm, dozens of turns each, until the clay is even and the air is gone. You cannot rush it and you cannot fake it: the air you leave in does not show on the board, only later, off-true on the wheel or burst in the kiln. A potter can only truly even so many balls before his arms tire and his attention slips. Slam the whole barrow together and the far balls go to the wheel unwedged — the more you set out at once, the more air you leave hidden. The cure is never more balls. It is fewer, wedged whole, each driven true before it is thrown.

So the board has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point. Wedge holds six balls. When the board is full the next lump waits wet and covered under a cloth, off the board, until one ball has been wedged even and thrown. A board loaded beyond what one potter can truly even would go to the kiln full of hidden air, ball by ball, before a single pot came up true.

Who keeps one

For people who set more on the board than their arms can even.

The whole-barrow slammer

A dozen balls all slammed together wet at once, hiding their air while you throw the near one. Wedge asks which six your arms can truly even, and lets the rest wait wet and covered.

The slow wedger

A skill, a plan, a body of work — things that only come sound when the plain, dull evening is done first, before the wheel. Wedge is built for the unglamorous turns on the board.

The one whose pot burst in the kiln

You skipped the wedging to save an hour, and a good pot came off the wheel a little off-true and then burst in the fire on hidden air. This is for wedging a few whole and driving the air out first.
