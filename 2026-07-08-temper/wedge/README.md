# Wedge

**Slam the whole barrow together wet and throw it in a hurry and the hidden air
bursts the pot in the kiln. Wedge a few whole, drive the air out, and they
centre sweet and come up true.**

Before a potter can throw a single pot, the clay has to be *wedged*. Fresh or
reclaimed clay is never even — it holds hard patches and soft ones, dry seams
and wet, and, worst of all, pockets of trapped *air*. So the potter wedges it:
cuts the lump on a wire, stacks it, and presses it down and forward in a steady,
whole-body rhythm — ram's-head, or a spiral — dozens of turns, over and over,
until the clay is one smooth homogeneous body and the air is driven out. It is
the dullest work in the shop, and there is a constant temptation to skip it and
save the hour: slam a barrow together, wet, and throw it fast. But the air you
leave in does not show on the board. It shows later — pulling the pot off true
under your hands on the wheel, and then, if it gets that far, expanding in the
heat of the kiln and *bursting* the pot, often shattering its neighbours on the
shelf with it. The pot that survives the fire was made on the wedging board, not
the wheel. So you wedge a modest ball, patiently, whole, and drive the air out
before you ever sit down to throw.

Wedge is a small page that holds that shape. It is a board for the few things
made sound by the plain, unglamorous work done first — the ones you are wedging
whole — against the many you slammed together wet and hoped over.

## The two axes

A ball is read two ways at once, and they are not the same reading.

**Stage** — how far along the clay has come:

- **Wet** — fresh or reclaimed; uneven, seamed, and full of trapped air.
- **Wedging** — cut, stacked, and pressed; the air being driven out.
- **Evened** — one smooth homogeneous body; no air, no hard patch.
- **Thrown** — centred and come up true on the wheel; made.

**Condition** — how the body is working, right now:

- **Short** — dry and crumbly; won't hold together or take a form.
- **Airy** — carrying trapped air; the hidden fault that bursts in the fire.
- **Slack** — too wet, slumping; won't stand up to being thrown.
- **Plastic** — wedged even and supple; holds a form and springs back.

The two come apart all the time. A ball can be well into the *wedging* and still
*airy* — worked, but not yet clear of its trapped air. It can be freshly
reclaimed and left *short* — good clay waiting, not yet evened and not yet
slammed together wet. How far along it has come and how the body is working
under the hands are different questions, and the potter asks both every time he
cuts a ball open on the wire to look.

## The board holds six

Wedging is real bodily work — cutting, lifting, stacking, and pressing a heavy
ball in a rhythm until the clay is even and the air is gone. You cannot rush it
and you cannot fake it, because the air you leave in does not show on the board,
only later on the wheel or in the fire. A potter can only truly even so many
balls before his arms tire and his eye slips. So the board has a hard ceiling,
and the ceiling is the point: **Wedge holds six balls.** When the board is full
the next lump waits wet and covered under a cloth, off the board, until one ball
has been wedged even and thrown.

## The shape of the page

Hero, the problem stated plainly, three steps, and then the board: a board of a
few seeded balls you can reclaim, wedge, even, and throw, each read on both
axes, renamed and reset as you like. This page keeps no live gauge — unlike its
two siblings, the smith's colour line and the confectioner's gloss line — and
that is on purpose: clay does not warn you it was rushed while you work it. It
tells you later, off-true on the wheel or burst in the kiln. The only gauge is
the discipline of the board itself. No account, no cloud, no build step. Open
`index.html` in a browser; it all stays in the browser.
