# Slake

**You slake lime in an afternoon. You cannot use it for months. The fast part is
the reaction; the slow part is the rest that makes it fat — and rushed lime does
not fail on the bench, it fails on the wall, one blown grain at a time.**

To **slake** is to give quicklime the water it is starving for. Quicklime is
limestone burnt until the carbon is driven off — caustic, thirsty, unstable. Add
water and it seizes it in a violent, steaming reaction and slumps to soft white
putty. That reaction is loud and over by evening, and it is not the part that
decides whether the lime is any good. The part that matters is silent: the putty
is skinned with water and left, for weeks and then months, growing fat and
plastic the whole time. The reaction made it lime. Only the rest makes it lime
you can work.

Slake is a small page built around that one move. It is a bench for a single tub:
set the water you give the burn and the months you leave it under water, and read
whether the batch comes out fat and true — or scorched, drowned, or merely green.

> The best lime is old lime. A batch slaked this morning and troweled this
> afternoon was measured; it was not *rested*. What you did not leave it collects
> later, above the line of sight, one blown grain at a time.

## The reaction, and the rest

The water starts two things, and only one of them is fast:

- **The reaction — what the water starts.** Fast, hot, loud; done by evening.
  Every grain that gets its water turns. Every grain that does not stays live
  quicklime, buried in the putty, waiting for its water somewhere later — in your
  finished wall.
- **The rest — what the water keeps doing.** Silent, measured in months. Under a
  skin of water the putty grows fat enough to stretch under the trowel and fine
  enough to heal its own hairline cracks. The oldest lime in the yard is the best
  lime in the yard.

## How much water to give it

The dose is a judgement set by the burn, not by the jug. Coarsest error on top,
the mark you want in the middle:

- **Scorched — starved of water.** Too little; the reaction boils itself dry
  before every grain has turned. Live quicklime survives and blows out of the
  finished coat months on, each grain a little crater.
- **Short — a jug shy.** Mostly turned, but a scatter of coarse grains rode
  through. Gritty and stiff; it fights the trowel and cracks as it goes off.
- **Slaked through — water matched to the burn.** The dose the burn asked for.
  Every grain takes its water and turns. Slaked all the way — and now it only
  wants leaving alone.
- **Slack — a jug over.** The reaction cools before it has finished; a soft putty
  that never quite comes to body.
- **Drowned — flooded cold.** Far too much, poured too fast. The slake chills dead
  before it turns; a lean, chalky, weak putty that no resting will bring to fat.

The dose stands for any transformation you feed. Starve it and it half-turns and
hides the rest; drown it and you chill the very reaction you wanted. Match the
water to the burn — then wait.

## The tub holds one batch

The bench draws one tub of lime in cross-section under its skin of water. Set the
**water** and the **months** and it reads the batch: live grains scatter through
the putty when it is starved, steam rises off it while it runs hot, and a fat
sheen gathers on the surface as the rest does its work. The reading comes
**fat & true** only in the narrow band where the water is matched *and* the putty
has rested past a few months — starve it and it blows, drown it and it stays
lean, match it but use it young and it is merely green. *Slake it true* sets the
water to the burn in one move; five jobs off the shelf — a limewash, a float
coat, a pointing mortar, a fresco intonaco, an ashlar bedding — hand you a batch
to correct for yourself.

## The shape of the page

Hero, the two things the water starts (the reaction and the rest), the ladder
from scorched to drowned, three steps, and then the bench: a tub, a dose of water
and a stretch of rest, and a reading that comes true only where the two meet. Set
the water, set the months, and read the coat it will make. No account, no cloud,
no build step. Open `index.html` in a browser; that is the whole of it.
