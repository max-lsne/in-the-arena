An old working word

You slake lime in an afternoon.
You cannot use it for months.

To slake is to give quicklime the water it is starving for — a violent, steaming reaction that turns caustic rock into soft white putty. That is the fast part, done by evening. The slow part is the rest: fat, workable lime is old lime, matured under water long after the reaction has gone cold. Rush it and it does not fail on the bench. It fails on the wall, months later, one blown grain at a time.

Slake a batch What water does to lime

A bench for one tub of lime — no account, no cloud, no build step.

What the water does

The water starts two things, and only one of them is fast

Quicklime is limestone burnt until the carbon is driven off — caustic, thirsty, unstable. Add water and it seizes it. But the reaction you can watch is not the part that decides whether the lime is any good.

The reaction — what the water starts

Fast, hot, loud. The tub steams and hisses and swells; by evening the rock has slumped to putty. Every grain that gets its water turns. Every grain that does not stays live quicklime, buried in the putty, waiting for its water somewhere later — in your finished wall.

Loud, over by evening, and not the point.

The rest — what the water keeps doing

Silent. Weeks and months under a skin of water, the putty grows fat and plastic — long enough to stretch under the trowel, fine enough to heal its own hairline cracks. The reaction made it lime. Only the rest makes it lime you can work.

Quiet, measured in months, and the whole of it.

The best lime is old lime. A batch slaked this morning and troweled this afternoon was measured; it was not rested. What you did not leave it collects later, above the line of sight, one blown grain at a time.

Scorched to drowned

How much water to give it

The dose is a judgement set by the burn, not by the jug. Too little and it boils dry before it turns; too much and it chills before it works. Coarsest error on top, the mark you want in the middle.

  1. Scorched — starved of water Too little. The reaction runs so hot it boils itself dry before every grain has turned. Live quicklime survives in the putty and blows out of the finished coat, months on, each grain a little crater.
  2. Short — a jug shy Nearly. Most of it turned, but a scatter of coarse grains rode through. A gritty, stiff putty that fights the trowel and cracks as it goes off.
  3. Slaked through — water matched to the burn The dose the burn asked for. Every grain takes its water and turns; the tub runs hot and comes down soft. Slaked all the way — and now it only wants leaving alone.
  4. Slack — a jug over A touch much. The reaction cools before it has done its work; a soft putty that never quite comes to body. It will work, but it is short of itself.
  5. Drowned — flooded cold Far too much, poured too fast. The slake chills dead before it turns; a lean, chalky, weak putty that no amount of resting will ever bring to fat.

The dose stands for any transformation you feed. Starve it and it half-turns and hides the rest; drown it and you chill the very reaction you wanted. Match the water to the burn — then wait.

Three steps

Slake it through, rest it out, read the wall

1

Give it enough water

Match the dose to the burn. Enough that every grain turns; not so much you flood the heat out of it. Neither scorched nor drowned — slaked all the way through.

2

Leave it under water

Skin it with water and walk away. Weeks, then months. The putty fattens the whole time it is left; the oldest lime in the yard is the best lime in the yard.

3

Judge the coat, not the tub

The tub always looks like lime. Whether it was slaked through and rested only shows in the coat it makes — how it stretches, whether it blows. Read that, not the mark on the bucket.

Who slakes one

Anyone feeding a reaction they will have to live with

The plasterer

Slakes fat lime a season ahead of the job, because the coat that stretches and heals is the coat made from lime that was left to grow old under water.

The one taught something hot

Takes the new idea while it is still steaming and trowels it straight onto the wall. It half-turned. The grains it did not slake blow out of the argument later.

The one who drowns it to be safe

Pours on water to be sure it is wet enough and chills the reaction dead. A lean, cautious putty that never comes to body, however long it waits.
