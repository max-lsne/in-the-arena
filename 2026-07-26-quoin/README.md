# Quoin

**A wall is only as sound as its corners. Two walls butted at a corner are two
walls; the quoin is the dressed stone that reaches into both and makes them one.
Lay it right and it never fails on the day you lay it — it fails later, from the
top down, along the straight joint you left.**

A **quoin** is the squared, dressed cornerstone that turns the angle where two
walls meet. It is not trim. A corner is the one place a wall tries to be two
walls at once, and where the two runs of masonry meet they want to peel apart
along the seam. The quoin refuses to face only one way: each course, its **long
face** returns down one wall and its **short face** shows on the other, and the
next course turns back. Long, short, long, short, all the way up. Because the
faces alternate, the joint at the back of the cornerstone lands somewhere new
every course — and a joint that never lines up is a joint that cannot become a
crack.

Quoin is a small page built around that one move. It is a bench for a single
corner: lay it course by course, choosing which face returns on the near wall,
and read whether the corner ties into one piece — or leaves a **straight joint**,
the continuous vertical seam that a rushed corner hands to the weather.

> The corner is where shortcuts hide, because it is the hardest place to lay and
> the last place anyone looks. What you do not tie there, the frost unties later.

## The bond, and the break

A straight run of wall is easy to make strong: each stone laps half over the two
below it, so every vertical joint is broken by the course above. That overlap —
the **bond** — is what makes a wall behave like one thing instead of a stack of
loose stones. At a corner the overlap has nowhere to go, and the quoin has to do
its work on purpose:

- **The bond — what alternation buys.** Long face, then short face, turning every
  course. Each back-joint lands off the one below it. Nothing lines up; the two
  walls become one.
- **The break — what running the same way costs.** Lay two courses the same face
  and their back-joints agree. Agree twice more and you have drawn the crack
  yourself, in mortar, top to bottom — a **straight joint**, sound in the demo
  and split by the first hard load it was never asked about.

## The ladder — five corners, worst to true

1. **Butted** — the two runs simply touch; no stone crosses over. A single mortar
   joint the full height of the build. Square today, leaning by spring.
2. **Straight-jointed** — cornerstones used, but faced the same way each course.
   The back-joints stack into one long seam. The crack is drawn; it only has to
   be found.
3. **Toothed** — alternating here and there, but dishonestly; a face too short to
   reach past the joint below. The bond is broken in name only.
4. **Racked** — alternating and honest, but built up faster on one wall than the
   other, left as an unfinished staircase. Sound as far as it goes; simply not
   done.
5. **Bonded** — long and short turning every course, both walls rising together,
   every joint broken. The two walls have stopped being two.

## What's in this folder

- `index.html` — the landing page and the bench, in the browser.
- `styles.css` — a limewash-and-sandstone palette: pale field, dressed-stone gold
  at the corner, iron-oxide ochre for the accent, lichen for a bond that holds
  and a dull brick-red for the straight joint; Fraunces for headings, Inter for
  body, JetBrains Mono for marginalia. Light and dark.
- `script.js` — the corner model. A course is a long or short return; the page
  draws the elevation, finds every back-joint that lines up, and reads the bond.
  It opens on a deliberately flawed corner — one straight joint to find — so the
  bench is never empty on arrival.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. No build step, no account, no network. The corner
you lay stays in the page.
