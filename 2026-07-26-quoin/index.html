An old working word

Two walls meet at a corner.
The quoin is what makes them one.

A quoin is the dressed stone at the corner of a wall — the big, squared block that turns the angle. It is not decoration. A corner is the one place a wall is trying to be two walls at once, and where the two runs of masonry meet they want to peel apart along the seam. The quoin reaches into both walls at once and ties them. Laid right, its long face and short face alternate all the way up, and no two vertical joints ever line up. Lay two courses the same way running and the joints stack into a straight joint — and the corner does not fail on the day you lay it. It opens later, from the top down, along the line you left it.

What the corner does

The wall is strong. The corner is the argument.

A straight run of masonry is easy to make strong: each stone sits half over the two below it, so every load spreads sideways into its neighbors and every vertical joint is broken by the course above. This overlap is the whole trick of a wall. It is called the bond, and a wall with a good bond behaves like one thing rather than a stack of loose stones.

At a corner the trick gets hard. Two runs arrive at ninety degrees and the neat, sideways overlap has nowhere to go — the last stone of one run simply meets the last stone of the other. Butt them and you have built a crease: a continuous vertical joint running the full height of the wall, held together by nothing but the friction of mortar that will not last a hard winter.

The quoin fixes this by refusing to face only one way. Each course, the cornerstone turns: its long face returns down one wall and its short face shows on the other; the next course turns the other way. Long, short, long, short, all the way up. Because the faces alternate, the joint at the back of the quoin lands in a different place every course — and a joint that never lines up is a joint that cannot become a crack.

The failure has a name and it is quiet: a straight joint. Lay two courses the same way and their joints agree. Agree three times and you have drawn the crack yourself, in mortar, top to bottom, and handed the weather a place to start.

The ladder

Five corners, worst to true

The same two walls, met five ways. Only the last one is a corner; the rest are excuses that stand until the first frost finds the seam.

  1. 1

    Butted

    The two runs simply touch. No stone crosses from one wall into the other; the corner is a single mortar joint the full height of the build. It looks square the day it is laid and leans the first spring.

  2. 2

    Straight-jointed

    Cornerstones are used, but laid facing the same way course after course. Their back joints line up into one long vertical seam a stone in from the angle. The crack has been drawn; it only has to be found.

  3. 3

    Toothed

    The courses alternate here and there but not honestly — a long face answered by a face too short to reach past the joint below. The bond is broken in name only; the load still finds the seam.

  4. 4

    Racked

    Alternating and honest, but left stepped and unfinished — built up faster on one wall than the other so the corner climbs as a staircase. Sound as far as it goes; it is simply not done.

  5. 5

    Bonded

    Long face and short face turning every course, both walls rising together, every joint broken by the course above. The two walls have stopped being two. This is the only one you can build the rest of the house on.

Who lays one

For the people whose work turns a corner

The one joining two teams

Two groups that each work fine alone, meeting at a project. Butt them and the seam is a status meeting. The quoin is the person who reaches into both and turns their face each week.

The one merging two systems

Two codebases, two schemas, met at an integration. A straight joint is the shared boundary nobody bonded across — sound in the demo, split by the first load it was not asked about.

The one who builds to last

Anyone who would rather the failure show on the bench than on the wall. The corner is where shortcuts hide, because it is the hardest place to lay and the last place anyone looks.

Build the wall however you like. But turn the corner honestly, because the corner is the part that has to be two things at once — and everything you did not tie there, the weather unties later, from the top down.
