What a soft peg does for a cask

Broach every cask at once and the lot turns.
Tend a few, drawn slow, and they keep for years.

A spile is the little soft peg you ease into the shive of a cask — not to open it wide, but to let it breathe a hair before you tap it and draw. Broach the whole cellar at once, knock every bung out and let the air at all of it, and by the weekend the lot is fretting, working over, turning to vinegar together. A spile is the patient opposite: you start a few, draw them a glass at a time, drop them bright and rack them off clear into the keeping cask. The cellar is not measured by how many casks you broach. It is measured by how many you bring down sound. Lay down what you can tend. Let the rest stay bunged.

The mistake is always the same: you treat the cellar like a tap-room on a feast day — knock every bung out, broach the whole stock at once, and let the air in everywhere. Every project, every half-started idea, every someday-cask stands open to the air together, and not one of them is being drawn down with any care. You work forty things at once, badly. The whole cellar goes hazy and fretting, because nothing that is broached and left ever gets the slow, patient drawing that brings it down bright.

A spile is the opposite habit, on purpose. It is a peg, not a mallet — a soft thing eased in to let a cask breathe just enough, so you can start a few, draw them slow, drop them bright and rack them off clear. You broach only what you can keep an eye on, you draw a glass at a time, and you let the rest of the stock stay bunged and sound rather than open and souring. It feels like doing less. It is doing less, of fewer casks at once, and far more for the few you have actually laid down to draw. Lay down what you can tend. Draw it slow. Let the rest stay bunged.

How it works

  1. 01

    Lay down only what the stillage holds

    A stillage holds only so many casks, and that is its whole virtue. You lay a cask on the rack and start to work it — but only a few, because the rack holds only a few. When the stillage is full, the next cask does not come down; it stays bunged in the back of the cellar, or it waits for another year. The cap is the point, not a flaw.

  2. 02

    Ease a spile in and draw it slow

    A cask laid down is sound, but not yet started. Bunged only means stoppered and sound — kept by the wood, not yet worked by you. So you ease a soft spile into the shive, let it breathe, tap it and draw a glass at a time. The drawing is the whole work of the cellar. A cask broached but left undrawn just frets, goes hazy, and slowly works over to vinegar.

  3. 03

    Let the rest stay bunged and sound

    The stock you did not broach is not souring. It sits bunged, stoppered and sound, keeping clear for years in the cool of the cellar without ever taking a breath of air it does not need. You do not have to broach it now. You do not have to draw it all at once. You let it keep — and the soundness of the few you are drawing, which is the only thing that ever comes down bright, is what leaving the rest bunged buys you.

The stillage

One fictional cellar, partway through a year. A few casks have been laid down on the stillage and started; one has been dropped bright and racked off into the keeping cask, the sound one the rest are worked toward; one was broached and never drawn and is quietly fretting over; one is still bunged in the back, not yet started at all. The cellar line reads the whole cellar at once — it lies level and clear when a few casks are drawn slow, and rolls, frets and turns as the air gets in: too many broached at once, or too many left to sour.

    • BungedStoppered and sound, not yet started. New, sealed, and waiting in the back of the cellar.
    • SpiledA soft spile eased into the shive, letting it breathe a hair before the tap. Begun.
    • DrawingThe tap driven, drawing a glass at a time, slow. In use, and being worked down properly.
    • RackedDropped bright and racked off clear into the keeping cask. The one cured and kept — the one that matters most.

    What the cask is doing

    • FlatUnconditioned and lifeless, or fretting if neglected. Wants tending before it goes off.
    • WorkingLively, fermenting, working hard. The active stage, full of life and needing a watch.
    • ClearingDropping bright, the lees falling out of it. Coming good, settling toward sound.
    • SoundClear and sound, keeps for years. Needs nothing now — it simply holds.

    Who keeps a cellar

    The maker with forty tabs

    Everything is broached, nothing is racked, and the whole cellar is fretting. A spile is the refusal: six casks on the stillage, the other thirty-four left bunged and sound, and for the first time something gets drawn slow enough to come down bright.

    The one who says yes too fast

    Every ask gets a bung knocked out and stands open to the air, half-started, none of it being drawn with care. The stillage gives a hard floor: this many, no more. What does not fit stays bunged — and saying so is the kindest thing for the few already on the rack.

    The keeper of a few works

    You cannot draw every cask at once, and trying sours them all. A handful laid on the stillage, spiled, drawn a glass at a time, racked off bright — and the rest of the stock, real as it is, allowed to keep bunged and sound in the cool until its year comes round.

    Lay one down

    You cannot draw the whole cellar.
    You can keep a few casks sound.

    So stop broaching forty casks at once and letting the lot turn. Lay the few you can tend on the stillage, ease a spile in, draw them slow, drop them bright and rack them off clear — and let the rest of the stock stay bunged and sound until its time. The slow drawing is the only thing that ever brings a cask down bright. Lay down what you can tend. Let the rest stay bunged.
