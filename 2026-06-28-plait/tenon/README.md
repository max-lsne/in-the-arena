# Tenon

**A small page about holding by fit, not by fastening — keeping only the
few commitments cut true enough to take the load, driving them home, and
letting the rest stay loose nails in the jar.**

A mortise-and-tenon joint is a timber frame's whole strength: a square
hole chopped through the post, a matching tongue sawn on the rail to the
line, dry-tried until it pushes home, then driven and drawbore-pegged so
it can never pull back out. Drive forty nails into the same frame instead
and it stands for a season — then the nails work loose, the joints
slacken, and the rack pulls the whole thing out of square to a leaning
parallelogram. The frame is not strong because it has many fastenings. A
crate has more nails in it than any framed building and carries nothing.
It is strong because a few of its joints are cut to fit one mortise
exactly, and seated.

## The pitch in one breath

You treat your commitments like a nail gun — quick, cheap, one bang and
it's "joined" — and you tack on everything that comes past. Forty things
get a fastening driven through them, and not one is cut to fit. A frame is
the opposite shape, on purpose: it holds by fit, not by fastening. You cut
a few joints to one line, you pare them to their fit, you drive them home
and peg them, and you let the rest stay loose nails. It feels like doing
less. It is doing less, fastening fewer things, and far more of the few
that are cut to take the load. Cut what fits. Drive it home. Let the rest
stay loose.

## The hard floor

The frame holds six joints, and no more. When it is full, the next thing
does not get cut — it stays a loose nail in the jar, or it goes out with
the offcuts. The cap is the point, not a flaw: a frame that fastens
everything seats nothing, and racks under the first real load.

## How worked the joint is

- **Rough** — sawn stock, squared but unmarked, not yet chopped or cut.
  New, and not yet worked.
- **Cut** — the mortise chopped and the tenon sawn to the line. Shaped,
  but not yet tried in its hole.
- **Fitted** — dry-tried in its mortise and pared back to a clean
  push-fit. In place, but not yet driven fast.
- **Seated** — driven home and drawbore-pegged; the joint the whole frame
  stands on. The one that matters most.

## What the joint is doing

- **Loose** — slack in the joint, would pull apart under load. Wants work
  before it rattles out.
- **Paring** — being pared with the chisel down to its fit. The real work
  of the frame.
- **Driving** — driven and drawn up tight on the peg. Going fast, taking
  the load.
- **Holding** — tight under load, racks nothing. Needs nothing now; it
  simply holds.

## What's in this folder

- `index.html` — the landing page, with an exploded-then-seated
  mortise-and-tenon joint: a vertical post with a square mortise chopped
  through it, a horizontal rail with a projecting tenon and a brass
  drawbore peg coming together into one clean pegged joint, a marking-gauge
  line scribed true across the bench, and a few rejected bent nails
  scattered to the side — the loose, fastened-on way that works out.
- `styles.css` — an oak-and-bench palette: planed-oak ground and shaving
  light, oak heartwood and bog-oak brown in the joint, brass-peg and
  linseed gold, split-heartwood red for the loose joint and the rack
  alarm, and the true workshop green-grey of the marking gauge. Fraunces
  for the headings, Inter for the body, JetBrains Mono for the trade.
- `script.js` — one fictional frame ("Oak trestle") partway through a
  build, with a handful of joints at different levels and conditions. A
  live *rack line* reads the whole frame at once: it lies flat and true
  when the frame is few and seated, and leans and racks as the joints work
  loose — too many cut, or too many left loose. Keyboard-first, and it
  remembers your frame between visits.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. No build step.
