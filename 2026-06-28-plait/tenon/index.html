What a cut joint does that a nail cannot

Many nails work loose and let it rack.
A few joints cut true hold the frame.

A mortise-and-tenon joint is a frame's whole strength: a square hole chopped in the post, a matching tongue sawn on the rail to the line, dry-tried until it pushes home, then driven and drawbore-pegged so it can never pull back out. Drive forty nails into the same frame instead and it will stand for a season — then the nails work loose, the joints slacken, and the rack pulls the whole thing out of square to a leaning parallelogram. The frame is not strong because it has many fastenings. It is strong because a few of them are cut to fit, exactly, and seated. Cut the few that fit. Drive them home. Let the rest stay loose nails.

The mistake is always the same: you treat your commitments like a nail gun — quick, cheap, one bang and it's "joined" — and you tack on everything that comes. Every message, every half-promise, every someday-project gets a fastening driven through it on the way past, and not one of them is cut to fit. For a week the frame looks finished. Then the nails work loose, every joint slackens at once, and the whole thing racks: leaning out of square, holding nothing, because nothing was ever seated in its mortise.

A mortise-and-tenon frame is the opposite shape, on purpose. It holds by fit, not by fastening — a few joints chopped and sawn to one line, dry-tried until they push home, driven, and pegged so they cannot pull back out. You cut a few things true, you pare them to their fit, you draw them up tight, and you let the rest stay loose nails in a jar. It feels like doing less. It is doing less, fastening fewer things, and far more of the few that are cut to take the load. Cut what fits. Drive it home. Let the rest stay loose.

How it works

  1. 01

    Cut only the joints that fit

    A frame is sound by virtue of a few true joints, not many fastenings. You mark out a joint, chop the mortise, saw the tenon to the line — but only a few, because the frame carries only a few before it racks. When the frame is full, the next thing does not get cut; it stays a loose nail in the jar, or it goes out with the offcuts. The cap is the point, not a flaw.

  2. 02

    Pare it to its fit and drive it home

    A joint cut roughly is shaped, but not yet fast. Cut only means sawn near the line — held by luck, not by fit. So you dry-try it, pare it back with the chisel until it pushes home clean, then drive it and draw it up tight on a drawbore peg. The paring and driving is the whole work of the frame. A joint left cut but never seated just sits loose and slowly works out.

  3. 03

    Let the rest stay loose nails

    The work you did not cut a joint for is not your frame. It rattles in the jar, it bends if you lean on it, and it never carries any load. You do not have to drive it. You do not have to fasten it on in passing. You leave it loose — and the squareness of the frame, which is the only thing that lets it stand under load, is what leaving-it-loose buys you.

The frame

One fictional frame, partway through a build. A few joints have been cut and worked; one is driven home and drawbore-pegged, the joint the whole frame stands on; one was cut and never seated and is quietly working loose; one is still rough sawn stock, squared but not yet marked out at all. The rack line reads the whole frame at once — it lies flat and true when the frame is few and seated, and leans and racks as the joints work loose: too many cut, or too many left loose.

    How worked the joint is

    • RoughSawn stock, squared but unmarked — not yet chopped or cut. New, and not yet worked.
    • CutThe mortise chopped and the tenon sawn to the line. Shaped, but not yet tried in its hole.
    • FittedDry-tried in its mortise and pared back to a clean push-fit. In place, but not yet driven fast.
    • SeatedDriven home and drawbore-pegged — the joint the whole frame stands on. The one that matters most.

    What the joint is doing

    • LooseSlack in the joint, would pull apart under load. Wants work before it rattles out.
    • ParingBeing pared with the chisel down to its fit. The real work of the frame.
    • DrivingDriven and drawn up tight on the peg. Going fast, taking the load.
    • HoldingTight under load, racks nothing. Needs nothing now — it simply holds.

    Who cuts a frame

    The maker with forty tabs

    Everything is started, nothing is seated, and the rack never stops creeping. A frame is the refusal: six joints cut true, the other thirty-four left as loose nails in the jar, and for the first time something is square enough to actually carry the load.

    The one who says yes too fast

    Every ask gets a fastening tacked through it in passing, half-promised, none of it cut to fit. The frame gives a hard limit: this many joints, no more. What does not fit does not get cut — and saying so is the kindest thing for the few that are seated.

    The carer of a few people

    You cannot drive a true joint into everyone, and tacking them all on holds no one. A handful of names, each cut to one mortise, dry-tried, driven and pegged — and the rest of the world's asks, real as they are, left loose in the jar without racking the frame.

    Cut a joint

    You cannot fasten the whole pile.
    You can seat a few joints.

    So stop tacking forty things on with loose nails. Cut the few that fit to one mortise, pare them to their line, drive them home, peg them — and let the rest stay loose nails in the jar. The squareness of the frame is the only thing that ever lets it stand under load. Cut what fits. Drive it home. Let the rest stay loose.
