kingpost · noun · /ˈkɪŋpəʊst/ — the central upright of a roof truss, running from the ridge down to the middle of the tie beam; despite the name it does not prop the ridge up but hangs the tie beam from it, and so stands in tension; from king, the chief member of a frame, and post

Kingpost

A post named for propping, that holds by pulling.

Set two rafters against each other and they make a stiff triangle — but their feet want to kick outward, and a wall cannot stop them. Lay a tie beam across the feet and it stops the spread, taking in tension exactly the thrust the rafters throw sideways. Now the tie beam has a new problem: it is long, and it sags in the middle under its own ceiling. So drop a kingpost from the ridge to the middle of the tie and hang the tie up to the apex. The surprise is in the word: a post should be in compression, propping something up — but this one is in tension, holding the tie beam up. Every force in the frame follows from the geometry of that one triangle, and the pitch of the roof is the dial that sets them all.

Where the load goes

One triangle, three jobs

A truss carries load by making every member either purely stretch or purely squash — no bending, no leverage, just tension and compression running along straight lines to the supports. The kingpost truss is the plainest of them: two rafters leaning together to the ridge, a tie beam across their feet, and a kingpost hung from the ridge to the middle of the tie. Load the roof and the rafters squash — they carry the load down to the walls in compression. But a leaning rafter pushes its foot outward as well as down, and that sideways push is the whole trouble with roofs: it spreads the walls until they crack and the ridge drops.

The tie beam does not hold the roof up. It holds the walls together — taking in tension the exact sideways thrust the rafters would otherwise spend pushing the building apart.

That leaves the tie beam itself, spanning the whole width with nothing under it, sagging under any ceiling hung below. The kingpost answers that: it reaches down from the apex and picks the tie beam up at its middle. So the post carries the ceiling — not by standing under it, but by hanging it from the ridge. A member called a post, doing the work of a rope. Read the frame and you find the two chords that lean — the rafters — in compression, and the two members that pull things together — the tie and the post — in tension.

The one relation it embodies

Three forces, all set by the pitch

Cut the truss at a joint and balance the arrows. With a roof load and a ceiling load bearing down, each wall carries half the total, a reaction R. Let the rafters rise at a pitch θ above the tie. Everything the frame does is three lines of arithmetic:

  • The rafters squash. At the foot, the reaction R pushes up, the rafter pushes down along its own slope; balancing the vertical gives C = R ⁄ sin θ. Flatten the roof and sin θ shrinks, so the compression climbs — a shallow rafter fights a steep uphill battle to carry the same load down.
  • The tie stretches. Balancing the horizontal at the same foot, the tie must hold back the rafter's outward push: T = R ⁄ tan θ. This is the wall thrust, and it is the number that bites — as the pitch flattens, tan θ goes to zero and the tie force grows without bound. Halve the pitch and you roughly double the thrust into the tie and the walls.
  • The kingpost hangs. Balance the joint at the middle of the tie: only the post reaches up from there, so it must carry whatever pulls down — the ceiling load, and nothing else. P = W_ceiling, in tension, independent of the pitch and of the roof load overhead. The post neither knows nor cares how steep the roof is.

So the pitch is the one dial, and it pulls the rafters and the tie in opposite directions. Steepen the roof and the rafters shorten their reach but grow long and slender — apt to buckle; the tie relaxes. Flatten it and the rafters stand almost upright and stout, but the tie and the walls take a punishing thrust. Somewhere between is the pitch that shares the load most evenly — and for almost any span it lands near the pitch old roofs were cut to. The kingpost sits out this whole argument: pitch the roof how you like, it still carries the ceiling and no more.

A steeper roof spares the walls and strains the rafters; a flatter roof spares the rafters and strains the walls. The post carries the ceiling either way.

Four states, from the pitch a load wants to a member past its limit

From sound to overstressed

  1. Sound. The pitch shares the load evenly — the rafters, the tie, and the post all sit inside their limits with margin to spare, and no member is doing work a change of pitch could hand to another. This is the roof the framer cuts: steep enough to spare the walls, shallow enough to keep the rafters stout.
  2. Over-pitched. Safe, but steeper than the load asked for. The tie and walls are barely worked, and the rafters have grown long and slender for nothing — more timber up in the air, more roof for the wind to catch, a taller ridge than the span needed. It stands; it is simply more roof than the job called for.
  3. Under-pitched. Safe, too, but flatter than it should be, and the tie is paying for it. The thrust into the tie beam and the walls runs far higher than a little more pitch would ask, so the joints at the feet strain and the walls feel the push. Strong enough for now, but throwing more sideways force than good practice likes.
  4. Overstressed. One member has run past what it holds — the rafters buckle, or the tie and its heel joint tear, or the post pulls apart under the ceiling it hangs. Which one gives first depends on the truss and the pitch, save for the post: when the ceiling is too heavy for it, no pitch on the dial will save it, because the post carries that load whatever the roof does.

Three who read the same frame

The same truss, three trades

The framer

the joints

Knows the whole truss lives or dies at the feet, where the rafter meets the tie. That heel joint carries the full thrust in a notch of end grain, and end grain in shear is the weakest thing in the frame — so it is cut long, strapped with iron, and never trusted to the timber alone. Hangs the kingpost from the ridge with a stirrup, not a tenon, because the post pulls down, and a tenon in tension is no joint at all.

The carpenter

the spread

Has seen the disease of a flat roof: the feet creeping outward, the tie beam straining, plaster cracking along the wall head where the thrust pushes it out. Reaches for pitch before strength — a steeper roof to pull the thrust back down — and keeps the tie in one unbroken length across the whole span, because a tie beam spliced in the middle is a tie beam that has stopped tying.

The engineer

the polygon

Draws the frame as arrows and closes them into a triangle at each joint — the method of joints, or the force polygon Cremona drew a century and a half ago. Reads the rafter as R ⁄ sin θ, the tie as R ⁄ tan θ, the post as the ceiling load flat, and checks the rafter against its buckling length, the tie against its heel joint, the post against its hanger — and adds nothing the polygon did not ask for.
