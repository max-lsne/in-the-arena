What you don't eat fresh, given salt and a cool dark

Not every idea should be eaten the day it is cut.
Some you put down in salt.

A raw notion is green and sharp and asks to be acted on the hour you have it. Most should not be. You lay it down instead in a stoneware crock, pack it in coarse salt, weight it under a clean stone on a plate, and carry it to the cool dark of the cellar. There it draws its own water, the brine comes up cloudy over the top, and you leave it. Now and then you go down to turn it and skim the bloom. Months on — a winter, two winters — the green has soured slow into something cured that keeps for years, and is better than anything you could have made of it fresh.

Most ideas eaten green
go off by Friday.

A new idea is loud and ripe and wants out the door before lunch. So you act on it raw — you start the company on Tuesday's enthusiasm, you send the cold draft the night you wrote it, you build the whole thing on the first version of the thought. And it spoils. Acted on green, most ideas turn in a few days: the heat goes out of them, the obvious flaw you couldn't see in the freshness surfaces, and what looked like a feast on Monday is something you'd rather not have served by Friday.

Brine is the other thing. You take the raw notion and you put it down — in salt, under a weight, in the cool dark where you cannot fuss at it. It draws its own water. The brine comes up. You go down once in a while to turn it and skim the bloom, and otherwise you leave it well alone. The good sour comes slowly, on its own time, and what keeps is keeper than anything you'd have rushed. You cannot hurry a cure. You put it down, you turn it, and one winter it is ready to take down.

How it works

  1. 01

    Cut it and pack it in salt

    Take the raw idea while it is green and lay it straight down — write the one line, name the crock, pack it in coarse salt and close it. No plan, no acting on it today. The salt is what stops it rotting while it keeps. Put it down the hour you have it, and then take your hands off.

  2. 02

    Weight it down in the cool dark

    Set a clean stone on a plate over it so it stays under, carry it to the coolest, darkest corner, and let it draw its own water until the brine comes up over the top. Nothing to watch and nothing to hurry. Out of the light and out of reach is exactly where it needs to be.

  3. 03

    Go down to turn it and skim the bloom

    Every so often you go down to the cellar, lift the stone, turn the crock, and skim the white bloom off the brine. That is the whole of the tending — a few minutes, a season apart. Each turn is a count. The working change is slow and underneath, and your job is only to keep it clean.

  4. 04

    Take it down when it has cured

    One winter you go down, taste it, and it is ready — soured through, cured, keeping. Now you take it off the shelf and act, and it is better than fresh could ever have been. The mother you keep feeding gives bread for years; the crock you cured once feeds you long after the green idea would have spoiled.

The four levels

  • Raw. Just cut, green, still on the board — only just put aside. The notion scribbled this week, the empty crock with one line on the lid. Not yet under the salt; act on it now and it spoils.
  • Crock. Down in the salt under a weight, drawing its own water, the brine just clouding up. The idea you've committed to keeping rather than doing. Real, under at last, but nowhere near ready.
  • Cellar. Kept in the cool dark and turned over a season, the good sour coming on. A draft, a plan, a project working its slow change while you leave it be and go down only to skim.
  • Larder. Cured, shelved, keeps for years — ready to take down when wanted. A sourdough mother fed a decade, a vintage laid down at a birth. The deep idea that only got better for the waiting.

Who keeps one for real

The baker with the old mother

A sourdough starter kept and fed in the same crock since 2016 — flour and water, a turn every day, the bloom skimmed, the sour grown deep and reliable. She has never bought yeast. The bread tastes of ten years of the same cool kitchen. A thing you keep alive by going down to it, not by finishing it.

The novelist's drawer of cold drafts

Two winters ago he put a chapter down and walked away from it on purpose. He goes back twice a year to read it cold and turn a page. It is souring well now — the false notes have surfaced and been skimmed, and the bones that held are harder than the day he wrote them. He will take it down when it is cured.

The luthier seasoning tonewood

Spruce and maple stacked in the cool loft, stickered to let the air through, laid down years before a single instrument is cut from it. Green wood sings flat and warps; wood that has kept a decade rings true. He marks each billet with the year and forgets it. The slow dark does the work no kiln can hurry.

The winemaker laying down a vintage

A cask put down the cold spring her daughter was born, bottled and cellared and not to be touched for eighteen years. She goes down to the racks to turn and check and otherwise leaves it to the dark. The idea and the child cure together. Some things are only worth having because you waited.
