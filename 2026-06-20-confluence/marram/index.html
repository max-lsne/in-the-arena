What holds the hill, given runners and a few hard seasons

You cannot hold a dune by standing on it.
You plant marram, a tuft at a time.

Marram is the blue-green grass that lives where nothing else will — on bare, blowing sand, in salt wind, with the ground moving under it. You push in one tuft and it looks like nothing, a few blades bent flat by the gale. But under the surface it is running: sending pale rhizomes a yard out and a yard down, throwing new shoots up through the very sand that tries to bury it. Tuft finds tuft, the runners cross, and a season on, the loose face has knitted into a mat that holds its shape in a winter storm — and shelters everything you plant in the slack behind it.

Most of what you mean to keep
blows away by Thursday.

A new intention is loose sand. You scatter it on a Monday full of resolve — the dawn walk, the cold page, the call home on Sundays — and it lies there bright and bare on the surface, holding nothing, gripping nothing. Then the first ordinary wind comes through: a bad night, a busy week, a day that goes wrong by nine. And the sand moves. By Thursday there is no sign you were ever there. You did not lack the will. You lacked the roots.

Marram is the other way. You do not try to hold the whole hill at once. You plant one tuft, low and sheltered, and you leave it alone to do the slow work no one sees — sending runners down through the loose sand, taking hold, then reaching sideways to find the next tuft and knit to it. Season by season the mat closes. It stops needing you. And once a stretch is bound, it holds against the gale on its own, and shelters whatever you plant in the calm behind it. You cannot hurry a dune into holding. You can only keep planting tufts, and let the runners do the rest.

How it works

  1. 01

    Plant one tuft, low and sheltered

    Not the whole slope. One intention, set where the wind is least — the smallest version, in the calmest corner of the day. Pushed in, nothing showing yet. A tuft planted in shelter takes; a flag planted on the crest blows out by morning. Begin small and begin low.

  2. 02

    Leave it to send its runners

    The work that holds happens underground, where you cannot watch it. The blades up top do almost nothing; the pale rhizome runs a yard out and a yard down, gripping sand a grain at a time. There is nothing to perform here and nothing to post. Just come back, and let it root.

  3. 03

    Let the tufts knit to each other

    One tuft holds a handful of sand; a dozen tufts that have run together hold a slope. The mat closes when the runners cross — when the morning page leans on the early night that leans on the walk. Plant near what already holds. A practice with neighbours binds; a practice alone is still loose.

  4. 04

    The bound dune shelters what comes after

    A dune that has stood forty winters does not need replanting — it throws its own shoots, takes its own drift, and breaks the wind for everything in the slack behind it. The first hard thing you bound is now the shelter under which the next thing takes easily. You held the sand once; now the sand holds you.

The four levels

  • Seed. A single intention just pushed into the sand, nothing showing yet — the first dark morning, the resolution said out loud once. Loose; the next ordinary wind could take it, and often does.
  • Tuft. A small clump that has taken, a few blades up and a runner gone down. The practice you have actually done a handful of times. Real now, holding a little sand, but still on its own.
  • Sward. A continuous mat of grass binding a whole stretch of slope. A habit knitted to its neighbours, holding its shape through weather. The thing you no longer have to decide to do.
  • Dune. A whole stabilised dune system that now shelters what is planted behind it. A way of living, years deep, that holds against the gale and makes the next hard thing easy.

Who holds the same slope

The coastal warden on the warren

Thirty winters planting marram into the same blowing foredune above the town. A tuft at a time, in the lee of the chestnut paling, ten thousand of them by hand. The blowout by the third groyne opened in one March storm and took four years to heal. He plants in November and waits. The dune is taller than him now.

The man keeping one morning ritual

Eighteen months sober and one thing held: the kettle, the chair, ten quiet minutes before the house wakes. Not the whole new life at once — one tuft, low and sheltered, planted every single morning whether he wanted it or not. It has rooted. On the bad days now, it is the bad days that lean on it.

The writer with her dawn page

One handwritten page before the light, every day since a wet January four years back. It blew away a hundred times before it took. Now it is a sward — knitted to the early night and the walk, holding its shape through deadlines and flu and travel. She does not decide to do it any more. It is simply ground she stands on.

The town holding back the sea

A whole coast's worth of marram, planted across four generations to keep the dune between the houses and the winter tides. Nobody alive planted the first tufts. The crest that breaks the gale was a bare sandhill in their great-grandparents' photographs. They tend it because it shelters them, and it shelters them because someone tended it.
