Geneva · noun · /dʒɪˈniːvə/ — an intermittent-motion gear: a pin on a steadily turning crank indexes a slotted star wheel one step per revolution and a shoulder holds it still between; from the Geneva stop of watchmaking, the croix de Malte, named for the town where it was made

Geneva

A Geneva drive is a shaft that never stops turning a wheel that mostly stands still. A pin sweeps into a slot, carries the star round by exactly one station, and slides out; for the rest of the turn a curved shoulder holds the star dead still, waiting. Continuous in, step-and-hold out — the motion has a stop built into it.

And that is the trap. Any pin dropped into any slot will turn the star intermittently — but it will also snatch it to a start and jerk it to a halt, a step in velocity at each end, an infinite acceleration, a hammer-blow that wears and rings. The Geneva earns its name by the single condition that removes the shock: the pin sits at radius r = a·sin(π/n) from the crank centre — a the shaft spacing, n the slot count — and at that radius alone the pin enters and leaves each slot moving exactly along it, with no sideways snatch, the star eased from rest and set back to rest. Same step, same hold; the difference between a mechanism that batters itself and one that indexes clean is one choice of radius.

The problem the shape solves

Steady drive in, turn-and-hold out

Some machines need motion that stops. Not slows — stops, dead still, on a schedule, and then moves again: a film frame held in the gate while the shutter opens and the lamp prints it, an indexing table paused under a drill while the hole is cut, a tool-changer presenting each tool square and motionless. The drive shaft, meanwhile, wants to spin at a steady rate; stopping and restarting a heavy shaft every cycle is wasteful and violent. The task is to turn one steady rotation into a rhythm of turn-and-dwell at the output, without ever checking the input.

The Geneva does it with two wheels and a pin. The driver turns without pause. For most of its revolution a raised circular locking shoulder on the driver sits inside a matching hollow on the star and holds it still — the star cannot move even if pushed. Then the shoulder's throat comes round, freeing the star, and a pin sweeps into one of the star's radial slots, carries it round by one slot-pitch, 360°/n, and withdraws, just as the shoulder swings back to lock the star at its new station.

One input revolution, one output step. The whole timetable — move now, hold there — is built into the geometry, and needs no sensing and no control to keep.

The one relation it embodies

The pin enters the slot from rest

Set the two shaft centres a distance a apart, the pin at radius r on the driver. The demand that makes the motion smooth is that the pin slip into the slot along the slot's own length — with no velocity across it — at entry, and leave the same way. Geometrically the crank arm must stand perpendicular to the slot at that instant. The slot points straight out from the star's centre, and at entry it stands half a slot-pitch, π/n, off the line joining the shafts. Crank, slot and centre-line then form a right triangle with the right angle at the pin:

  • The pin radius — r = a·sin(π/n). The one radius that makes the star enter and leave from rest, tangent to the slot, with a finite acceleration instead of a snatch. Everything else falls out of this triangle.
  • The index angle — the driver is engaged only while its crank swings through 180°·(n−2)/n either side of the centre-line; the star turns for that fraction, (n−2)/(2n), of every revolution.
  • The dwell — for the rest, (n+2)/(2n) of the turn, the shoulder holds the star still. Four slots: a quarter turning, three-quarters held.

And the violence of the flick is set by the same count. Write m = a/r = 1/sin(π/n), the centre distance in pin radii; the star's speed peaks, at the middle of the index, at (ω_star/ω_driver)_max = 1/(m − 1). Read that downward and the whole character of the drive is in it:

few slots flick hard — a three-slot star whips past six times the driver's speed — many slots index gently but hold briefly; the four-slot cross of the projector sits in the middle, a peak near 2.4×, a clean 90° index, a generous 75% hold.

The bench

Run it, count the slots, read the ratio

Two wheels on their shafts, a apart: the driver on the right, turning steadily, with its pin and its curved locking shoulder; the star on the left, its radial slots cut for n stations. Press Run and watch the pin catch a slot, sweep the star round one notch, and let go as the shoulder swings back to hold it — turn, hold, turn, hold. How many slots the star carries, and so how many steps make a full output revolution. More slots means a smaller pin, a longer and gentler index, and a shorter dwell; fewer means a big pin, a short violent flick, and a long hold. The pin radius r = a·sin(π/n) re-cuts itself to keep the entry tangent every time you change it.

The tempo of the driver, nothing more. The index angle, the dwell fraction and the speed ratios are fixed by the slot count alone — turning the driver faster only runs the same schedule quicker. Below the nominal, the whole cycle plays in slow motion.

Why keep it

The drive that carries a stop

The Geneva is worth a page because it turns a demand about time — move, then hold, then move — into a fact about shape, and answers it with two wheels that need no clutch, no brake, no controller. Cut the pin to the right radius and the star indexes itself, holds itself, and cannot drift, the way an involute tooth obeys the law of gearing by its profile alone, or a cam hands its follower a whole schedule in the swell of an edge. The name is a loan from watchmaking — the Geneva stop, a Maltese cross with one arm left solid to stop a mainspring over-winding, made in the watch town and named for it — and turned from a stop into a drive it became the heart of the film projector, yanking each frame into the gate and holding it still to be lit, twenty-four times a second, behind nearly every reel of film for a century.

It keeps company with the other machines that hand out a schedule and hold to it: the escapement that doles a clock its beat one tooth at a time, the governor that holds an engine's speed against a swinging load, the fusee that keeps a mainspring's pull level as it dies. The Geneva is their cousin that carries a stop in its very motion — steady drive in, staccato out. Run it on the bench, then pull the slot count down to three and watch the same clean index turn into a snatch: the whole discipline is in one radius, and one count of slots.
