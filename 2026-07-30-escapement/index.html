escapement · noun · /ɪˈskeɪpmənt/ — the catch that lets a clock's drive go, one measured beat at a time

Escapement

A clock does not make time. It counts a swing it did not start — and lets its weight go one tooth at a time to keep it counting.

A weight hangs on a cord wound round a barrel, and its pull runs up through a train of wheels. Left alone, the weight would drop, the wheels would blur, and the whole run of the clock would be spent in a few seconds. The escapement is what stands in its way. Tooth by tooth it lets the wheel escape — releases one tooth, locks the next — and at each release it steals a sliver of the weight's push and hands it to the pendulum, to replace what the pendulum loses to the air. The tick you hear is that exchange: a tooth let go, a tooth caught, a nudge paid out. Do it thirty-six hundred times and an hour has passed — but only if the swing between the ticks is true.

Where the beat comes from

Two jobs in one small catch

An escapement does two things at once, and it is easy to credit it with a third it does not do. First, it is a gate: it releases the going train one tooth at a time, so the falling weight is spent slowly instead of all at once. Second, it is a pump: at each release it passes a little of that weight's push to the pendulum, an impulse, just enough to make up for the drag of the air and the pivots — otherwise the swing would die away like any pushed thing left alone.

What the escapement does not do is set the rate. It follows the pendulum and counts it. The count is only as good as the swing it counts.

So a clock is really two machines lightly touching. One is a store of energy paid out slowly — weight, barrel, wheels, escapement. The other is a thing that swings at a rate all its own — the pendulum. They meet for an instant at every tick: the train asks to move, the pendulum says now, a tooth escapes, a nudge is paid, and both go back to their own business until the next beat.

The one rule

The length keeps the time — not the weight

A pendulum's swing takes the same time whether it swings wide or narrow, and that time depends on almost nothing except how long it is:

period ≈ 2π × √(length ÷ gravity). Nothing about the weight of the bob is in it, and — for a small swing — nothing about how far it swings. Only the length.

This is the whole reason a pendulum can keep time at all, and it has three hard consequences. The drive does not set the rate: wind a heavier weight and the clock does not gain — it swings a little wider and keeps the same time. The length does: a pendulum a hair too short beats a hair too fast, and gains through the day; too long, and it loses. And heat is the enemy of length: a steel rod grows as the room warms, so a clock set true in winter will lose in summer — which is why fine clocks carry rods of mixed metals, or a jar of mercury, built to hold their length as the temperature moves.

There is a fourth, quieter consequence. Almost nothing about the swing's width matters — but not quite nothing. Swing very wide and each beat takes a touch longer, so a clock driven too hard loses a little. The regulator's craft is to drive the pendulum just enough to keep it alive and no more, and then to leave the rate to the length.

Four movements, dead to true

From a still pendulum to a clock you set your watch by

  1. Dead. The drive is too weak to pay the pendulum back what the air takes from it. Each swing is smaller than the last; the impulse cannot catch up; the pendulum coasts to a stop and the train locks. A clock that will not start is not broken — it is starved.
  2. Drifting. It runs, but the length is wrong for the rate you want, and it gains or loses through the day. The escapement is doing its work perfectly; it is faithfully counting a pendulum that beats too fast or too slow. Nothing here is failing except the setting.
  3. Keeps time. The length is trimmed so the beat is true, and the drive is set just past what the swing needs to stay alive. The clock gains and loses nothing worth naming across a day, and you stop looking at it against another — which is the only real test of a clock.
  4. Knocking. Far more drive than the swing can use. The pendulum is thrown so wide the pallets slam past their proper drop and the movement knocks — a clock beating itself to wear, and, because the wide swing drags the rate, losing time while it does. Ease the weight: quiet is faster here.

Three who read a beat

The same tick, three trades

The clockmaker

the movement

Cuts the pallets so the drop is clean and the impulse lands square, and sizes the drive to the losses — enough to keep the swing alive with a little to spare, never so much it knocks. Builds a gate that lets go the same amount every time, because an even beat is the whole of the craft.

The regulator

the rate

Never touches the escapement to fix a clock that gains. Raises or lowers the bob a hair to trim the length, and leaves the beat to look after itself. Judges the clock only against a truer one, over days, and trims by the seconds it drops or steals against it.

The horologist

the temperature

Fights the one thing the length cannot ignore: heat. Builds the rod of steel and brass in opposition, or hangs a jar of mercury from the bob, so that as the room warms and the metal grows, the centre of swing stays put — the length held true while the temperature moves under it.
