What you cut, and what is fit to store

Cut hay does not go straight to the barn.
It lies in the field, and you turn it, until it is dry.

Nothing you cut is ready to store the moment it falls. Mown grass lies thick and green in the swath, heavy with water. You rake it up into a windrow — a long, loose row that catches the air — and you turn it, so the damp underside comes up to the sun. Only when a handful cracks as you twist it, cool and sweet and bone-dry, is it fit to bale. Bale it green and it heats in the barn: it moulds, it rots, it can catch fire from its own ferment. Leave it too long and the sun bleaches the feed out of it. So there is a pause between cutting a thing down and storing it away — and the pause is the work.

Two readings, not one

A row is read two ways at once.

How far it has been made — cut, raked, turned — is not the same question as how it fares in the weather right now. A good haymaker asks both of every row before the baler comes.

Grade — how far it is made

  1. Swath — just cut, lying flat and thick where it fell behind the mower. Nothing raked yet.
  2. Windrow — raked up into a long, loose row to catch the air and dry.
  3. Turned — flipped so the damp underside comes up to the sun and the wind.
  4. Baled — dry, gathered, and stored: done, off the field.

Condition — how it fares in the weather

  1. Green — wet and heavy; bale it now and it heats in the barn and rots.
  2. Drying — giving up its moisture to the air, hour by hour.
  3. Dry — nearly there; cool to the hand, but not quite crisp.
  4. Fit — fit to bale: bone-dry, it cracks when twisted, and smells sweet.

The whole of the method

Cut a little. Turn each row. Bale only what is dry.

  1. 1

    Cut only what you can dry

    The field holds seven rows and no more. When it is full the next cut waits, standing uncut — kept, not lost — until one row is baled and taken off. You never mow more than the sun can dry.

  2. 2

    Turn every row

    Rake the swath into a windrow, then turn it grade by grade. Read it on both axes as you go: how far it is made, and how it is faring in the weather.

  3. 3

    Test before you bale

    A row is not fit because it is raked. Twist a handful. If it cracks dry, cool and sweet, it is fit — bale it and cut the next. If it is still green, it goes back to lie in the field.

Who keeps one

For work that is finished, and still not fit to store.

The writer

A draft is done the night you write it and fit to publish a week later. The field is the few pieces you have cut and are letting air — turned once a day — against the itch to bale them green and send.

The maker

A feature that passes its tests is cut, not cured. Lay it in the field. Turn it under real use. Bale it into the release only when it comes up dry — not the afternoon it first went green.

The decider

A choice made hot bales green and heats in the barn. Rake it into a row, let it lie a night, turn it in the morning. If it still reads dry and cool, it is fit.
