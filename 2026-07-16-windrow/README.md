# Windrow

**Cut hay does not go straight to the barn. It lies in the field, and you turn
it, until it is dry — because green hay stored away heats, moulds, and can catch
fire from its own ferment.**

Nothing you cut is ready to store the moment it falls. Mown grass lies thick and
green in the **swath**, heavy with water. The haymaker's craft is the pause after
the cutting: you rake the swath up into a **windrow** — a long, loose row that
holds itself off the ground and lets the air run through — and you **turn** it,
flipping the damp underside up to the sun and wind, once and again, until the
water is gone. Only when a handful cracks as you twist it, cool and sweet and
bone-dry, is it **fit to bale**. Bale it green and it heats in the barn: the
trapped moisture ferments, the bale moulds and rots from the inside, and a
green-baled mow has burned barns to the ground. Leave it too long and the sun
bleaches the feed value out of it. So there is a disciplined space between
cutting a thing down and putting it away, and the whole skill lives in that
space.

Windrow is a small page that holds that shape. It is a field for the few pieces
of work that are *finished but not cured* — the draft you want to publish today
but shouldn't, the decision that needs to air, the feature that works but isn't
dry. You cut only what you can dry; you turn each row; you test a handful before
you bale.

> Haymaking has a word for the turning itself — *tedding*, spreading and lifting
> the row so the wet stuff underneath comes up. The windrow is what you ted. This
> page is about the row, and the patience it asks for.

## The two axes

A row in the field is read two ways at once, and they are not the same reading.

**Grade** — how far it has been made:

- **Swath** — just cut, lying flat and thick where it fell behind the mower.
  Nothing raked yet.
- **Windrow** — raked up into a long, loose row to catch the air and dry.
- **Turned** — flipped so the damp underside comes up to the sun and the wind.
- **Baled** — dry, gathered, and stored: done, off the field.

**Condition** — how it fares in the weather, right now:

- **Green** — wet and heavy; bale it now and it heats in the barn and rots.
- **Drying** — giving up its moisture to the air, hour by hour.
- **Dry** — nearly there; cool to the hand, but not quite crisp.
- **Fit** — fit to bale: bone-dry, it cracks when twisted, and smells sweet.

The two come apart all the time. A row can be well made to **Turned** and still
only **Drying** — raked and flipped exactly right, but the weather has not yet
taken the water out of it. It can sit at **Swath**, never raked, and read **Dry**
after a hot still day, fit in condition but not made into a row you can lift.
How far it is made and how it fares in the weather are different questions, and a
good haymaker asks both of every row before the baler comes.

## The field holds seven

Haymaking does not scale by wanting it to. Every row wants the same slow
attention — raked, turned, tested, baled at the one hour it is fit. Mow more than
the sun can dry and the discipline breaks: rows get baled green because the sky
is clouding and there is no more time, and a green bale is worse than grass left
standing. So the field has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point:
**Windrow holds seven rows.** When the field is full the next cut waits, standing
uncut — kept, not lost — until one row has been dried and baled off. A field
mown past what one spell of weather can cure leaves good work lying green.

## The haze

The whole field is seen at once through the heat-shimmer over it. A line across
the top of the board is that shimmer drawn out: it lies **clear** — a slow, warm,
bright air — when the rows are drying well and few are worked at once, and it
**hangs heavy and grey**, the way haze does over wet ground, as the field fills
with green swaths all lying uncured at once. It is the fastest read there is — you
do not have to walk the field to know it is lying green.

## The shape of the page

Hero, the problem stated plainly, three steps, and then the field: a board of a
few seeded rows you can cut, turn along the grades, rename, and reset as you like
— each read on both axes. No account, no cloud, no build step. Nothing is saved
and nothing is sent; the field is a thing to work, not a barn to keep. Open
`index.html` in a browser; that is the whole of it.
