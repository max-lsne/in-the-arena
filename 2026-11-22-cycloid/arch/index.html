cycloid · noun · /ˈsʌɪklɔɪd/ — the curve traced by a single point on the rim of a circle as it rolls, without slipping, along a straight line; one arch per turn of the wheel, cusp to cusp

Arch

Roll a wheel along a line, and a point on its rim writes an arch.

Paint a dot on the edge of a wheel and roll it along a rail. The dot does not travel in a straight line — it is on the wheel — and it does not go round in a circle — the wheel is moving on. It does a third thing: it writes an arch, rising off the rail in a sharp cusp, sweeping up and over, coming down to a cusp again, one arch for every turn. That curve is the cycloid, x = r(φ − sin φ), y = r(1 − cos φ). Two facts hide in it, dead against intuition: at the bottom, where the dot touches the rail, it is momentarily at rest — the contact point is the pivot the whole wheel is turning about — and at the top it moves at twice the speed of the hub. The height alone is a clean raised cosine, kin to the swash's sine; it is the rolling that bends it sideways into cusps.

The bench

One turn of the wheel, one arch

The wheel rolls along the rail and the marked point traces its curve behind it. A faint line runs from the point to where the wheel touches down: that contact is the instantaneous centre, the point the whole wheel is pivoting about this instant, and the marked point's speed is just its distance from that centre times the turning rate — so the speed falls to nothing at the cusp, where the point is the contact, and rises to twice the hub's speed at the top. Slide the tracing point in off the rim and the cusps soften to rounded humps; slide it out past the rim and the path dips below the rail and loops backward — the paradox of the train wheel, part of whose flange is always travelling the wrong way.

How far out the traced point sits, as a fraction of the wheel's radius. On the rim (1.0) it writes the true cycloid, cusps and all. Inside gives a curtate curve, rounded humps that never stop; outside a prolate one that loops below the rail and runs backward.

How fast the wheel turns. It sets the pace, not the shape — the arch is the same curve drawn quickly or slowly, and the cusp is still a dead stop at any speed.

What the arch is made of

The one relation, read for its surprises

  • The curve is x = r(φ − sin φ), y = r(1 − cos φ), φ the angle the wheel has rolled through. The r·φ is the hub marching forward at the roll; the −r·sin φ and r(1 − cos φ) are the point swinging round the hub. Their sum is the arch.
  • Take the velocity, (r − r cos φ, r sin φ) per unit roll. At the cusp (φ = 0) both parts are zero: the point is at rest. That is not a trick of the algebra — the wheel touches the rail there, the contact point is the instantaneous centre of rotation, and a point sitting on the pivot cannot be moving. At the top (φ = π) the horizontal speed is 2r: the top of a rolling wheel outruns its hub two to one.
  • Move the tracing point to radius ρ and it becomes x = rφ − ρ sin φ, y = r − ρ cos φ. Its horizontal speed is r − ρ cos φ. For a point outside the rim (ρ > r) this goes negative near the cusp — the point travels backward. A train's wheel-flange reaches below the rail-head, so at every instant a slice of the train is moving the wrong way.
  • One arch is exactly 8r long — eight radii, a length with no π in it, which so surprised the seventeenth century that Wren's rectification of it was a small sensation. The area under one arch is 3πr², precisely three times the wheel that drew it.
  • The height alone, r(1 − cos φ), is a plain raised cosine — the same clean rise and fall the swash plate makes, and close kin to a crank's stroke. What makes the cycloid its own curve is the sideways sin φ, the roll smearing that rise along the rail. Turn the same curve upside down and it stops being a curiosity and becomes the answer to two hard questions — the quickest slide and the evenest fall.

A wheel rolls forward, steadily; the dot on its rim stalls, races, and — if it reaches past the rim — turns and runs backward. All of it is one arch.
