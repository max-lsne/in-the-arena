brachistochrone · noun · /brəˈkɪstəkrəʊn/ — from Greek brákhistos, shortest, and khrónos, time: the curve down which a body slides between two points in the least time

Brachistochrone

The quickest way down is not the straight way.

Hold a bead at A and let it slide, frictionless, to a lower point B off to the side. The straight ramp is the shortest road — and a slow one, because the bead crawls at the start, where it has no speed, and never makes it up. Bow the track into a circular arc and it arrives sooner. But the fastest track of all is an upside-down cycloid — the very arch a rolling wheel writes, hung with its cusp at A. It plunges first, steep and vertical, to buy speed while the distance is cheap, then eases toward B. Johann Bernoulli set this as a challenge to the mathematicians of Europe in 1696; Newton, the story goes, solved it in a night. Race the three and watch the cycloid come home first.

The bench

Three tracks, one drop

All three beads leave A at the same instant, from rest, and feel the same gravity; the only difference is the track under them. Watch the order they arrive. The straight bead leads for a heartbeat — it has the shortest way — and then the others, having spent their first moment falling nearly straight down, come past it with the speed that early plunge bought. Drag B, or set its reach and drop, and the race re-runs. Push B far to the side and the cycloid will dive below it and climb back to finish — proof that the least-time path is not the least-distance one.

Why the plunge pays

The one curve, read as a ramp

  • The time down a track is T = ∫ ds ⁄ v, and for a frictionless bead starting from rest the speed depends only on how far it has fallen: v = √(2g·y). So to go fast you must fall early — but falling early is distance spent going down, not across. The brachistochrone is the exact balance of the two, and it is a cycloid with its cusp at the start.
  • The straight line is the shortest path and loses anyway, because the bead idles through the start at low speed. The circular arc, tangent to the vertical at A like the cycloid, plunges too and comes much closer — but only the cycloid gets the balance exactly right the whole way down.
  • The giveaway is this: when B is far to the side, the cycloid passes below it and climbs back up to finish. A bead that overshoots downward and returns beats one that never dips, because the speed banked in the dip more than pays back the climb. Least time is not least distance.
  • The curve is x = a(θ − sin θ), y = a(1 − cos θ), the same two lines as the arch — only now read downward, as a slope a bead runs on, with a and the sweep θ fixed so it threads from A to B. One curve; turned over, it stops describing a wheel and starts solving a race.
  • Snell's law hides inside it. Bernoulli's own trick was to treat the falling bead like a ray of light through ever-faster layers, bending so that sin θ ⁄ v stays constant — and that bending law, integrated, is the cycloid. The same curve that is quickest down is also the one that is evenest in time from any height — the next page.

The short way is the straight way. The fast way falls first, and is willing to go below its own target to get there sooner.
