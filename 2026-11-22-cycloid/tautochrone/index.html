tautochrone · noun · /ˈtɔːtəkrəʊn/ — from Greek tautó, the same, and khrónos, time: the curve on which the time to the bottom is the same from any starting height

Tautochrone

From any height, the same time down.

Line a cup with an upside-down cycloid and set a row of beads on it at different heights. Let them all go at once. They do a thing a round bowl never quite does: they reach the bottom together — not nearly, exactly — and stay in step for every swing after. The far bead has more track to cover, but it starts higher and runs faster, and on this one curve the two cancel perfectly. The reason is clean: on a cycloid the slide is simple-harmonic motion in the bead's own arc length, s̈ = −(g⁄4a)·s, and a harmonic oscillator keeps the same period whatever its amplitude. The time to the bottom is π√(a⁄g), with no height in it. Huygens saw this in 1659 and bent a pendulum onto cycloidal cheeks to make a clock that keeps time however wide it swings — the escapement's problem, solved from the other side.

The bench

Let them go, and watch them meet

All the beads are released at the same instant, from rest, from wherever you set them. On the cycloid cup they arrive at the bottom in lock-step and cross it together forever; the trace below shows their heights over time sharing the same zeros, the same period, nested one inside another. Switch the cup to a circle — matched so the two agree for tiny swings — and the spell breaks: the wide swings run slow, the beads smear apart, and the bottom-crossings fan out. That gap is the error every pendulum clock fights, and the cycloid is the shape that makes it zero.

How big the cup is, the cycloid's a. It sets the pace — a bigger cup gives a longer time to the bottom, π√(a⁄g) — but changes nothing about the beads arriving together; that is the shape's doing, not the size's.

How high the topmost bead starts; the rest are spread below it. On the cycloid the arrival is the same whatever the spread — that is the whole point. On the circle, the wider you spread them, the more they drift.

Why the height cancels

The one curve, read as a cup

  • Measure arc length s from the bottom of the cup. On the cycloid it works out to s = 4a·sin(φ⁄2), and the height climbed is y = s²⁄8a — the height is exactly quadratic in the arc length. A quadratic potential is a spring, and a spring is a harmonic oscillator.
  • So the slide obeys s̈ = −(g⁄4a)·s: simple-harmonic motion in the distance along the curve. Its period is 2π√(4a⁄g), and the time from release to the bottom is a quarter of that, π√(a⁄g) — no amplitude, no height, nothing about where the bead started. That is the tautochrone property, dropped straight out of the algebra.
  • A circle is not quite this. Its arc-length potential is y = R(1 − cos(s⁄R)), quadratic only for small s; the real motion is a pendulum, whose period grows with the swing. Match the circle's radius to the cycloid's bottom curvature (R = 4a) and the two agree for a whisker of a swing — then diverge, the wide circular swings always running a touch slow.
  • The curve is the same x = a(θ − sin θ), y = a(1 − cos θ) as the arch and the brachistochrone — read now as a valley rather than a wheel-track or a ramp. One cycloid is at once the quickest way down and the one whose descent time forgets where it began.
  • Huygens built it. A pendulum bob hung on a string that wraps a pair of cycloidal cheeks swings along a cycloid (the cycloid is its own evolute), so its period stops depending on how far it swings — an isochronous clock. In practice the cheeks' friction cost more than the amplitude error they cured, and the trick fell out of use; but as a piece of geometry it is perfect, and it is the same curve, met a third time.

Drop a bead high, drop a bead low. On a circle the high one dawdles. On a cycloid they touch the bottom in the same breath.
