In the arena · 2026-08-20

fusee · noun · /fjuːˈziː/ — the spiral-grooved cone in a spring-driven clock or watch that equalises the pull of the mainspring, giving the train a constant drive from wound to run down; from Old French fusée, a spindleful of thread, from Latin fūsus, a spindle

Fusee

A mainspring is a broken clock waiting to happen. Wound tight it shoves hard; run down it barely pushes — and a train driven straight from it would gallop when fresh and crawl when tired. The fusee is the fix, and it is nothing but a well-chosen curve turned in brass.

A hanging weight never tires: it pulls with the same force freshly wound or nearly spent, and a weight clock keeps even time for free. A spring is not so kind. A coiled mainspring is a torsion spring, and it obeys Hooke's law — the torque it returns falls very nearly in proportion to how far it is still wound, so it might hand the train three times the push when fresh as it does when almost run down. That would be harmless if a clock did not care how hard it is driven, but every escapement leaks a little of its drive into the swing it is meant only to count, so a bare spring clock gains while it is fresh and loses as it tires. The fusee cancels that by feeding the train a torque that does not change — and it makes that constant torque out of geometry alone, a spiral cone cut so its radius rises exactly as fast as the spring's pull dies.

The problem the cone solves

A spring's push fades; a clock's rate must not

Wind a coiled mainspring and it stores its energy the way any spring does, by being bent away from rest — and it pays that energy back as a torque very nearly proportional to how far it is still wound. Call the torque of a full spring τ_max and of one almost let down τ_min; the ratio between them, for a real spring, is often two or three to one. Across a single winding the drive is not a fixed thing but a sliding one.

A clock could ignore that only if its rate were deaf to its drive. No escapement is. The verge, the anchor, the lever, the balance — each hands the timekeeper a small push on every beat, and each of them lets the size of that push bleed into the rate, so the pendulum or balance swings a touch wider and faster under a stronger drive. Feed it a torque that falls by half over a day and it will gain in the morning and lose by night, an error stitched into the spring itself that no filing of the pallets removes.

The cure is not a better escapement but a steadier supply: give the train the same torque whether the spring is fresh or spent. The fusee is a machine that does exactly that, and its only working part is a radius chosen with care.

The one relation it embodies

A rising arm for a failing force

Hold the spring in a barrel of fixed radius R. The chain wound off the barrel pulls with a tension that is just the spring's torque over that radius,

F = τ_spring / R,

and because R does not change, that tension rises and falls in lockstep with the spring — strong when wound, weak when let down. The chain carries that same tension across to the fusee and pulls the train through the fusee's arbor, so the torque handed on is the tension times whatever fusee radius the chain happens to be riding:

τ_out = F · r(w) = (τ_spring / R) · r(w).

For that output to stay level while τ_spring sinks, the fusee radius must climb in exact inverse proportion to the spring's torque — r(w) ∝ 1 / τ_spring(w). That is the whole design: the fusee's profile is the reciprocal of the mainspring's torque curve, drawn as a solid of revolution. Narrow at the top, where the spring is strong and the chain must ride a short arm; flared at the base, where the spring is weak and needs a long one. And the flare is fixed, not chosen: the biggest fusee radius over the smallest must equal the strongest spring torque over the weakest,

r_max / r_min = τ_max / τ_min.

A spring that sags three to one wants a fusee that flares three to one. The cone you see is the spring's own weakness, turned in brass so the two cancel.

The bench

Wind it, run it down, then take the fusee away

A spring barrel on the left, the fusee on the right, the chain slung between, and along the foot a plot of torque against how far the spring is wound. Draw the wind down and watch the chain climb to the cone's wider turns as the barrel's pull weakens — the two cancel, and the output torque holds flat where the barrel's own torque slides away beneath it. Change the spring droop and the fusee is re-cut on the spot, its flare always the exact reciprocal of the droop. Press Run to let the spring drain from full to empty, or Remove the fusee to drive the train straight off the barrel and watch the drive droop with the spring — the error the fusee was built to erase.

How much the mainspring's torque falls from wound to run down. A stiff, even spring droops little and wants a nearly straight cone; a spring that sags hard wants a sharply flared one, cut to match.

Why keep it

A reciprocal, computed in brass

The fusee is worth a page because it is a mechanism that works a reciprocal without arithmetic. No clockmaker sat down to solve 1 ⁄ τ(w) and then turned the answer on a lathe; the cones were cut to a curve found by trial — filed and tried and filed again until the going stayed even — and the shape they arrived at is the inverse of the spring's failing torque, discovered in metal a century before the calculus that would have named it. The spring's weakness and the cone's flare are the same fact written twice.

It keeps company with the other small governors of constancy. The centrifugal governor flattens an engine's speed by letting rising balls throttle the steam; the escapement meters a clock's push into equal beats; the fusee holds the drive itself still while the spring behind it dies. Each is a machine built around a single stubborn idea — that one quantity shall not move while another runs away — and each solves it not with cleverness but with a shape. John Harrison trusted a going fusee to hold his sea-clock even through the very act of winding it, because at sea the seconds lost while you turned the key were seconds of longitude, and a ship cannot afford to forget where it is. Wind the spring on the bench, let it run down, and watch the drive refuse to fall: that refusal is the fusee, and it is only a radius, well chosen.
