cotter · noun · /ˈkɒtə/ — a flat wedge-shaped key driven through a slot across two members to draw them tightly together and hold them there; its slight taper both multiplies the driving force into a large pull and, cut shallow enough, keeps the key from working back out; from Middle English cotters, a fastening pin

Cotter

A wedge so slight it can pull, yet hold what it pulled.

Two members meet end to end, and you must draw them together and keep them there. A bolt would do it, but a bolt needs a head, a nut, and a thread cut true. The old answer is plainer: cut a slot across both, offset the slots by a hair, and drive a flat tapered key through. As the key goes in, its taper wedges the two slots apart along the joint — and because it is a wedge, a light tap on its head becomes a heavy, steady pull along the members. Then comes the trick that makes it a fastener and not just a lever: cut the taper shallower than the friction on its faces, and the joint, straining to spit the key back out, cannot. The key holds itself. Too shallow, though, and it seizes; too steep, and it creeps loose. The taper is the one dial, and it sets the pull, the hold, and the price of both.

Where the force goes

One wedge, two jobs

A cotter is the humblest of the inclined planes — a flat bar, a little wider at the head than the point, so its two long edges are not quite parallel. Drive it through a slot cut across two members and each edge bears on a slot wall; as the key descends, the widening section forces the walls apart along the line of the joint. Drive it a distance down and the joint draws up by the taper's small fraction of that distance. That is the first job, and it is pure lever: a small motion of the hammer traded for a large force in the members. The shallower the taper, the harder the key pulls for the same blow.

A cotter turns the direction of a force as much as its size — a sideways tap on the head becomes an end-on pull in the members, multiplied by however gentle the taper is.

The second job is the one a plain lever cannot do: to stay. Take the hammer away and the joint, under whatever load it carries, pushes back on the key, trying to expel it the way it came. Now the taper cuts the other way. The load bears on the key's faces as a normal squeeze, and friction on those faces resists the key sliding out. If the taper is gentle enough that its tendency to slide out is smaller than the friction holding it in, the key sits fast — self-locking. The very wedge that made a weak pull into a strong one also makes the key its own nut. Read the joint and you find two members drawn up in tension, a key across them squeezing its bearing faces, and the friction on those faces standing quietly between holding and letting go.

The one relation it embodies

Taper against friction

Let the key's taper be t — its rise per length, the 1 in the fitter's 1 : n — and let friction on its two bearing faces be μ. Balance the key as it is driven, and as it is held, and the whole of it is three short lines:

  • The pull. To seat the key against a draw T in the members, you must overcome the taper and the friction on both faces: the drive needed is F = T · (t + 2μ). So the draw won per unit of drive is T ⁄ F = 1 ⁄ (t + 2μ) — the mechanical advantage. Cut the taper finer and the same blow pulls harder.
  • The hold. With the hammer gone, the draw T tries to expel the key; its taper helps it out by T·t, and friction resists by 2μ·T. The key stays put — self-locking — exactly when t ≤ 2μ: the taper is shallower than the friction. This is the whole reason a cotter needs no nut. Flatten the taper and the margin grows; steepen it past the friction and the joint spits the key.
  • The price. The taper cannot cheat the load. Whatever the draw, it bears on the key's faces and the slot walls; the key sees it as shear across its section and the timber sees it as crushing on the bearing. A finer taper pulls harder and holds surer, but does nothing to relieve the members — the draw they carry is set by the joint, not the key.

So the taper is the one dial, and it pulls two ways at once. Steepen it and the key seats with a light blow and knocks out easily — but past the friction it will not stay under load. Flatten it and the hold grows unshakeable and the pull enormous — but the key seats only under a heavy blow, travels a long way to draw the joint up, and, cut too fine, seizes: it will neither drive home nor strike free. Between them is the taper the fitter cuts — shallow enough to lock with margin, steep enough to still strike out with a drift and a hammer. And that taper depends on nothing but the friction: rough faces lock at a bold taper, greased steel needs a fine one.

A cotter holds when its taper is gentler than the grip of its own faces. Everything a fitter knows about keys is that one line, cut into brass and oak.

Four states, from the taper a joint wants to a member past its limit

From holding to overstressed

  1. Holds. The taper is shallower than the friction, with margin to spare, yet not so shallow it seizes — self-locking, and still strikeable free with a drift. The key draws the joint tight, sits fast under load, and every member is inside its limit. This is the key the fitter drives: it needs no nut, and it comes out when it is meant to.
  2. Seized. Self-locking, but far too shallow. The hold is beyond any doubt — and that is the trouble: the key seats only under a heavy blow, has to travel a long way to draw the joint up, and, once home, will not strike out without splitting something. Safe, and a curse to a fitter who ever has to take the joint apart.
  3. Works loose. The taper is steeper than the friction, so the key is not self-locking. It drives home with the lightest tap and pulls a fair draw — for a moment. But the joint's own load, helped by any jar or vibration, works the key steadily back out, and the draw bleeds away. A key that will not stay is no fastener; this one needs a nut, a split pin, or a shallower cut.
  4. Overstressed. The draw has run past what the joint holds — the key shears across its slot, or the bearing crushes into the softer member, or the member itself tears across the section the slot left it. Which gives first depends on the joint, and no taper on the dial will save it: the key sets how the load is held, not how large it is. To relieve it you must ease the draw or widen the bearing.

Three who read the same key

The same cotter, three trades

The fitter

the drift

Knows a cotter by how it strikes. Seats it with a few firm blows on the head, feels the joint draw up tight, and expects it to stay when the hammer stops — a key that keeps sliding is cut too steep. To free it he backs a drift against the point and strikes upward; a key that will not come has been cut too fine. Files a fresh key with a taper he trusts by eye — bold on a rough oak strap, fine on a greased steel rod — and never trusts a nut to a taper the friction alone would hold.

The wheelwright

the draw

Uses the key to pull, not merely to pin: a cotter through the strap of a shaft, driven a little each season, takes up the wear and keeps the joint tight where a bolt would need re-threading. Reaches for the taper before the hammer — a gentler key to pull harder on a stubborn joint — and watches the bearing, because a key that draws hard enough to crush the timber it bears on has stopped fastening and started splitting.

The engineer

the cone

Draws the taper angle against the friction angle and reads the whole story off the picture: inside the friction cone the key locks, outside it creeps. Sizes the draw as T ⁄ (t + 2μ) per blow, checks the key in double shear and the socket in crushing, and cuts the taper near 1 : n = μ with a margin — shallow enough to lock, steep enough to serve. Adds a gib to spread the bearing, and a split pin behind the point for the day the friction is less than he hoped.
