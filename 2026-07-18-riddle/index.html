One gauge, and the pile sorts itself

You cannot pick a heap apart by hand.
Tip it on a screen, shake, and let one mesh sort it.

A riddle is a coarse sieve — a hooped screen you tip a heap onto and shake. You do not weigh each stone and decide. The mesh decides, and it asks one question only: are you smaller than this gap? What is fine enough falls through to the pan; what is too big stays over on the mesh. Sort by one gauge and a hopeless heap falls into a clean gradient in a few shakes. Sort by ten at once and you are back to picking it over by hand.

Two places a thing can land

Through the mesh, or over it.

A riddle has no opinion about worth. It has a gap, and it reads every piece against that one gap. Everything ends up in one of two places — and which place is the whole answer the riddle gives you.

Through — the fines

Small enough to fall clean through the gap and into the pan. These are the quick, light pieces — the ones a single gauge settles for good. Do them on the spot, or let them go; either way they are off the screen and out of the way.

Over — the tailings

Too big to pass. They stay up on the mesh, and that is not a verdict of importance — only of size against this gauge. The tailings are what you riddle again, through a finer screen and a different question, until the heap is graded all the way down.

One gauge, a whole gradient

A graded stack sorts by size — and only size.

Stack the screens coarsest on top, finest at the bottom, a solid pan beneath. Tip the heap in and shake, and every piece settles on the first screen too fine for it to pass. One shake, one gauge — and the pile lies in bands from boulder to dust.

  1. Stone — won't pass any screen. The project you keep meaning to start; it stays up on the coarse mesh, whole and heavy, until you break it smaller.
  2. Cobble — a week's weight. Passes the coarse screen, caught by the next. Real work that needs planning before it moves.
  3. Gravel — an afternoon's worth. A task with edges you can already see. It falls a rung or two and rests.
  4. Grit — small and quick, but not nothing. Nearly through — caught only on the finest mesh.
  5. Dust — the two-minute thing. Falls clean through every screen to the pan. Do it as it drops, or blow it away.

Size here stands for whatever single gauge you choose — bulk, cost, urgency, risk. The riddle does not care which. It only asks that you pick one, and it will spread the heap for you along it.

The whole of the method

Tip it in. Pick one gauge. Shake, and read the bands.

  1. 1

    Tip the heap onto the screen

    Everything goes on together — the boulder and the dust, unsorted. You do not decide anything by hand. The riddle holds only so much at once; overfill it and it blinds itself with dust.

  2. 2

    Gauge each piece by one question

    Set each piece's size against a single gauge — how big, how heavy, how long. Not ten questions at once. One. The one you pick is the mesh the whole heap is read against.

  3. 3

    Shake, and let the bands appear

    Shake and the dust settles to the pan, the boulders stay up top, and the middle grades itself between. Take the fines off. Riddle the tailings again through a finer question. The heap is beaten, one gauge at a time.

Who keeps one

For any heap too big to pick apart by hand.

The maker

The backlog is a tip of mixed rubble. Riddle it by one gauge — can it ship this week? The dust falls to the pan and gets done between things; the boulders stay up top, named as the real work, not lost in the fines.

The overwhelmed

A full inbox reads as one solid weight until you gauge it. Ask one question — is this two minutes? — and half of it falls straight through. What stays over is a much smaller, honester pile.

The planner

You cannot size a quarter by importance and effort and risk all at once. Riddle by one, read the bands, then tip the tailings through the next screen. A gradient, built one gauge at a time, beats a heap argued over all at once.
