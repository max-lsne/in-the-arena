# Slake

**Quench the whole kiln at once and it flashes off and sets dead. Slake a
little, knock it up fat, and leave it in the tub — it only comes richer.**

Lime is burned in a *clamp* kiln — stone and fuel stacked in layers and fired
for days until the limestone gives up its gas and comes out as *quicklime*.
Quicklime is a hungry, dangerous thing: add water and it does not just wet, it
*slakes* — it boils, hisses, throws steam, and swells to twice its bulk as it
turns to lime putty for mortar and plaster. That reaction is the whole craft,
and it is easy to get wrong in one direction: greed. Tip water over the whole
kiln at once and it flashes off in a violent boil, spits, burns off its
strength as steam, and sets hard and dead in the pit — a week's stone wasted.
So the limeburner slakes a *little* at a time: a barrowful into the tub, water
run in slow, knocked up to a smooth fat putty, and then — this is the part no
one has patience for — *left*. Lime putty only improves with keeping; months
under water in the tub and it comes back fatter, stickier, richer than the day
it was slaked. The strength was never in slaking the whole kiln. It was in
slaking a little and letting it fat.

Slake is a small page that holds that shape. It is a board for the few things
that only come right slaked slow and left to mature — the ones you are knocking
up and keeping — against the many you quenched all at once in a hurry and set
hard and dead.

## The two axes

A batch is read two ways at once, and they are not the same reading.

**Stage** — how far along the lime has come:

- **Stone** — raw limestone, not yet burned; inert, waiting for the kiln.
- **Burnt** — drawn from the kiln as quicklime; caustic, hungry, ready to slake.
- **Slaked** — water run in, boiling down and knocking up to a fat putty.
- **Fat** — matured under water in the tub for months; fat, sticky, and rich.

**Condition** — how the lime is tempering, right now:

- **Cold** — raw or drawn but untouched; nothing reacting yet.
- **Boiling** — slaking violently, hissing steam; hot, and easy to spoil.
- **Fatting** — knocked up thick and smooth; coming right in the tub.
- **Rich** — long matured, fat and workable; better than the day it was slaked.

The two come apart all the time. A batch can be well *slaked* and still only
*boiling* — knocked up, but not yet settled and fat. It can be freshly *burnt*
and left *cold* — good quicklime waiting, not yet spoiled by a rushed quench.
How far along it has come and how it is tempering are different questions, and
the limeburner asks both every time he walks the tubs.

## The yard holds six

Slaked lime is a live thing kept under water for months. It wants tending — the
tub kept topped so the putty never dries and carbonates, the fresh boil watched
so it does not flash off, the matured fat knocked up before it stiffens. That
is slow, patient work, and a limeburner can only mind so many tubs. Quench the
whole kiln at once and the far tubs boil over unwatched, flash off their
strength, and set dead — the more stone you slaked, the more you spoiled. So the
yard has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point: **Slake holds six
tubs.** When the yard is full the next barrow waits as quicklime, dry and
covered, until one tub has fatted and been knocked up and used. A yard slaked
beyond what one burner can walk would flash off, tub by tub, before the mortar
was ever wanted.
