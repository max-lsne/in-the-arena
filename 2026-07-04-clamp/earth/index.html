What keeps through a fallow winter

Heap the whole crop,
and the middle rots.

A root clamp is how you keep roots through the winter without a cellar: a long ridge laid on straw, thatched over, covered with a skin of earth patted firm — and a wisp of straw left at the crown so it can breathe. That last part is the whole craft. Roots are alive; they throw heat and damp long after they're lifted. Heap the entire crop in, wet and airtight, and the middle sweats, heats, and rots by Christmas — the more you saved, the more you lose. So you clamp a sound few: ripe, dry roots in a ridge you can walk the length of, earthed but breathing, opened a little at a time so you can read them for heat. A sound few, stored right and walked, against the whole harvest heaped in and left to heat.

How it works

01

Pull it, and lay it up dry

A thing you mean to keep through a fallow time starts as a pulled root — lifted ripe, left to dry and firm its skin, then laid up on a bed of straw where the frost can't reach it.

02

Earth it, and read the ridge

A clamp is kept by reading it. You move it along — pulled, strawed, earthed, opened — and you read how it fares: damp, sweating, keeping, sound. How far into keeping it has come and how it is faring in there are not the same thing.

03

Leave it a breath, and walk it

A clamp lives or dies by its breather. Heap in more than you can walk and the middle goes airless, sweats unread, and rots outward. A sound few, felt for the warm patch and kept breathing, keep sweet the whole winter through.

The clamp

A farmer walks only as many ridges as he can feel the length of.

A clamp is a living store kept on trust for months. For the whole winter it wants walking — the crown felt for the warm patch that means it is heating in the middle, the breather kept clear so it does not go airless, the odd turned root drawn out before it taints its neighbours. That is slow, weekly work, and a farmer can only walk so many ridges. Heap the whole crop into one great pit and the middle goes unread, sweats, and rots outward — the more you stored, the more you lose. The cure is never a bigger pit. It is fewer ridges, laid where you'll pass them, and felt every week.

So the clamp has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point. Earth holds seven roots. When the clamp is full the next root waits in the field, topped and drying, until one has been opened and taken in. A pit heaped beyond what one farmer can walk would heat in the middle before the frosts were even done.
