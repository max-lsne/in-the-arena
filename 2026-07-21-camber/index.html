Build in the sag before it happens

You do not build a beam
to the shape you want it to hold.

A camber is the slight upward curve a maker builds into a beam on purpose — a crown you can sight down the edge before it is ever loaded. It looks like a mistake. It is the opposite. Under its own weight and everything it carries, the beam will settle; left flat, it settles below the line into a visible dip. So you build it proud by exactly what the load will take away, and the load itself brings it true. The good camber is the one you cannot see once it is carrying.

Every span settles under what it holds

Flat is not the shape you build. It is the shape you arrive at.

A beam is not rigid. Loaded, it bends — and the longer the span and the heavier the load, the more it bends. Build it dead level and the only direction left to go is down. Two things fight over where it ends up, and the maker sets one of them before the load is ever there.

Deflection — what the load takes

The distance a loaded beam drops below where it started. It is not a flaw in the timber; it is the fixed answer to a span and a weight. You cannot argue it away — a long beam under a real load will come down. The only question the maker gets is where it comes down from.

Camber — what you build in

The upward crown you put in on purpose, before the load arrives, so there is somewhere for the beam to settle down to. Match it to the deflection and the beam lands on the line. It is the one move in the whole business that is invisible when it works and glaring when it does not.

The crown is a judgement, not a constant

Too little and it dips. Too much and it humps.

There is a right amount of camber, and it is set by the span and the load — not by taste. Build in less than the beam will lose and it sags under the line; build in more and it stands proud forever, a hump that never comes down. The window that reads as true is narrow, and the whole skill is landing in it.

  1. No camber — dead flat as built. The honest-looking choice, and the wrong one. The load has nowhere to settle but below the line; a long span drops into a dip you will see from across the room.
  2. Shy — less than the deflection. A crown, but not enough of one. The load eats it and keeps going. Better than flat, still short of true; the dip is smaller but it is there.
  3. True — camber equals deflection. The mark you are aiming for. The beam is built proud by exactly what the load will take, and the load brings it level. You cannot see the camber because it did its job.
  4. Proud — more than the deflection. Over-built. The load cannot pull it all the way down; the beam keeps a hump. On a floor you would feel it underfoot; on a bridge you would see it against the sky.
  5. Way proud — a permanent arch. The camber you would build if you feared the sag and never measured it. It never settles; it just stands there, curved, announcing that nobody ran the numbers.

The crown here stands for any correction you build in ahead of a load you know is coming — the buffer, the head start, the over-provision. Aim it at the deflection you can actually expect. Build for a sag that never comes and you are left with a hump.

The whole of the method

Set the span and the load. Read the sag. Build in its opposite.

  1. 1

    Set the span and the load

    Start from the two things you do not control: how far the beam has to reach, and how much it has to carry. Between them they fix the deflection. Nothing you build in changes it — it only changes where the beam falls from.

  2. 2

    Read the deflection

    The bench works out how far this span under this load will drop, and draws the ghost of where a flat beam would end up — below the line, in the dip you are trying to avoid. That number is your target: it is exactly how much crown to build.

  3. 3

    Build in the camber and land it true

    Dial the crown up until the loaded beam lands on the datum. Short of it, the beam dips; past it, it humps. The reading turns green in the narrow band where the camber and the deflection cancel — where the correction disappears under the load.

Who builds one

For anyone who corrects for a load before it arrives.

The builder

A dead-level joist reads as a sagging one the day the furniture goes in. Camber the span to the load it will carry and it settles flat — the floor is true because it was built wrong on purpose.

The planner

A schedule built to the exact hours available sags the moment reality loads it. Build in a crown of slack where you know the weight lands, and the plan settles onto the deadline instead of below it.

The estimator

Quote a job to the penny and the first surprise puts you under. Camber the number by what the unknowns will cost, and you land on the figure when the load comes in — not beneath it, and not so proud you lose the work.
