cam · noun · /kæm/ — a rotating piece whose shaped edge drives a follower through a set pattern of movement, once per turn; from Dutch kam, comb, the projecting tooth of a wheel, cognate with English comb

Cam

A cam is a motion drawn as a shape. The whole timetable a follower must keep — risen this far by this angle, held level here, brought back by there — is stored in the swelling radius of an edge and read off once every revolution. Nothing computes it in the moment; the shape is the schedule.

And that is the trap. Any lump on a shaft will lift a follower and drop it; the demand a cam really answers is how — how steadily, how squarely, with what violence at the corners. Cut the rise as a plain straight ramp and the follower must leap from still to full speed in no angle at all: a step in velocity is an infinite acceleration, a hammer-blow at every start and stop. Round the ramp into simple harmonic motion and the acceleration is bounded, but it still jumps where the rise meets the dwell — a step in acceleration is an infinite jerk, and the follower rings. Only one common law brings the acceleration itself gently to zero at both ends, matched to the still dwell it joins: the cycloidal. Same lift, same rise, same dwell — and the difference between a machine that pounds itself apart and one that runs quiet is a choice of curve.

The problem the shape solves

A fixed timetable, delivered once a turn

Many machines need a part moved through the same stroke again and again, in step with a turning shaft: a valve opened just as a piston wants air and shut before it fires, a needle dipped as cloth advances, a tool fed in, held, and withdrawn. You could try to time each one with linkages and stops, but the timing would drift and the pattern would be hard to change. The cam does it with a single shaped wheel: the follower rides the edge, and the edge's rising and falling radius is the stroke, laid out around the turn.

Write the follower's height as s(θ) — how far it has lifted at cam angle θ — and the cam is just that graph wrapped into a disc: its radius at every angle is R(θ) = R_base + s(θ). Turn the shaft at a steady speed and the follower re-lives s(θ) exactly, every revolution, forever. The displacement diagram, that graph of lift against angle, is the whole design; the metal is only its shadow.

But a follower does not feel displacement. It feels the second derivative — the acceleration, and so the force. Two cams with the identical lift can treat their followers as gently or as brutally as the difference between their curves.

The one relation it embodies

The law lives in the derivatives

Rise a follower through lift h over a rise angle β, at shaft speed ω. Differentiate the lift twice with respect to time and every law shares the same skeleton — a velocity that scales as h·ω/β and an acceleration that scales as h·ω²/β² — but the shape of those curves, and their peaks, are the law's own:

  • Constant velocity — s = h·(θ/β). The follower climbs at one speed. Elegant on paper, but velocity jumps 0 → v → 0 at the ends, so the acceleration is a pair of impulses — infinite. The corner cannot be run.
  • Simple harmonic — s = (h/2)(1 − cos(πθ/β)). Velocity is a smooth half-sine, zero at both ends. But the acceleration is a half-cosine: it starts and ends at its full value and steps to the dwell's zero. Bounded force, but an infinite jerk at each junction — the follower is snatched, and rings.
  • Cycloidal — s = h·(θ/β − sin(2πθ/β)/2π). Velocity and acceleration are both zero at both ends. The acceleration is a full sine over the rise, rising from nothing and returning to nothing, matched to the dwell it meets. The jerk stays finite. Its peak acceleration is actually the highest of the three — and it is still the one that runs smooth.

That last line is the quiet lesson. The cycloidal law does not win by pushing less hard; at 2π · h·ω²/β² it pushes harder than simple harmonic's (π²/2) · h·ω²/β². It wins by never surprising the follower — no step in acceleration, no infinite jerk, no ringing. And every one of these peaks scales as 1/β²:

halve the rise angle and you quadruple the acceleration — the same lift, hurried, is four times as violent.

Three laws for the same rise and fall. Constant velocity ramps straight and hammers with infinite acceleration at every corner. Simple harmonic rounds the speed but steps the acceleration at the dwells — an infinite jerk. Cycloidal eases the acceleration to zero at both ends and runs smooth, at the cost of the highest peak of the three.

How far the follower is raised between the low dwell and the high. Taller lift, taller everything — the velocity and the acceleration both scale straight up with it. The base circle underneath is fixed; the lift is the swell added onto it.

How much of the revolution the rise is given — the return is given the same, and the two dwells share what is left. Hurry the rise into a smaller angle and the lift is unchanged but the acceleration climbs as one over β squared: halve the angle, quadruple the force.

Why keep it

The motion is the metal

The cam is worth a page because it turns a demand about time into a fact about shape. The demand — move this follower through exactly this stroke, in exactly this phase with the shaft, again and again without drift — is a statement about every instant of the turn; the cam answers all of it at once by being cut to the right edge, and then needs no thought to obey, the way an involute tooth satisfies the law of gearing by its shape alone, or a hanging chain finds its catenary without solving for it. The cam is old — the trip-hammers of Han foundries, the shafts of pegs in Al-Jazari's water-clocks and automata around 1206, the lobes on every engine's camshaft opening its valves on the beat — but the discipline hidden in it is young: that the lift is the easy part, and the whole art is in the second derivative, in cutting a curve whose acceleration will not surprise the follower.

It keeps company with the other machines that hand out a schedule and hold to it: the escapement that doles a clock its beat from a failing push, the governor that holds an engine's speed against a swinging load, the fusee that keeps a mainspring's pull level as it dies. The cam is their cousin that carries the whole programme on its rim. Turn it on the bench, then switch the law and watch only the acceleration lane: the lift barely changes, the corners change utterly — a hammer, a ring, or nothing at all. That is the cam, and it is only a graph, wrapped into a wheel.
