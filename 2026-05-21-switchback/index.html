Some ridges do not have a straight line to the top. You get there by going sideways, and sideways again, and sometimes a hundred feet back down to find the bench that goes up. Switchback is a small page for keeping the trail honest as you cut it. One leg at a time. The shape is the work.

The straight line
lies.

Most roadmaps draw an arrow. The arrow is short and straight and goes up. The arrow does not know about the unclimbable face on the south side, the rotten cornice at the col, the half-day traverse that ends at a cliff and sends you back to the bench. The arrow is for slide decks.

Switchback is for the work. A leg is the smallest thing you can plan: a piece of the trail you can see the end of. You name it, you give it a shape, and you walk it. Some legs gain altitude. Some lose it. Most of the long ones are sideways. The shape of all the legs together is the climb.

How it works

  1. 01

    Name one long ridge

    One climb. Hard. The kind you measure in months. The migration. The book. The roof. The recovery.

  2. 02

    Cut a leg

    The smallest piece of trail you can see the end of. Give it a name, an aspect, an altitude.

  3. 03

    Walk it. Or redraw it.

    If the line goes, set it. If it cliffs out, redraw it — take it off the map and cut a new one.

  4. 04

    Read the shape

    The profile is your climb. The traverses, the doublebacks, the steep pitches that gain a thousand feet in a morning.

The three aspects

  • Up. A steep leg. Short, hard, big gain. The push.
  • Across. A long sideways traverse. Small gain. The bench you walk to clear the face.
  • Back. A deliberate doubling back. You lose altitude so the next leg can go up.

Who walks a switchback

The engineer mid-migration

Six months into a payments rewrite. Half the legs are read-replicas and shadow writes. The straight arrow lied to the planning doc in January. The trail tells the truth in May.

The composer rewriting the second act

An opera at the third draft. Four legs were across — translating one motif into the woodwinds. Two were back. One was a thousand feet of climb in a single morning.

The carpenter re-roofing alone

Twelve weekends, one farmhouse, no help. The crickets and the chimney flashing are across legs, slow and sideways. The first dry storm is the summit.

The founder after a pivot

The original arrow is gone. The trail is new and the bench is wider than the deck implied. Each leg is a customer call. The shape is starting to look like up.
