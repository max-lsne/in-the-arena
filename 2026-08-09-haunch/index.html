haunch · noun · /hɔːntʃ/ — the flank of an arch, the quarter between the springing at the foot and the crown at the top, where the stone leans out over nothing

Haunch

An arch does not fail at the crown. It fails at the haunches — the flanks, a third of the way up — where the thrust presses hardest against the stone.

A masonry arch is dry logic: wedge-shaped stones, no mortar worth the name, standing on nothing but each other's weight. It holds because the load coming down through it can find a path — a line of thrust, the running total of all the compression — that stays inside the ring of stone the whole way from the keystone to the ground. Draw that line and you have the arch's secret: it is not the shape of the stones that matters but whether this invisible line can be threaded through them. And when an arch is pushed too far, the line does not snap at the top where a beam would break. It sags, at the flanks, at the haunches — and when it sinks below the belly of the stone there, the joint opens on the soffit and the ring turns on that edge like a hinge. Watch an old arch fail and the crack is never at the crown. It is a hand's breadth up from the spring, out on the haunch, on the underside, where the line of thrust sinks closest to the edge.

Where the line comes from

The arch is only as good as the line you can draw in it

Set a keystone at the top and let its weight fall. It cannot fall straight — the stones beside it are in the way — so it leans, and its weight is passed sideways and down into the next stone, and that one leans into the next, and so on, a chain of pushes running down each flank to the ground. Join the points where that push crosses each joint and you have drawn the line of thrust: the single path the compression actually takes through the arch. It hangs inside the stone like an upside-down chain — a hanging chain finds the shape of pure tension, and an arch is that shape flipped into pure compression.

The one rule is Heyman's safe theorem, and it is almost too simple: if you can draw any line of thrust that stays inside the ring of stone from crown to springing, the arch will stand. It does not have to be the real one. It only has to exist.

So the arch is not tested by its stresses — the stone is barely working, a cathedral loads its masonry to a fortieth of what would crush it. It is tested by geometry. Is the ring thick enough, and shaped well enough, that a thrust line can be threaded down it without ever poking through the back or the belly of the stone? A thick ring holds a whole family of lines and stands easily. A thin ring pinches that family down to almost nothing, and past a limit holds none at all — and then, no matter how sound the stone, the arch cannot stand, because there is nowhere for the load to go.

The one bargain

Ease the ground and the line sags onto the haunch

A thrust line is not free to sit wherever it likes; it is pulled taut between two things. Its horizontal thrust — how hard the arch shoves outward on the ground at each foot — sets how tightly it is drawn. Push hard on the abutments and the line pulls up taut through the ring. Let the abutments give even slightly, and the thrust eases, the line sags, and the slack gathers exactly where the arch is steepest and carries most: down onto the haunches, against the soffit.

Draw the thrust too high — squeeze the abutments — and the line is pressed down onto the intrados at the crown and flung out through the extrados at the foot. Let it fall too low, as an arch on yielding ground does of its own accord, and it rides over the extrados at the crown and sinks through the intrados at the haunch. One ring, and every state of the ground walks the line to one edge or the other.

The haunch is the flank that gives way, because it is the edge the arch reaches on its own. Left alone — on ground that yields even a little — an arch settles toward its minimum thrust, the least it can push and still stand, and the minimum-thrust line is precisely the one that sinks deepest onto the intrados at the haunches. That is why the crack, when it comes, opens on the soffit out on the flank and not at the keystone: gravity walks the line down to the haunch unless something holds it up. And the thing that holds it up is weight, laid on in exactly the right place — packed over the haunch, where its load lifts the sagging line back off the soffit and into the stone.

Four arches, standing easy to hinged

From a ring too thick to fail to a flank cracked open

  1. Standing easy. The ring is generous — thick, or well filled at the flanks — and holds a wide family of thrust lines. The load can take any of a hundred paths down and every one stays buried in the stone. This is the Roman arch, the railway viaduct: overbuilt on purpose, and nothing you can do at the surface troubles it. There is slack in every joint.
  2. Seated. The one line centred in the ring, threading the middle of the stone from crown to foot, as far from both faces as the geometry allows. This is the arch at rest with its haunches packed and its abutments firm — the state a builder aims for, where the same margin stands on both walls at once and neither is anywhere near touched.
  3. Sinking onto the haunch. The abutments have eased, or the ring is thin, and the minimum-thrust line has sagged down onto the intrados at the flanks. The stone still holds — the line is inside, barely — but the whole reserve is spent there on the haunch soffit, and a cart crossing, or a winter of frost in the fill, is all it would take. The crack that is coming already has its address.
  4. Hinged. The line has left the stone at the haunch: it wants to pass below the intrados, under the soffit, where there is no stone to carry it, so the joint opens on the belly and the ring turns on that edge like a door. A hinge at the crown, one at each haunch, one at each spring, and the ring is a chain of stiff blocks folding — a mechanism — and it comes down. Not crushed. Unthreaded.

Three who read an arch

The same ring, three trades

The mason

the ring

Cuts the voussoirs and sets the shape. Chooses how thick to make the ring and how steep to spring it, knowing a round arch pushes hard and low on the haunch and a pointed one carries its thrust steeper and gentler. Builds the ring true on its centering, then — before he strikes the timber and lets the stone take its own weight — packs the spandrels solid over the flanks, so the arch never once feels its haunch unloaded.

The engineer

the line

Never touches the stone; draws the line. Threads a thrust line down the ring and asks only whether it fits — not where the real one runs, only whether any can be made to. Measures the arch not in stresses but in how many times thicker the ring is than the thinnest that would still hold a line: the geometric factor of safety. If that number is comfortably over one, the arch is sound, and the stone is barely trying.

The inspector

the crack

Reads the failures that have already started. Knows a crack in an old arch is not damage but information — it is the arch telling you where a hinge has formed, and three hinges is a warning and four is a fall. Looks first at the haunches, a third of the way up, on the soffit — the underside — because that is where the line runs closest to the edge and where the stone will speak first. A hairline out on the flank is worth a hundred at the crown.

The bench

Draw the line. Keep it in the stone.

One arch, seen face-on: a ring of stone on two abutments, the spandrels filled over its flanks, and the line of thrust threaded down through it. Set the arch's rise — shallow and segmental, or a full half-round — the thickness of the ring, and how deep you pack the haunch fill, then choose which line to draw: the least thrust the arch will settle to on its own, the seated line centred in the stone, or the most the abutments can be squeezed to give. The page threads the line, works out the horizontal thrust on the foot, the thinnest ring that would still hold a line, and the geometric factor of safety — and lights where the line runs closest to the edge. It opens on a thin half-round with dry haunches: the least-thrust line has sagged through the intrados on the flank, and a hinge has opened at the haunch, on the soffit. Pack the haunches.

The line of thrust is the path the arch's own weight takes down to the ground; the arch stands only while it can be kept inside the ring of stone. The ladder below the arch is the same line unrolled — its distance from the middle of the ring at each joint, crown at the left, springing at the right, with the back and belly of the stone as the two walls. Where the ladder touches a wall, a hinge opens: on the soffit at the haunch when the thrust runs low, and over the crown and out at the springing when it runs high. Thicken the ring or pack the haunch fill to pull the line home; raise the rise toward a half-round and the thrust falls low onto the flank, where the fill does the most good. Pack the haunches raises the fill to the depth that just seats the line; Reset returns to the thin, dry arch it opens on.
