# Haunch

**A masonry arch is dry logic — wedge stones, no mortar worth the name,
standing on nothing but each other's weight. It holds because the compression
running down through it traces a single path, the *line of thrust*, and that
line can be kept inside the ring of stone from the keystone to the ground.
Heyman's safe theorem is almost too simple: if *any* such line fits, the arch
stands. It need not be the real one — only exist. So an arch is not tested by
its stresses, which are trifling, but by its geometry: is the ring thick enough,
and shaped well enough, to thread a line down without poking through the back or
the belly of the stone? And when it is overloaded, the line does not snap at the
crown where a beam would break. It sags at the flanks — at the *haunches* — sinks
below the soffit, and the joint opens on the underside and turns like a hinge.
The crack, when it comes, is never at the keystone. It is a hand's breadth up
from the spring, out on the haunch.**

The word *haunch* is the flank of the arch, the quarter between the springing at
the foot and the crown at the top — the same word a carpenter uses for the
squared shoulder of a tenon. On the arch it is the place the load leans hardest,
and the place the arch fails first.

## The one shape

Draw the line of thrust — the funicular of the arch's own weight, a hanging
chain flipped into pure compression — and every consequence falls out of where
it runs:

- Left to itself, on ground that yields even a little, an arch takes its
  **least thrust**, and the least-thrust line rides over the *extrados* at the
  crown and **sinks onto the intrados at the haunches**. That is the crack's
  address: on the soffit, out on the flank. Push the ring thin enough and the
  line passes clean below the stone there — a hinge on the underside, and with
  its mates at the crown and the springings, a five-hinge mechanism that folds.
- The remedy the old builders knew by eye: **pack the haunches**. Spandrel fill
  laid over the flanks adds weight exactly where it lifts the sagging line back
  off the soffit and into the stone. A bare half-round needs a ring near a ninth
  of its radius to stand; filled, it will stand on far less.

The page is one arch in elevation: a stone ring on two abutments, the spandrels
filled over the haunches, and the line of thrust threaded down through it. Set
the **rise** — shallow segmental to full half-round — the **thickness** of the
ring, and how deep you pack the **haunch fill**, then draw the **least thrust**
the arch settles to alone, the **seated** line centred in the stone, or the
**most** the abutments can be squeezed to give. It reads back the horizontal
thrust on the foot, the thinnest ring that would still hold a line, the
geometric factor of safety, and the flank where the line runs closest to the
edge. The numbers are the real ones: the line of thrust is the funicular of the
self-weight and the spandrel fill; the factor of safety is the ring thickness
over the minimum that contains a line, after Heyman — near 0.11·R for a bare
half-round, the figure the masons found without the arithmetic.
