Quantum Grain

A photograph's pixels, held as the amplitudes of a quantum state. You can't read an amplitude. Only measure it.

A block of pixels becomes a quantum circuit's amplitudes, the same method Moth's own quantum-audio package uses for sound. No operation reads a circuit's amplitudes back. You measure it, many times, and count outcomes. Few measurements, grainy result: literally what too little evidence looks like. The atlas engine runs the same idea on a real pipeline instead: the whole photo, sent to one of Moth's own Atlas engines, encoded, measured, and decoded on a real quantum processor, decoherence included.

Where the grain comes from

An amplitude is not a number you can read

An n-pixel block takes log₂(n) qubits: 64 pixels, 6 qubits, because every combination of qubit states indexes one amplitude. That's the whole "encode" step: pixel values become amplitudes, normalised so their squares sum to one.

Only one operation exists on a quantum state: measure it. One measurement returns one bitstring, with probability equal to the square of its amplitude, and destroys the state. You measure the same circuit repeatedly, count outcomes, and treat the frequency as a probability estimate.

Statistical estimates carry statistical noise. At S shots, each bucket's count has a relative uncertainty around 1⁄√S, the same law behind shot noise in a photon count or a Geiger counter. A few hundred shots: visible grain. Tens of thousands: the grain nearly disappears.

The one relation, read three ways

Shots buy fidelity; qubits spend it

  • Fidelity rises with shots, but only as a square root. Halving the noise takes four times the shots, not two. On a real QPU, shots are queued time on a scarce device.
  • Bigger blocks spend the same shot budget on exponentially more amplitudes. An 8×8 block (6 qubits) and a 16×16 block (8 qubits) look close, but the second has four times as many amplitude buckets sharing the same S shots. Coarser detail costs less per pixel; finer detail gets fewer shots per amplitude.
  • A real device adds a floor no shot count erases. A simulator's only error is shot noise, which vanishes as S → ∞. A real QPU also decoheres, its gates aren't perfect, its readout misfires. That floor sits under the shot noise, untouched by more shots, and no simulator can fake it.

Every other bench in this series idealises frictionless bearings; its numbers are only as accurate as that idealisation. This one can't, because the texture only exists if a real device measured something. That's what the Atlas engine is for.

Four states, from a clean frame to a useless one

Around the one loop: encode, measure, decode

  1. Clean. Shots to spare. Reconstruction close to the original; grain present in principle, invisible in practice.
  2. Grainy. Fewer shots, or a bigger block. Structure and colour hold; fine detail turns into a visible, even texture, exactly what 1⁄√S predicts.
  3. Noisy. Shots stretched over too many amplitudes. Each block drifts from its neighbours: still recognisable, visibly patchwork.
  4. Collapsed. A handful of shots, or real decoherence on top. Barely better than a guess: blocky, discoloured, sometimes unrecognisable. Too few measurements, quantum or not.

Who reads it, and for what

The same grain, three ways

The compressionist

wants the least noise per shot

Reads PSNR against shots and block size the way a codec engineer reads bitrate against distortion: a lossy quantum channel, shots standing in for bits.

The hardware skeptic

wants proof this needed a QPU

Checks the job id and machine Atlas reports, then runs the same photo twice. A simulator repeats itself exactly; real hardware drifts with its calibration. No simulator can fake that drift.

The media artist

wants a texture, not an error

Dials the shot count down on purpose, the way photographers once chose faster, grainier film for its look.

The discipline in the word, and the one it breaks

Grain, honestly measured

Every other page in this series runs entirely in your browser: one file, one script, no build, no network. This one breaks that on purpose. A real measurement's cost, in shots and fidelity, can't be simulated honestly without faking data or running an actual quantum computer. So this bench keeps a small server for the encode → measure → decode loop: this page's own amplitude-encoding implementation, modelled on Moth's open quantum-audio package, for simulator; one of Atlas's own engines, tessa-image-v1, for atlas. Nothing on screen is faked to look quantum, and the reading panel always says which engine produced it.

Run it: pip install -r requirements.txt && python server.py, then open http://localhost:5057. Full source, including the Atlas adapter, is in this folder.
