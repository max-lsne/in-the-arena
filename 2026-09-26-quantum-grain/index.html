grain · noun · the fine, granular texture a picture has when it's rebuilt from too little evidence, here, too few measurements of a quantum state

Quantum Grain

A photograph's pixels, held as the amplitudes of a quantum state. An amplitude is a thing you can never read. You can only measure it.

Take a block of pixels, flatten it into a signal, and prepare a quantum circuit whose amplitudes are exactly those pixel values. It's the same method Moth's own quantum-audio package uses to put sound on a circuit, applied here to sight instead of hearing. There is no operation that returns a circuit's amplitudes directly. You can only measure it, many times, and count how often each outcome came up. From that histogram you reconstruct the pixels, approximately, because a finite number of shots is a finite amount of data. Few shots and the picture comes back grainy. That's literally what a reconstruction looks like when it's based on too few measurements. Run the same circuit on a real quantum processor through Moth's Atlas API instead of a simulator and the result also contains something a simulator cannot produce. The device's own decoherence adds to the shot noise.

Where the grain comes from

An amplitude is not a number you can read

Encoding a block of n pixels takes log₂(n) qubits: an 8×8 block is 64 pixels and needs only 6 qubits, because a quantum state's amplitudes are indexed by every combination its qubits can take at once. Preparing that state is the whole of the "encode" step. After it, the pixel values live in the circuit as amplitudes, normalised so their squares sum to one, the way any quantum state must be.

There is only one operation you can perform on a quantum state: measure it. One measurement returns one bitstring, chosen with probability equal to the square of its amplitude, and destroys the state in the process. You don't read the amplitudes back directly. You measure the same circuit over and over, count how often each bitstring came up, and treat that frequency as an estimate of the probability it came from.

That's a statistical estimate, and statistical estimates have noise. With S shots, the count in any one bucket has a relative uncertainty on the order of 1⁄√S. It's the same square-root law that produces shot noise in a photon count or a Geiger counter. Decode the circuit after only a few hundred shots and every pixel you reconstruct shows that uncertainty visibly, as grain. Decode it after tens of thousands and the grain becomes nearly invisible, because there are finally enough measurements to determine the amplitude precisely.

The one relation, read three ways

Shots buy fidelity; qubits spend it

  • Fidelity rises with shots, but only as a square root. Halving the noise takes four times the shots, not two. Each further halving costs disproportionately more shots than the one before it. On a real QPU, shots are not free. They're queued time on a scarce device.
  • Bigger blocks spend the same shot budget on exponentially more amplitudes. Doubling a block's side doesn't double the qubits it needs. An 8×8 block (6 qubits) and a 16×16 block (8 qubits) look close, but the second has four times as many amplitude buckets sharing the same S shots, so each one is measured four times more thinly. Coarser detail costs less per pixel. Finer detail gets fewer shots per amplitude at the same budget.
  • A real device adds a floor no shot count erases. A simulator's only error is shot noise, and shot noise goes to zero as S → ∞. A real QPU's qubits decohere, its gates aren't perfect, and its readout misfires sometimes. That's a noise floor set by the hardware itself, sitting under the shot noise, not reduced by adding more shots. A simulator cannot reproduce that floor, because reproducing it would mean simulating a specific machine's specific imperfections rather than an idealised one.

Every other bench in this series idealises its bearings as frictionless; its numbers are only as accurate as that idealisation. This one can't do that. The whole point is a texture that only exists if a real device measured something, which is what the Atlas engine is for.

Four states, from a clean frame to a useless one

Around the one loop: encode, measure, decode

  1. Clean. Shots to spare against the block size, thousands per qubit's worth of amplitude. The reconstruction is very close to the original; the grain is present in principle but too small to see.
  2. Grainy. Shots thinned by a larger block or a smaller budget. Structure and colour survive; fine detail is replaced by a visible, even texture, exactly what 1⁄√S predicts at this shot count.
  3. Noisy. Shots stretched thin over too many amplitudes. Each block's reconstruction differs visibly from its neighbours; the picture is still recognisably itself but every patch was reconstructed independently, and it shows.
  4. Collapsed. A handful of shots per block, or, on Atlas, a real device's decoherence added to that. The estimate is barely better than a guess, and it shows in blocky, discoloured, sometimes unrecognisable results. Not a bug: this is what too few measurements produce, whether the measurement is quantum or not.

Who reads it, and for what

The same grain, three ways

The compressionist

wants the least noise per shot

Reads PSNR against shots and block size the way a codec engineer reads bitrate against distortion. This is a lossy quantum channel, and shots play the same role bits do in a codec.

The hardware skeptic

wants proof this needed a QPU

Compares the Atlas patch against the simulator directly, block for block. If the two ever produced identical results, Atlas wouldn't be using real hardware at all. They don't. The patch shows patterns specific to that device, which no simulator can reproduce.

The media artist

wants a texture, not an error

Sets the shot count deliberately for more grain, not less, the way a photographer once chose a faster, grainier film for its look rather than in spite of it.

The bench

Encode a photo, measure it, and look at what came back

Drop in a photo or use the sample. Pick a block size, how many pixels share one circuit, and so how many qubits it takes. Pick a shot budget, how many times that circuit gets measured. Pick an engine: simulator runs instantly and locally with no queue; atlas sends the same circuits to a real quantum processor through Moth's API. Because real QPU time is queued and rate-limited, not free, it processes a bounded patch of the frame rather than all of it, leaving the rest of the frame shown exactly as uploaded, never simulated in its place.

The discipline in the word, and the one it breaks

Grain, honestly measured

Every other page in this series idealises its mechanism and runs entirely in your browser: one HTML file, one stylesheet, one script, no build, no account, no network once it has loaded. This one breaks that on purpose. Its whole subject is what a real measurement costs, in shots, in fidelity, and on Atlas, in a real device's own imperfection. There is no way to simulate that honestly without either presenting false data or actually running it on a quantum computer. So this bench keeps a small local server whose only purpose is the encode → measure → decode loop, using Moth's open quantum-audio package for the quantum-signal-processing half and Atlas for the real-device half. Nothing about the picture on screen is faked to look quantum. It either came from a simulator's shot noise or a real QPU's shot noise plus decoherence, and the reading panel always says which.

Run it: pip install -r requirements.txt && python server.py, then open http://localhost:5057. Full source, including the Atlas adapter, is in this folder.
