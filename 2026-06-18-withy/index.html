What the moor throws up, given a year and a soak

You cannot weave a green rod.
A withy has to be given its winter.

A withy is a willow rod the wet moor throws up in a single year, straight as a wand off a pollarded stool. You cut it in the dead of winter, when the sap is down and the leaf is gone, tie it into bolts, and sink them in the soak-pit for three weeks of tea-brown water until the rod bends round your wrist without a crack in it. Only then will it weave. A basket made of green, hurried withy splits at the first bend. One made of seasoned rod carries a hundredweight of apples for forty years.

Most of what you make
cracks at the first bend.

Most work is green and forced. It is cut in the morning and woven in the afternoon, with the sap still up in it and no soak behind it, and it splits the first time you ask it to take a real bend. The quick basket, the rushed draft, the habit kept for nine loud days — green rod, all of it, and the moor takes it back by Tuesday.

A withy is the other thing. A withy is given its year on the wet moor, its cut in the dead of winter when the sap is down, its three weeks under the tea-brown water of the soak. By the time it goes on the bench it bends round your wrist without a sound, and the weave holds a hundredweight for forty years. You cannot hurry a withy. That is the whole of the thing. You give it the winter and the soak, and then it will weave.

How it works

  1. 01

    Cut in the dead of winter

    Cut when the sap is down and the leaf long gone — December to March, the moor white with rime. A rod cut in the green of summer is full of water and will never season true. The corner of the year that nobody wants. The cold hour at the same desk. Cut then, and let the cold do half the work.

  2. 02

    Tie the bolt, sink the soak

    Bind the rods into a bolt, sort them to length, and sink them in the soak-pit. Three weeks under the dark water and not a day less. There is nothing to watch and nothing to hurry. The rod is taking the water it needs to bend. A page reread, a scale walked slow, the draft left in the drawer to settle.

  3. 03

    Weave when it bends without cracking

    Take a rod from the soak and bend it round your wrist. If it cracks it wants another week; if it bends silent it is ready. Now, and only now, weave. The randing and the slewing and the upsett — the work that was impossible in the green is easy in the seasoned. The hands know. The rod knows.

  4. 04

    The holt throws new rods next winter

    Cut a willow to the stool and it does not die — it throws a fan of new rods the next spring, straighter than the last, and goes on doing it for eighty winters. The work is held by a standing holt, not a single basket. You cut, and it gives back, and you cut again, and the bed your grandfather planted is still throwing rods.

The four levels

  • Rod. A single year's willow rod, just cut, the sap still in it — the first half-page, the empty notebook with one line, the morning you began. Green; it will crack if you force the bend today.
  • Bolt. A tied bundle, sorted to length, weeks in the soak — the first real gathering of work, supple now and ready to go in. Real, but not yet woven into anything that holds.
  • Basket. A finished weave — a creel, a hurdle, an eel-trap that holds. A body of work woven tight enough to carry a hundredweight, by hands that gave every rod its soak.
  • Holt. The osier holt itself, the standing ground that throws new rods every winter for a lifetime. A lifework, a craft, a watershed — the bed your grandfather planted and you still cut.

Who cuts the same bed

The basket-maker at the same bench

Fifty winters at the same bench on the Levels, the copper steaming, the brake by the door. The market creel woven every autumn since 1976 to the same pattern her mother used. The young hand's first green rod went in the loft to season this January. It is not ready. It will be.

The hurdle-maker in the wet coppice

Forty winters laying the same brown-withy holt below the rhyne. A sheep hurdle takes sixty rods and a long afternoon and stands in the field for fifteen years. He cuts to the stool, and the stool throws more, and he has never once had to plant.

The eel-fisher mending the putcher

The withy putcher rewoven every close season since 1979, sunk in the same reach of the river each spring. The water rots a basket in a few years; the mending never stops. The trap is never finished and never the same trap, and the eels come back to it anyway.

The translator at the same desk

Two pages of the slow Italian every winter morning since 2012. A holt now in the corner of the desk, fifteen winters cut, throwing straighter rods each year. Nothing woven from it cracks at the bend any more. The hands gave it the soak. The bed keeps giving back.
