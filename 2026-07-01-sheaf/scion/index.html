What makes the tree you actually wanted

Sow a thousand pips,
reap a thousand wildings.

A scion is a cutting of a tree you already know and want — this apple, this exact plum — joined to a strong rootstock so it grows as itself. It is the only way to keep the good tree: a pip from the finest apple grows into a wilding you have never tasted, sour as often as sweet. So you do not sow the whole field and hope. You take a few scions of the tree you chose, cut them clean to the stock, bind and wax the union, and mind it through the spring until it knits and grows away. The whole idea is deliberate and patient: a few good grafts minded till they take, against a field of chance seedlings left to come up as they will.

A small, capped bench. No sign-up. It just wants minding.

How it works

Stop sowing pips. Graft a few of the tree you chose.

01

Take it, and cut it to the stock

A thing you mean to grow true starts as a cut scion — a length of the tree you chose, sliced clean and matched to the rootstock so the two green rims meet.

02

Bind it, and read its condition

Grafting is minding a wound. You move it along — cut, bound, taking, knit — and you read how it fares: dormant, swelling, leafing, bearing. How joined it is and how alive it is are not the same thing.

03

Mind it till it knits

A graft lives or dies in its first spring. Set more than you can mind and the wax cracks, the union dries, and the scion dies back unnoticed. A few, watched closely, knit and grow away as the tree you meant to have.

The stock

A grafter sets only as many unions as he can walk in a morning.

A graft is a wound kept alive on purpose. For the weeks it takes to knit, it wants watching — the binding eased before it strangles, the wax mended where it cracks, the rootstock's own shoots rubbed off so they do not rob the scion. That is close, daily work, and a grafter can only walk so many in a morning. Set the whole nursery at once and the far rows go unwalked, the unions dry, and you lose the lot — good scions wasted on stock you could not tend. The cure is never more grafts. It is fewer, set where you will pass them, and walked every day.

So the bench has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point. Scion holds six unions. When the bench is full the next scion waits in the cool, wrapped and dormant, until one has knitted and grown away on its own. A nursery set beyond what one can walk would lose every union in it.

Who keeps one

For people who sowed more than they can walk.

The scatter-sower

A hundred hopeful pips flung wide, most coming up sour or not at all. Scion asks which six trees you actually chose, and grafts those onto good stock.

The slow grower

A craft, a discipline, a life — things that only come true when a known-good cutting is joined to strong roots and minded till it takes. Scion is built for the long knitting.

The one who lost a graft

You set a good scion, then stopped walking it, and it dried and died back before you noticed. This is for setting a few and passing them every single day.
