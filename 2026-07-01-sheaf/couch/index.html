What brings barley to sweet malt

Steep the whole harvest,
and the floor moulds.

On the malting floor a couch is a bed of steeped barley, heaped up to germinate. Wet the whole crop at once and the floor heats, the grain mats, the rootlets tangle, and the lot sours before the week is out. But steep a few couches, spread them thin, and turn them by hand every few hours — and the barley chits even, the starch mellows, and it comes off the kiln sweet. The whole idea is patient and hands-on: a few things brought to readiness by turning them often, against a whole harvest steeped at once and left to heat.

How it works

Stop steeping everything. Bring a few beds to malt.

01

Steep it, and couch it

A thing you mean to bring to readiness starts steeped — just wetted, no life showing yet. You heap it into a couch on the floor and let the first rootlet break.

02

Turn it, and read its condition

Malting is turning. You move it across the floor — steeped, couched, floored, kilned — and you read how it fares: slack, chitting, working, mellow. Progress and condition are not the same thing.

03

Watch the floor's heat

The floor holds only so much unturned. Heap too many couches, or leave them working too long, and it heats. One line reads the whole floor — cool and sweet when you keep it turned, matting when you don't.

The floor

A malting floor holds what it can turn, and no more.

The floor is not a granary. Grain sits in a granary cold and does nothing; on the floor it is alive, breathing, throwing heat, and it has to be turned or it mats and moulds. A maltster can only turn so much barley by hand in a shift. Push past that and the far couches go unturned, heat in the middle, and the whole floor takes the taint. The cure is never more barley. It is fewer couches, spread thinner, turned oftener.

So the floor has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point. Couch holds six. When the floor is full the next crop stays in the steep until a couch comes off to the kiln. A floor that tried to work the whole harvest would sour every grain of it.

Who keeps one

For people who steeped more than they can turn.

The over-steeped

Twenty things wetted at once, all heating in the middle, none of them turned. Couch asks which six are on the floor for real, and holds the rest in the steep.

The slow maker

A body of work that only comes sweet if it is turned patiently over weeks — a book, a craft, a practice. Couch is built for the long, hands-on malt.

The one who got a taint

You left a good thing working too long, untouched, and it soured. This is for going back to a turned few, and catching the heat before it mats.
