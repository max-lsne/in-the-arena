# Pleach

**A fence rots. A laid hedge is alive in twenty years — if you lay a few stems
and don't nail up the lot.**

To *pleach* is to half-cut a living stem near its foot, bend it down along the
line, and stake or weave it so it keeps growing sideways instead of up. It is
how a hedge is made stock-proof: not by planting a wall of thorn, but by taking
the stems already there, laying a chosen few, and letting them knit. A
post-and-rail fence does the same job in an afternoon — and it is dead the
moment the last nail goes in. It rots at the ground, it sags, and in a decade it
is a heap of grey rails. A laid hedge, tended, is thicker every year and lasts a
century. The strength was never in doing it fast. It was in laying a few living
stems and binding them into one line.

Pleach is a small page that holds that shape. It is a board for the few things
you are laying into something lasting — the ones you are actually cutting,
bending, and binding — against the many you nailed up quick to look finished,
and which are already going soft at the foot.

## The two axes

A stem is read two ways at once, and they are not the same reading.

**Stage** — how far along the line it has been worked:

- **Standing** — a whippy upright stem, growing any way it likes.
- **Cut** — half-cut low at the pleach, ready to bend along the line.
- **Laid** — bent down and staked into the line, still fed by its root.
- **Bound** — woven and tied along the top, holding the whole line true.

**Condition** — how well it has taken:

- **Whippy** — loose and upright, no side growth, nothing to hold.
- **Set** — grown a leader and a live bud; it has caught.
- **Knitting** — throwing side shoots into its neighbours.
- **Stockproof** — dense and woven; a bullock could not push through.

A stem can be bound into the line and still whippy, or knitting hard but never
yet cut. Minding one axis tells you nothing about the other.

## The line holds six

The board caps at six stems, and the cap is the whole point. A hedge-layer has
only so many short winter days to cut, bend, stake, and bind before the sap
rises. Take on too much of the boundary at once and half of it is cut but never
laid — and a half-laid hedge is worse than none, all gaps and dying stumps. When
the line is full, the next stem stays standing until one is bound and knitting
on its own.

## The gaps

Two of the three pages in this folder carry a live gauge. Pleach's reads the
**gaps in the line** — how much of what you are minding is still standing loose
rather than laid and knitting. Keep it laid and bound and the line runs solid.
Leave stems standing and it opens into a gap a bullock could take. It is held
still when the browser asks for reduced motion.

## Running it

No build step. Open `index.html` in a browser.

- **Set in** a stem with the field, or press <kbd>N</kbd>.
- **Click the stem-mark** to work it — advance how well it has taken.
- **The row of stages** moves it along the line; the **&times;** takes it out.
- **Rename the hedge** by clicking its name.
- Everything is kept in your browser's `localStorage`. Nothing leaves the
  machine. Double-click the count to clear the line back to the example.
