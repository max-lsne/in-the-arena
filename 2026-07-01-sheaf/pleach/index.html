What makes a barrier that lives

A fence rots.
A laid hedge lives.

To pleach is to half-cut a living stem near its foot, bend it down along the line, and peg or weave it so it keeps growing. Do that to a few chosen stems and, in a season or two, they knit into a hedge a bullock cannot push through — alive, thickening, stock-proof for a hundred years. The quick way is a post-and-rail fence: nailed up in a day, and dead the moment it is done. It rots, it sags, and in a decade it is a heap. The whole idea is slow and living: a few stems laid right outlast everything nailed up in a hurry.

How it works

Stop nailing up rails. Lay a few living stems.

01

Choose it, and cut it

A thing you mean to make lasting starts standing — a whippy upright stem, growing any way it likes. You choose it and make the cut: half through, low, so it will bend without breaking.

02

Lay it, and read its condition

Laying is the craft. You move it along the line — standing, cut, laid, bound — and you read how it takes: whippy, set, knitting, stock-proof. Where it is on the line and how well it has taken are not the same thing.

03

Watch the line for gaps

A hedge is only as good as its worst gap. Leave too many stems standing, or laid but not knitting, and the line gaps. One line reads the whole hedge — solid when it is laid and bound, open where it is not.

The line

A hedge-layer works the length he can bind before the sap drops.

Laying is winter work, done in the cold months while the stems are bare and the sap is down. A layer has only so many short days to cut, bend, stake, and bind a length before spring. Take on too much of the field boundary at once and half of it gets cut but never laid, or laid but never bound — and a half-laid hedge is worse than none, all gaps and dying stumps. The cure is never a longer line. It is a shorter one, laid and bound properly before you start the next.

So the board has a hard ceiling, and the ceiling is the point. Pleach holds six stems. When the line is full the next stem stays standing until one is bound and knitting on its own. A layer who cut the whole field in a day would leave the whole field to die back.

Who keeps one

For people who nailed up more than they can keep standing.

The quick-fencer

A dozen things nailed up fast to look finished, all quietly rotting. Pleach asks which six are worth laying into something living, and lets the rails go.

The slow grower

A relationship, a body, a craft, a place — things that only become stock-proof by being laid patiently over years. Pleach is built for the long knitting.

The one with the gap

You laid a good line once and let a stretch of it go — and now there is a gap a bullock could take. This is for going back and laying the gap shut.
