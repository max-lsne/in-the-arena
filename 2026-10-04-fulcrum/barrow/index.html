barrow · noun · /ˈbarəʊ/ — a one-wheeled carrier with handles, worked as a lever against its own wheel; the load rides between the wheel and the hands, so the wheel carries most of the weight and the hands only the rest

Barrow

Put the weight where the wheel can hold it, and your hands lift only what's left.

A wheelbarrow is the second class of lever, and the one that can never work against you: the load rides between the fulcrum — the wheel — and your hands, so the effort arm is always longer than the load arm, and the advantage is never less than one. You do not choose whether it helps; it always helps. What you choose is where the load sits. Pile it back by the handles and your hands bear nearly all of it and cannot lift. Pile it forward over the wheel and your hands bear almost nothing — but a barrow with no weight on the handles has nothing to steer by, and tips on its nose. The whole skill of a barrow is the one thing the class leaves you to get wrong.

Where the advantage comes from

The load between the wheel and the hands

Stand a loaded barrow on its wheel and leg and the ground holds all of it. Lift the handles and you take over the leg's job — but not the wheel's. The weight is now shared between two supports, the wheel at the front and your hands at the back, and it shares in proportion to where the load sits between them: close to the wheel, the wheel takes the lion's share; close to the hands, you do. Because the load is always somewhere between the two, your hands never carry the whole of it. That is the gift of the second class — the fulcrum is always on the far side of the load from you, so some of every load is carried for free.

A first-class lever can help or hinder, depending where the fulcrum goes. A second-class lever can only help. The wheel is always on the load's far side, so some of the weight is always carried for you.

The same arrangement is a nutcracker, a bottle opener, a pair of nail clippers, a wheel-and-axle brake lever — anywhere the load is pinned between a hinge and a hand, and the hand wins. The cost is the one every lever charges: your hands move further than the load. You lift the handles a foot to raise the load a few inches, because the advantage you are given in force is paid back, exactly, in distance.

The one relation, read three ways

Arm ratio, share, travel

Call the distance from the wheel back to your hands the effort arm — fixed, the length of the barrow — and the distance from the wheel out to the load the load arm, which is where you choose to pile it. Take the load's weight W. Idealise the barrow as rigid and weightless and the wheel as frictionless:

  • The advantage is the arm ratio, and never below one. MA = effort arm ⁄ load arm. Because the load sits between the wheel and the hands, the load arm is always the shorter, so MA ≥ 1 — the only class for which that is guaranteed.
  • Your hands bear the load divided by it. The hands carry W ⁄ MA; the wheel carries the rest. Slide the load toward the wheel and MA climbs and your share falls toward nothing; slide it back to the handles and MA → 1 and you carry all of it.
  • Travel is divided by it, too. Lift the handles a distance d and the load rises only d ⁄ MA. Work is conserved — (W ⁄ MA) · d = W · (d ⁄ MA) — the force you save is the distance you spend, to the last inch.

Those three are one fact, and they hand you a trap the first class never does. There is no placement that fails to help — but there are two that fail the barrow. Load it back and your share rises until you cannot lift; load it hard forward and your share falls until the handles float and there is nothing to steer or balance by, and the barrow pitches on its nose. The advantage is free; the control is not.

You cannot set a barrow so the lever works against you. You can only set it so your hands have too much to lift, or too little to steer. The right place is between — the wheel bearing the weight, the hands keeping the reins.

Five placements, from a nose-dive to a dead lift

Around the one load

  1. Tips. The load crammed right over the wheel. Your hands bear almost nothing — which sounds like success, but a barrow with no weight on the handles has no grip on its own balance: it pitches forward, dumps its nose, and steers like a shopping trolley with a bad caster.
  2. Light. The load well forward. The hands bear a token share — easy to hold, but twitchy: little margin against a bump tipping it, and the wheel doing nearly all the work while you merely trail it.
  3. Fair. The load set so the wheel takes the weight and the hands keep enough to lift and guide — a comfortable share, well under what the hands can bear, well over nothing. The whole aim: carried mostly for free, still yours to steer.
  4. Loaded. The load back toward the handles. It lifts, but the hands bear most of the weight and tire fast. The advantage is slipping toward one; you are nearly carrying it yourself, with a wheel along for the ride.
  5. Dead. The load at the handles. The load arm has grown to the effort arm, MA → 1, and the hands bear the whole weight — past what they can raise. The barrow gives nothing; it may as well be a crate.

Who loads it, and for what

The same barrow, three wants

The bricklayer

wants the heaviest load moved

Carries bricks and wet mortar — loads past any back. Piles them well forward over the wheel so the hands bear a liftable share, and keeps just enough weight on the handles to hold a line across the site. Takes the free carry the wheel offers and no more lift than needed.

The gardener

wants to tip it where it goes

Carries light, bulky loads — leaves, cuttings — and must tip them out at the end. Loads a touch back, keeping weight on the handles, so the barrow balances and steers between beds and goes over cleanly when the handles lift, instead of nosing down early.

The mason

wants to weigh as he lifts

Feels the share on the handles as a reading of the load and its place: too light and he's crowded the wheel and lost the steering; too heavy and he's loaded it back and will tire. Sets the load by the weight in his hands — the arm ratio, felt rather than measured.

The discipline in the word

A weight carried mostly for free

A barrow is the kind lever — it asks nothing of your judgement about whether to help, only about how to load it. Give it a weight and it will always hand most of it to the wheel; your hands take only the share the placement leaves them, and the more of the load you slide over the wheel the less you carry — down to almost nothing. But almost nothing is its own failure: a barrow you cannot feel is a barrow you cannot steer, and it will put your load on the ground at your feet. The craft is to load it so the wheel bears the weight and your hands keep the reins — a fair share, not the least. The advantage is a gift of the geometry; the balance is the one thing left to you. Load it back and it's dead; load it forward and it tips; and the right place keeps both the carry and the control.
