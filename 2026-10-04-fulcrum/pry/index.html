pry · verb · /prʌɪ/ — to raise or part a thing with a lever, by putting a bar across a fixed point and bearing down on the far end; the oldest machine there is, a stick and a stone

Pry

A bar, a stone to rest it on, and a slab that will not move — until you put the stone in the right place.

A pry bar is the first class of lever, and the only one that turns a force around: the fulcrum sits between your hands and the load, so when you push the long end down, the short end lifts the weight up. All of its advantage is geometry — the arm ratio, how far your hands are from the fulcrum against how far the load is. Slide the fulcrum toward the slab and the ratio climbs: you can lift far more than you weigh, but the slab rises only a hair for a long push. Slide it toward your hands and the slab moves freely — but now you cannot shift it at all. The whole skill of prying is where you set the stone.

Where the advantage comes from

A rigid bar turns about one point

Lay a bar across a fulcrum and it can do only one thing: turn about that point. Push one end down through some small arc and the other end sweeps up through the same angle — but the ends travel different distances, because the far end is further out. That difference in distance is the whole machine. The end that moves further is the end that is easier to move; the end that moves less is the end that moves with more force. A lever is a trade of one for the other, and nothing more.

A lever cannot make work. It can only spread a force over a longer distance, or gather it into a shorter one. What you gain in force you pay for, exactly, in travel.

Because the fulcrum is between the hands and the load, the two ends go opposite ways — your hands down, the load up. That reversal is the signature of the first class, and it is why a pry bar is the reach for anything pinned: a crowbar under a nail, a tyre iron under a bead, an oar against a thole. The same bar, with the fulcrum moved, is a balance, a pair of scissors, a seesaw. Move the one point and you choose the trade.

The one relation, read three ways

Arm ratio, force, travel

Call the distance from the fulcrum out to your hands the effort arm, and the distance from the fulcrum to the load the load arm. Take the effort you can bear down, E, and the weight of the slab, W. Idealise the bar as rigid and weightless and the fulcrum as frictionless, and the whole of it is one ratio read three ways:

  • The advantage is the arm ratio. MA = effort arm ⁄ load arm. Set the fulcrum so your hands are three times as far out as the load and you push with a third of the weight — the geometry multiplies your force by three, and asks nothing else.
  • Force is multiplied by it. The force delivered to the load is E · MA. It lifts when that reaches the weight: E · MA ≥ W. So the least advantage that will raise the slab is fixed by the load you have and the effort you can give — MA = W ⁄ E — and everything above that is margin.
  • Travel is divided by it. The same ratio that multiplies force divides distance: the load rises d ⁄ MA for a hand-push of d. Work is conserved — E · d = W · (d ⁄ MA) — so a huge advantage buys a tiny lift, and you must re-set and pry again, and again.

Those three are one fact. The arm ratio you set is the force you gain and the travel you lose, in the same breath — there is no dial for force alone. So the right place for the fulcrum is not as close to the load as it will go. It is the furthest point from the load that still lifts the slab: the least advantage that does the job, bought back as the longest stroke.

Too far from the load and the bar will not raise it. Too near and it raises it a hair. The art is the placement between, where it heaves — lifts, and lifts a useful amount.

Four placements, from a dead slab to a nibble

Around the one lift

  1. Strains. The fulcrum set far from the slab, out toward your hands. The load arm is long, the advantage below W ⁄ E, and the force the bar delivers never reaches the weight. You bear down with all you have and the slab does not stir — the geometry, not your strength, was short.
  2. Heaves. The fulcrum placed so the advantage just clears the weight, with a little margin. The slab lifts — and lifts through the longest stroke this load allows, because you spent no more advantage than you had to. The whole aim: it moves, and it moves usefully.
  3. Nibbles. The fulcrum crowded right up to the slab. The advantage is enormous and the slab rises easily — but only a sliver, because the travel was divided away. You lift, re-set the bar, lift, re-set: real work, in maddening crumbs.
  4. Folds. The fulcrum pushed past the load, or the load so light the bar just tips. The arrangement is no longer a first-class lift at all — the ends no longer oppose, and the bar skids or flips rather than prying. The one point left its place between.

Who sets the stone, and for what

The same bar, three wants

The mover

wants it to shift at all

Faced with a block past any lift, slides the fulcrum close to it until the bar bites and the block stirs. Takes the huge advantage and the tiny stroke gladly — a crumb of motion beats a dead weight — and resets the bar as many times as the job needs.

The fitter

wants a clean stroke

Wants the load up and over in one motion — a bead onto a rim, a crate onto a sill. Sets the fulcrum as far out as will still lift, trading surplus force for the longest sweep, so the thing clears in a single heave instead of a dozen.

The weigher

wants the ratio read

Does not care to lift at all, but to balance: a known weight far out against an unknown near in, read off where the bar hangs level. Same first-class bar, same arm ratio — used not to multiply a force but to measure one. E · a = W · b.

The bench

Set the slab against the fulcrum

A bar across a fulcrum, a heavy slab on the short arm, your heave on the long one. Set how much the slab weighs and where the fulcrum sits. Watch the arm ratio set the force the bar delivers and the stroke the slab lifts through: too far from the slab and the bar strains and will not raise it; too near and it lifts a hair and asks to be re-set. The discipline in the word

To raise a thing by where you stand on it

To pry is not to be strong. It is to know that a weight past any lift becomes liftable the moment you put the fulcrum in the right place — and that the place is a choice, not a given. Crowd it to the load and you trade away your stroke for brute force; stand it off and you trade force for reach, until there is too little left to lift at all. The bar makes no work and keeps no secrets: whatever force it hands you, it takes back in distance, to the last foot-pound. The craft is reading the load against your own strength and setting the one point so the slab moves — and moves as far as it can for the heave you have. Too far and it strains; too near and it nibbles; and the best is rarely the most.
