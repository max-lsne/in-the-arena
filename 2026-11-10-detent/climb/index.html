Climb

A detent costs nothing to rest in — and a definite, repeatable amount to leave.

To move off a seat, the ball has to be driven up and over the crest. All the way up the flank the spring pushes back, and that pushback is a torque that starts at zero in the seat, rises to a peak partway up, and falls back to zero right at the crest. That peak is the breakaway torque — the firmness of the click, the thing your fingers read as a good knob or a stiff one. Turn with any less than it, and the wheel settles somewhere on the flank and holds: there is a resting place for your push. Turn with more, and there is no resting place short of the crest — so the ball runs over and clicks into the next seat. Raise the torque and feel for the edge.

What the peak says

Why a click has a size

  • The resistance is the spring force times the flank slope: τ(φ) = (F₀ + k·x)·dx/dφ. Zero at the seat, zero again at the crest, and a peak in between — that peak is the breakaway.
  • Below breakaway there is always an angle on the rising flank where resistance equals your push, and it is stable: lean harder into it and the resistance climbs to meet you. So the wheel holds, off-seat, wherever your torque lands on the flank.
  • At breakaway that meeting point reaches the top of the flank's steepness. One hair more and there is no equilibrium short of the crest — the ball runs over and drops into the next seat. That sudden give is the click.
  • The peak rises with depth, with preload and stiffness, and with flank steepness — and a finer pitch (more notches) steepens the flanks, so a many-notched dial clicks crisper and harder for the same depth.
  • Drive well past breakaway and the wheel never finds a seat to rest in: it ratchets, clicking through notch after notch for as long as you overpush — a knob spun too hard, a torque-limiter letting go.

The seat holds for nothing. The crest charges a toll. A detent is a rest with a price on the exit — and the price is the click you feel.
