seat · noun · /siːt/ — the shaped recess a part rests in and is located by; from Old Norse sæti, a place to sit — and so, the cut notch that gives a spring-pressed ball one place to settle, and holds it there against nothing but its own shape

Seat

A spring presses a ball into a notch, and the bottom of the notch is the one place it wants to be.

A detent is a potential well cut on purpose. A spring holds a ball against a wheel whose rim has a notch pressed into it. The bottom of the notch is where the spring is stretched out longest and pushing least — the lowest energy the ball can have — so left alone, the ball sits there and the wheel does not move: no catch, no latch, just a shape the ball has rolled to the bottom of. Nudge the wheel a little and the ball has to ride up the side of the notch, squeezing the spring, which pushes straight back down the slope. That push is a restoring torque — zero at the very bottom, growing the further you climb — and it is the whole of why a detent holds a position and snaps back to it. Drag the wheel off its seat and let go.

The bench

One notch, one ball, one place to rest

Drag the wheel to roll the ball up out of its seat, then let go — it falls back to the bottom, overshoots, and settles, because near the bottom the notch is almost a perfect bowl and the ball returns like a weight on a spring. The lower panel is that bowl drawn as energy against angle: the ball is the marble in it. Steepen the sides — deeper notch, closer spacing, more preload — and the same nudge is thrown back harder.

What the well says

Why a shape can hold a position

  • The ball rests where its energy is least, and the least is the bottom of the notch, where the spring is longest. Nothing clamps it there — it simply has nowhere lower to go.
  • The restoring torque is the spring force times the slope of the flank: τ = F · dx/dθ. At the very bottom the slope is zero, so the torque is zero — a detent holds a position while asking for no force to hold it.
  • Climb a little and the slope grows almost in step with the angle, so near the seat the well is a parabola and the ball behaves like a torsional spring: nudge it and it swings back, overshoots, and settles.
  • The seat's stiffness — how hard it throws a small nudge back — rises with preload, with depth, and with closeness of spacing: κ ≈ 2π²·F₀·d / P² near the bottom. Steeper sides, firmer seat.
  • The well has edges. Ride up past the crest — the top of the flank, halfway to the next notch — and the slope flips: now it pushes you on, into the next seat. That edge is the whole subject of climb.

A latch holds by getting in the way. A detent holds by being the bottom of a bowl — the ball is not trapped, it has merely arrived.
