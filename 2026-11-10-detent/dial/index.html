dial · noun · /ˈdaɪəl/ — a face or disc turned to set and read a value; from Latin dies, a day, by way of the sundial's round of hours — and so, a knob that reports its setting by the stop it rests in, counted off by feel

Dial

Ring the notches round a wheel, and the ball turns it into a knob that counts.

A single seat holds one position. Set a ring of them evenly round a wheel and the sprung ball makes a dial that moves in counted steps: each notch is the bottom of its own little well, so wherever you leave the knob it rolls to the nearest stop and rests there — it will not sit between. That is why a detented knob can be read by feel alone, with no scale and no eyes: a camera's exposure dial, a rotary switch, the indexing ring on a lens. Turn it and it clicks from stop to stop, landing on each the same. Add a little friction and a dead-band opens around every seat — a span within which the centring pull is too weak to beat the rub, so the knob holds anywhere inside it. That band is the slack you feel in a loose dial, and it sets how exactly the knob lands.

The bench

Turn it, and feel it count

Grab the knob and turn it. It clicks from stop to stop and settles on a seat — never between. The lower panel is the detent's torque drawn across several notches, the washboard the ball rides, with the friction band shaded: where the curve lies inside the band, near each seat, the knob can hold without snapping dead-centre — the dead-band. Raise the friction and the band widens; the dial stiffens and lands less exactly. Add notches and the stops come faster and crisper.

What the ring says

Why a knob can be read by feel

  • A ring of seats is one well tiled round the wheel: the same curve, repeating every step. Wherever you let go, the ball is on some flank, and every flank drains to the nearest seat — so the dial has no resting place between stops.
  • That is repeatability: the knob lands on the same angle every time, because the only places it can stop are the bottoms of the notches. You can count the stops in the dark and trust the number.
  • Friction opens a dead-band. Near a seat the centring torque is small — it fades to zero at the bottom — so within a span ±δ = ±τ_f / κ the rub exceeds the pull and the knob stays put, wherever in the band it was left.
  • So friction is a double edge: it stiffens the click, raising the breakaway the same amount each way, and it loosens the landing, widening the band the knob may stop anywhere within. A good dial has enough detent to swamp its own friction.
  • Count, depth and friction are the whole character of a dial — from a camera's crisp, close stops to the few stiff notches of a mode switch. Same ball, same spring; only the ring changes.

A plain knob can stop anywhere and means nothing by it. A detented dial can stop only somewhere in particular — and so, at last, the stop is the reading.
