rode · noun · /rəʊd/ — the anchor line, chain or rope, and the length of it you pay out

Rode

Pay the chain out long, and the anchor is pulled flat along the bottom while the sag soaks up every gust.

An anchor does not hold by weight. It holds by biting into the ground, and it can only bite while the pull on it stays flat — along the seabed, not up off it. What keeps the pull flat is the rode: the chain between the boat and the anchor, and above all how much of it you veer. Pay out plenty and it cannot go straight — its own weight drags the middle down into a deep curve, a catenary, that arrives at the anchor lying along the bottom. That curve is the slack, and the slack is the whole trick. When a gust throws its weight on the boat, the first thing that happens is not a jerk on the anchor but the curve straightening — lifting tonnes of chain off the bottom — and all that lifting is force the anchor never feels. The rode breathes the gust in and lets it out. Veer too little and there is no curve left to give: the rode comes up in a straight line, the pull tips upward, and the next surge levers the anchor clean out of the ground.

Where the hold comes from

The curve pulls the anchor flat

A heavy line hung between two points cannot make a straight line — gravity pulls its slack belly down into a catenary, and the more line you hang, the deeper the belly and the flatter the ends. At the anchor, "flat" is everything: the shank is designed to be dragged along the ground so the flukes dig, and it only digs while the rode leaves it horizontally. The scope — the ratio of rode length to water depth — is the dial that sets how flat that arrival is.

The rode meets the seabed flat only while enough of it hangs. The load it takes to lift the last link off the bottom rises with the square of the scope: a little more chain out, and a great deal more blow before the anchor feels any upward pull at all.

Two things set the reserve. The scope is the length: veer more and the catenary deepens, laying more chain flat on the bottom as a buffer, and steepening the load it takes to pull the whole thing bar-taut. The ground tackle is the weight: heavy chain sags harder for the same length, so it hangs a deeper curve and holds its flat arrival to a stronger blow than light rope would. Neither depends on the anchor being big. A modest anchor on a deep, heavy curve out-holds a heavy anchor on a short, straight one — because holding was never about the anchor's weight. It was about the angle the rode hands it.

The one catch

The give that holds you can also let you wander

Slack this useful is paid for in room. The same length that lays the pull flat also lets the boat range — it can lie anywhere on a circle whose radius is the rode reaching out along the ground, and swing across it as the wind backs and veers:

Too little scope and the rode snatches — it comes up straight, tips the pull upward, and levers the anchor out. Too much and it holds, but the boat sails a wide circle around it and fouls whatever shares the anchorage.

Between the two is the working country. Holding is when the rode arrives flat, a good length of chain still resting on the bottom in reserve, and the curve deep enough to absorb the surge without ever coming bar-taut. Snatching is when the reserve is gone: the rode straightens on every gust and jerks the anchor, and one jerk too hard breaks it out. Wandering is the far wall — so much scope that the anchor holds beautifully while the boat roams a circle wide enough to drag a neighbour's rode or find the shallows. The craft is to veer enough that it holds, and no more than the anchorage has room for.

Four scopes, snatching to roaming

From an anchor being levered out to one lost in its own swing

  1. Snatching. Too short a scope: the rode comes up in a near-straight line and the pull at the anchor tips well up off the bottom. Every gust jerks the shank upward instead of dragging it flat, and the flukes are levered out of the ground. Nothing has parted — there is simply no curve left to give.
  2. Just holding. Enough scope that the rode arrives flat and a short length of chain rests on the bottom. It holds in the present blow, but the reserve is thin — a harder gust would straighten it and start it snatching. The classic "lee-shore, no room" set: watched all night.
  3. Set and easy. A generous curve, a good reserve of chain flat on the bottom, and the pull arriving dead level. The surge is absorbed by the rode lifting and settling, and the anchor never feels the gusts as gusts. This is the band you veer for: give enough that the chain does the worrying.
  4. Wandering. More scope than the anchorage can hold: the anchor is set hard, but the boat ranges a circle wide enough to foul a neighbour, sail into the shallows, or snub across the tide. Holds the boat, loses the anchorage. Too much give is its own kind of trouble.

Three who veer a rode

The same curve, three trades

The cruiser

the scope

Reads the depth at high water, not the depth now, and veers to it — five to one for a lazy afternoon, seven or more when it will blow — then backs down hard to set the flukes and watches the shore for an hour to be sure the reserve is really there.

The ground-tackle rigger

the weight

Puts the weight where it deepens the curve: heavy chain near the anchor, or a kellet slung on the rode to force the belly down. Buys the flat arrival with mass on the line rather than length, where the anchorage is too tight to veer long.

The mooring master

the room

Lays the field so every swing circle clears the next, because the same slack that holds each boat lets it roam. Trades scope for room with heavier ground tackle and shorter rodes when the harbour is packed, and knows a snubbing line saves the deck from the shocks a short rode passes on.
