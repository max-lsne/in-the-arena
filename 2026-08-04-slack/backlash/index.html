backlash · noun · /ˈbaklaʃ/ — the clearance between meshing teeth, and the lost motion it costs on reversal

Backlash

Cut the teeth a hair loose so they never bind — and pay for it in lost motion the instant you reverse.

Two gears cannot be cut to touch on both flanks at once. If the teeth met with no gap they would jam the moment anything changed — a film of oil, a fleck of grit, a degree of warmth swelling the metal — and a jammed gear train does not slip, it seizes, and the heat of seizing welds it. So every gear pair is cut a little loose: a measured backlash, a clearance between the back of one tooth and the front of the next, so the driving flanks can carry the load while the trailing flanks stand clear. That clearance is the give, and the give is what lets the train run free, run cool, and run dirty. But it has a price, and you pay it the instant the load reverses: before the far side of the tooth can take up the drive, it must first fall clear across the gap — and everything downstream sits still, waiting, through that fall. That waiting is lost motion, and it is why a hair too much clearance turns a precise machine into a vague one.

Where the freedom comes from

The gap is what keeps it from seizing

A gear tooth drives on one flank at a time — the leading face pushing the load. The trailing face need never touch, and it must not: the clearance behind it is the room the tooth needs to enter and leave the mesh without wedging, to hold a film of lubricant, and to grow when it warms. Metal in a working train can swell a few thousandths across a hot afternoon, and load bends the teeth as well; if the clearance is smaller than that swelling and bending together, the trailing flanks close, the teeth bind on both sides at once, and the friction that follows makes still more heat.

The clearance must be larger than everything that would close it — thermal growth and load deflection together — or the teeth bind. But every thousandth of clearance beyond that is lost motion, waiting to be felt on the next reversal.

Two things set the floor under the gap. The duty is heat and load: a fast, hard, hot drive swells and bends far more than a slow, light, cool one, so it must be cut looser just to stay free — and it is also allowed looser, because it was never asked to be precise. The speed sets what the surplus costs: cross a gap slowly and the trailing flank arrives with a tap; cross it fast and it arrives with a hammer, and the hammer of reversed backlash is what pits the flanks and rattles a train to pieces. Neither has anything to do with how strong the gears are. A tight gap on a cool, slow drive out-positions a loose one every time — because precision was never about strength. It was about the size of the gap.

The one catch

The gap that frees the mesh loses its place

Clearance this useful is paid for at every change of direction. The same gap that keeps the teeth from binding is a hole the drive falls through before it bites the other way:

Too little clearance and the teeth bind — they close on both flanks, jam, and cook themselves solid. Too much and the train hammers on reversal and loses its place — the output lags the input by the whole width of the gap.

Between the two is the working country. Meshing clean is when the gap is just larger than everything that would close it, so the teeth run free and cool, yet small enough that the lost motion stays inside what the job can tolerate. Binding is when heat and load close the gap to nothing: the teeth wedge, the friction climbs, and a seized train wrecks itself in seconds. Slop is the far wall — so much clearance that the output wanders behind the input and every reversal lands as a knock. The craft is to cut the gap just wider than it must be to stay free, and not one thousandth wider.

Four gaps, binding to sloppy

From a train that seizes to one that hammers itself apart

  1. Binding. Too tight: thermal growth and load deflection close the gap to nothing, the teeth touch on both flanks at once, and the mesh jams. Friction climbs, heat follows, and a seized train can weld itself in seconds. Nothing was under-built — the clearance was simply smaller than the day.
  2. Just free. Clearance barely larger than what closes it: the teeth run free at temperature, but the margin is thin — a hotter run or a harder load would take up the gap and start it binding. Runs today, watched tomorrow.
  3. Meshing clean. A gap comfortably clear of binding yet tight enough that the lost motion sits inside the tolerance and the reversal lands as a tap, not a knock. The band you cut for: free, cool, and precise enough for the job.
  4. Slop. Far more clearance than needed: the train runs free but the output lags the input by the whole gap, so every reversal is a lurch and every change of direction lands as a hammer that pits the flanks. Holds nothing in place. Too much give wears itself out.

Three who set a gap

The same clearance, three trades

The gear cutter

the gap

Cuts the tooth a controlled thou undersize so the pair meshes with a measured backlash — enough to run free and hold oil, no more than the drive can tolerate — and checks it with a feeler and a dial before the train ever turns.

The machine-tool builder

the reversal

Fights lost motion where a cut depends on it: preloaded ballscrews, split anti-backlash gears sprung apart, encoders on the far side of the gap. Spends money to shrink the clearance the axis feels, because the reversal is where the part goes wrong.

The millwright

the heat

Sets the gap for the hot, hard duty, not the cold bench: reads the swelling and the deflection, leaves the clearance to survive the worst afternoon, and knows a train that runs sweet cold can seize an hour into a heavy shift.
