breath · noun · /brɛθ/ — the room a structure is given to expand and contract with the heat

Breath

Leave the rail a gap to grow into — or the summer sun turns the growth it cannot make into a force that buckles it.

Steel does not know how long you meant it to be. Warm it and it grows; cool it and it shrinks, and the amount is not small — a welded rail across a hot afternoon can want to be several inches longer than it was at dawn. If it is free to grow, it grows and nothing happens. If it is pinned at both ends and cannot, then every millimetre of length it was denied comes back as force, and the force is enormous, because the same stiffness that makes steel useful makes it a terrible spring: a fraction of a percent of strain it cannot shed is tonnes of thrust. So a rail, a bridge deck, a long run of steam pipe is not laid tight. It is laid with a gap — a designed slack, room to breathe — and the gap is sized against the hottest day the metal will ever see. Cut it too small and the heat it cannot spend as growth it spends as thrust, and the rail throws itself sideways in a buckle — a sun kink — that will derail a train. Cut it too large and the joint gapes all winter and hammers every wheel that crosses.

Where the force comes from

Growth denied becomes thrust

Heat a bar of length L by ΔT and it wants to lengthen by αLΔT, where α is how eagerly the material expands. Let it, and there is no force at all — expansion is free when it is allowed. Deny it, even partly, and the denied length becomes strain, and strain in a stiff material becomes stress at a fearsome rate: multiply the strain it cannot shed by the elastic modulus and you have the compressive stress in the rail, and it climbs to buckling on a temperature rise most people would call a warm day.

ΔL = α · L · ΔT is the growth the heat demands. Give it a gap at least that big and it grows into the gap freely. Give it less, and the shortfall becomes compressive stress E · (shortfall / L) — until the rail can no longer hold a straight line and throws itself sideways.

Two things set the demand. The span is the length: a long welded run wants to grow far more than a short one, so it needs a bigger gap or a way to hold itself neutral. The material is its eagerness and its stiffness together — aluminium grows nearly twice as hard as steel for the same warming, concrete far less; and the stiffer it is, the more violently a denied millimetre answers back. None of it depends on the rail being strong. A stronger rail buckles at almost the same temperature as a weaker one, because buckling is not a failure of strength — it is a failure to leave room.

The one catch

The gap that saves it in summer gapes in winter

Slack this necessary is paid for at the joint. The same gap that lets the rail breathe in the heat is an open mouth in the cold:

Too small a gap and the summer heat buckles the rail. Too large and the winter joint gapes wide and hammers every wheel — the impact that batters the rail-ends, pounds the ballast, and cracks the joint it lives in.

Between the two is the working country. Breathing is when the gap is sized to the swing: it closes almost shut on the hottest day — so the joint runs smooth when it matters and the rail never builds real thrust — and opens only a little on the coldest. Buckling is when the gap is gone before the peak: the growth it cannot make becomes thrust, and the rail throws itself out of line. Clattering is the far wall — so much gap that the joint gapes all year and every wheel lands a blow that fatigues the ends. The craft is to size the gap to the hottest day and no larger, or, on the longest spans, to stop laying gaps at all and hold the rail neutral so it never needs one.

Four gaps, buckling to hammering

From a rail thrown out of line to a joint that pounds itself apart

  1. Buckling. The gap closes before the hottest hour, and the growth the rail cannot make becomes compressive thrust. Past a threshold the rail can no longer hold a straight line and throws itself sideways — a sun kink. Nothing was weak; there was simply no room left to grow into.
  2. Just breathing. The gap closes right at the peak and the rail sits in light compression on the hottest day — normal, and safe, but with no margin. A hotter summer than the one it was laid for would take up the last of the gap and start it thrusting.
  3. Breathing easy. The gap is sized to the swing: nearly shut at the peak, a little open at the trough, and the rail never builds real thrust nor gapes enough to pound. The band you lay for — room for the hottest day the metal will see, and not a millimetre wasted.
  4. Clattering. Far more gap than the swing needs: the joint gapes wide the year round and every wheel drops into it and climbs out, hammering the rail-ends, battering the ballast, and cracking the joint. Holds against no heat that was ever coming. Too much give pounds itself to pieces.

Three who leave a gap

The same growth, three trades

The plate-layer

the gap

Sets the expansion gap between rail-ends to the rail temperature on the day of laying, reading a chart of gap against thermometer, so the joint closes at the design maximum and never sooner — and re-gaps a joint that has crept out of true before it can kink.

The CWR engineer

the neutral

On continuous welded rail, stops laying gaps at all: stresses the rail to a neutral temperature so it sits in neither push nor pull at the middle of the range, and clamps it hard enough that the whole swing is held as stress the sleepers and ballast can take without letting it bow.

The bridge builder

the joint

Gives a long deck a finger joint or a roller bearing — an expansion device built to gape wide and still carry a wheel — because a span that grows a hand's breadth cannot be left to a plain gap. Buys the breath the deck must have without the hammer a bare gap would deal.

It opens on a gap cut too small for a long steel span in a hard summer — the rail buckling. Cut the gap wider — and watch the thrust fall away before it kinks.

The breathing band is where the gap is at least the growth the heat demands, so it closes near the peak without thrusting — yet no wider than that, so the cold joint does not gape and hammer. Cut more gap to grow into; a longer span, a harder heat, or a more eager material all raise the demand.
