# Sponge

**You do not commit the sack of flour to find out whether the yeast is alive.
You commit a spoonful.**

A **sponge** is the small pre-ferment a baker mixes the night before the bake —
a spoonful of flour, water, and the culture, stirred slack and set aside warm.
By morning it has answered the one question the whole day's baking hangs on. It
has either domed, gone webby and sour-sweet and alive, or it sits there flat and
dead. Either way it told you the truth about the culture, and it told you at the
cost of a spoonful — not at the cost of a sack of flour, a proving trough, and a
morning at the oven.

Sponge is a small page built around that one move: run the risk small, where you
can afford to lose, before you run it large, where you cannot.

> Yeast is alive, and living things fail quietly. A spent culture looks and
> smells fine right up until the dough will not rise — hours in, with the batch
> mixed and the oven hot. The sponge moves that discovery to the front, where it
> is cheap.

## The batch, and the sponge

Two things are on the table, and the whole discipline is telling them apart:

- **The batch — what you cannot afford to lose.** The full mix: the sack, the
  trough, the proving time, the heat of the oven, the morning. Commit it against
  a dead culture and you find out at the end, when every input is already spent.
- **The sponge — what you can.** A spoonful, set apart on purpose, that runs the
  same risk the batch runs and costs almost nothing. It proves or it fails, and
  either answer is worth having before the trough is touched.

## What proving tells you

A sponge answers in stages — it moves through the same rise the batch will, only
smaller and sooner. Coldest first, the reading you want last:

- **Set — mixed and put by.** It tells you nothing yet; a sponge read too early
  reads as dead when it is only cold.
- **Waking — the first bubbles.** The culture took, but not proof of how far it
  will go. Hopeful, not decisive.
- **Domed — risen and alive.** The reading you set the sponge to get. Commit the
  batch to it with your eyes open.
- **Flat — set and never rose.** The other honest answer, and the valuable one.
  You are out a spoonful and a night, and you have saved the sack.

The sponge stands for any small, faithful trial you run ahead of a large
commitment — the pilot, the sample, the dry run. It is only worth setting if it
runs the same risk the batch runs, and only useful if you will actually read a
flat one as a no. A sponge you overrule is flour you wasted twice.

## The proving shelf

The shelf sets a dozen sponges from a dozen starters. Some cultures are lively
and some are spent, and side by side you cannot tell which by looking. **Prove**
a jar to spend a spoonful and learn the truth — it domes or it comes up flat —
or **commit** it blind and stake a sack on a guess. The gauge keeps the tally:
how many you proved, how many flat cultures you caught cheap, and how many sacks
you committed without looking. Prove the whole shelf and it reads back exactly
which sacks are worth mixing; commit blind and it tells you, too late, which
mornings you threw away. *Fresh batch* re-sets the shelf, and the odds wander a
little each time, so no two batches read the same.
