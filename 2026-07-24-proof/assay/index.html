An assay is the test a metal must pass before anyone trusts it at its claimed fineness — a draw of a few grains, cut, weighed, and tried, that reports what the bar actually is rather than what its stamp says it is. The stamp is a promise; the assay is the proof. A few grains, spent on a test, stand between you and valuing a whole lot of debased metal at the price of fine. The stamp is a claim; the assay is the check

The lot is what you pay for. The assay is how you stop paying on trust.

A mark is only as honest as whoever struck it, and metal can be debased in ways no eye or scale will find — alloyed down, plated over, cored with something base. The stamp travels with the bar and asserts a fineness. The assay is the one thing that can contradict it.

The lot — valued on the claim

The whole parcel: every bar, priced at the fineness its stamp asserts. Trust the marks and you have bought a promise by weight. If one bar in the lot is cored with base metal, you paid fine for it and will find out only when you go to sell or to melt.

The draw — valued on the test

A few grains cut from the bar and actually tried — on the touchstone, in the cupel, against a known standard. It costs a sliver of the metal and a little time, and it returns the one number the stamp cannot fake: what the bar is, not what it says.

Not every check is an assay

Eyed, weighed, touched, cupelled — only the last two can catch a good fake.

There are cheaper tests than a true assay, and each of them can be fooled by a bar built to pass it. Knowing which test you actually ran is half of knowing what you know. From the most easily deceived to the one that settles it:

  1. Eyed — it looks right. The colour and the lustre are those of fine metal. But colour is the first thing a plater gets right; a base bar washed in gold passes the eye across the counter every time.
  2. Weighed — the mass is close. Better: density is hard to fake. But not impossible — a core of the right heavy base metal brings the weight back into range. A scale narrows the field; it does not close it.
  3. Touched — a streak on the stone. A real assay in miniature: rub the bar on the touchstone and try the streak with acid. It reads the surface truly, and catches most debasement. A deep plating can still lie beneath it.
  4. Cupelled — a draw tried to the metal. The settling test. A sample cut through the bar and fired in the cupel reports the fineness of the metal itself, core and all. It costs the draw; it cannot be shown a false face.

The assay stands for any test that samples the real thing rather than its presentation — the reference check, the audited figure, the traced claim. The cheap tests read the surface the seller chose to show you. The assay cuts through it. Run the test that matches the price you are about to pay.

The whole of the method

Cut a draw. Try it to the metal. Value the lot on what assayed.

  1. 1

    Cut a representative draw

    Sample the bar where a fake would hide — through it, not off its polished face. A draw that only touches the surface tests the surface; a debased core needs a cut that reaches it.

  2. 2

    Try it against a known standard

    Assay the draw to a fineness you can trust — a real number, not a comparison to the seller's word. The point is a reading the stamp cannot supply and cannot contradict.

  3. 3

    Value the lot on the assay

    Price every bar at what its draw returned, not at what its mark claimed. The debased bar caught on a few grains is a bargain avoided; the same bar bought on its stamp is a loss you carry.

Who assays one

For anyone who can test the sample before they trust the claim.

The buyer

Do not value the deal on the deck. Draw the one figure that carries the price — the cohort, the contract, the reference — and test it to the metal. The number a seller chose to show you is the stamp, not the assay.

The analyst

Do not trust the summary because it is formatted well. Pull the raw sample the headline claims to summarise and check it yourself. A clean chart is a polished face; the assay is in the rows beneath it.

The hirer

Do not staff the team on the résumé. Give one real task that draws on what the page asserts, and read the work itself. The claim is stamped; the ability is what assays out when you cut into it.
