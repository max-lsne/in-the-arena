hypotrochoid · noun · the curve traced by a point fixed inside a circle that rolls around the inside of a fixed circle — the figure a spirograph winds

Rosette

The pen drawn in off the rim, and the flower it winds.

Take the same wheel rolled on a ring, but pin the pen short of the edge instead of on it, and the sharp cusps round off into petals — the woven flower a spirograph draws. Rolling inside the ring it is a hypotrochoid; outside, an epitrochoid. The count is still the ratio of the two radii: reduce R:r to p:q and there are p petals, closing after q turns. What the offset changes is only how deep the petals cut — near the centre a shallow scallop, out toward the rim deep loops that cross themselves, at the rim the cusps return, at the centre a plain circle. Set the radii and the offset, roll inside or out, and watch the flower wind.

The bench

The pen, held short of the edge

The gold circle is the ring, the steel circle the wheel rolling on it, exactly as before. What is new is the faint dotted circle inside the wheel: the offset circle of radius d that the pen now rides, short of the rim. The pen still turns at the no-slip rate against the centre's swing — so the count of petals is unchanged — but because it no longer reaches the ring it never quite stops, and the curve stays smooth. Slide the offset out to the rim and the dotted circle grows to the wheel; the petals sharpen toward cusps. Slide it to the centre and the circle vanishes to a point; the figure shrinks to the plain path the centre rides.

Why the offset only sets the depth

The ratio counts the petals; the offset cuts them

  • The pen's two turns are geared by the same no-slip ratio as on the rim: the centre swings once round the ring while the wheel spins R/r times under it. Moving the pen in from the rim shortens its reach but does not touch that gearing, so the petal count is still the ratio — R:r = p:q gives p petals, closing after q turns.
  • The offset d sets only the amplitude of the fast turn against the slow one. Small d: the fast wobble is gentle and the petals are shallow scallops. Large d: the wobble outruns the drift and each petal throws a loop that crosses itself — the dense, over-woven flowers a spirograph is prized for.
  • The two limits are the neighbours of this curve. At d = r the pen is back on the rim and every petal pinches to a point: the cusped epicycloid or hypocycloid. At d = 0 the pen sits on the wheel's centre, which only rides the deferent, so the figure is a plain circle — the fast turn has nothing to say.
  • Because the count comes from the ratio, common factors flatten the flower: a 6:3 is really a 2:1, two petals, not six. The richest rosettes come from radii that are coprime — 7:3, 5:2 — where the wheel must travel its full several turns, laying every petal, before the curve finally meets its own start.
  • Slip breaks the gearing just as it does on the rim: a rolling rate a hair off the rational ratio never brings the pen home, so the rosette does not close but precesses, each winding rotated from the one before — a flower drawn by a wheel that is forever almost, but never quite, in step with its ring.

The ratio decides how many petals there are; the offset decides only how deep they are cut — the same flower, held nearer the rim or nearer the heart.
