epicycloid · noun · the curve traced by a point on the rim of a circle that rolls without slipping around the outside of a fixed circle — a hypocycloid when it rolls around the inside

Cusp

A wheel rolled on a wheel, and the pointed curve its rim writes.

Roll a small wheel around the outside of a fixed ring and a pen pinned to its rim draws an epicycloid; roll it around the inside and the same pen draws a hypocycloid. The whole figure is carried by a single number — the ratio of the two radii. Reduce that ratio to lowest terms p:q and you can read the figure before it is drawn: it will have p cusps and will close after q turns of the wheel around the ring. One cusp outside is a cardioid, two a nephroid; three inside is a deltoid, four an astroid — and two inside is the Tusi couple, which falls flat into a straight line. Set the radii, roll inside or out, and watch the pen draw.

The bench

One wheel, rolled on a ring

The gold circle is the ring, held still. The steel circle is the wheel that rolls on it without slipping; the dashed circle is the path its centre rides — a circle of radius R+r outside, R−r inside. The copper dot is the pen, fixed to the rim at the wheel's edge, and the copper line is the curve it leaves. Because the wheel does not slip, its own turning is locked to how far it has travelled, so the pen is driven at a fixed rate against the centre's march — and that one coupling is the whole figure. Where the pen's own point of the rim touches the ring, the pen stops dead for an instant: a cusp.

Why the ratio is the whole figure

A curve that wears its radii as cusps

  • No slipping means the arc rolled is the arc travelled: when the wheel's centre has swung through an angle θ round the ring, the wheel has turned under it by exactly (R/r)·θ. The pen's two motions — the centre's swing and the wheel's spin — are therefore locked in the ratio R:r, and nothing else sets the shape.
  • Write the ratio in lowest terms R:r = p:q. The figure then has p cusps, evenly spaced, and closes after q turns of the wheel around the ring. A wheel that divides the ring evenly (q = 1) closes in one lap; a 5:2 needs two laps to shut, crossing itself into a five-pointed star.
  • A cusp is where the pen's own point of the rim is the one touching the ring: at that instant it is the pivot, momentarily still, and the curve comes to a sharp point. The cusps all sit on the ring, so counting them against the ring reads the ratio with no measurement at all.
  • Small ratios are the named curves. Outside: R = r is a cardioid (one cusp), R = 2r a nephroid (two). Inside: R = 3r a deltoid, R = 4r an astroid. And R = 2r inside is the Tusi couple — the wheel half the ring — where the rim point runs dead straight along a diameter: two circles making a line.
  • Slip breaks the lock. Let the wheel turn a hair faster or slower than rolling demands and the rate is no longer the rational p:q: the pen never returns to a cusp it has already made, so the figure will not close and instead precesses, each near-repeat rotated from the last — the drawn record of a ratio that is almost, but not quite, whole.

A wheel on a ring has only one freedom once it may not slip — and the curve is the whole of what that single freedom, counted in two radii, is made to say.
