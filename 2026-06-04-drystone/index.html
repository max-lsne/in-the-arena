A drystone wall holds because every stone is in conversation with three others — never forced, always fitted. The Yorkshire wall stands four hundred years. The Burren walls stand eight centuries. The walls that last are not the ones bound by glue, but the ones whose fit was patient. Drystone is a small page for the practices that hold the same way.

Plans
are mortared.

Most planning is mortared — every commitment locked in, scheduled, name-tagged, joined with glue. A mortared plan is tidy until the ground moves; and then the whole thing cracks in a line, because nothing could give and nothing could settle.

A drystone is built by fit, not glue. Each stone earns its place by sitting truly against the three it touches. The wall stands not because it is forced together, but because each piece has found its hold. The ground moves; the wall shrugs and stays. The fit is the work.

How it works

  1. 01

    Find a stone

    One practice — a habit, a ritual, a commitment — that you mean to fit into the working life. Not yet placed. Found in the field, weighed in the hand.

  2. 02

    Pick a course

    Foundation for the wide base stones. Wall for the middle running courses. Coping for the topmost stones that lock the wall down for weather.

  3. 03

    Set it true

    A stone begins found, then set, then settled after a season, then lichened — held long enough for moss to grow. The longer it sits, the more the wall is the wall.

  4. 04

    Don't force the fit

    A forced stone falls in the first frost. If a practice will not seat against the three it touches, set it down and find another. The fit is the practice. The fit is what holds.

The three courses

  • Foundation. The wide stones at the base — the practices that hold up everything else: the morning hour, the Sunday with no work, the weekly review that does not get skipped.
  • Wall. The middle running courses — the steady commitments that fit between the base and the top: the Friday close-of-week, the monthly mentoring call, the seasonal cull of inboxes.
  • Coping. The topmost stones that lock the wall down for weather — the rituals that finish a day, a quarter, a year: the broom at the end of work, the long walk after a hard week, the December retreat.

Who keeps a wall

The runner, nine winters in at six

The 6am run is a foundation stone lichened across nine winters. The Wednesday yoga is a wall stone, settled three years. The post-marathon week of rest is a coping — placed once a year, locked tight at the top. The wall stands because the stones underneath have not moved.

The therapist between two long cases

The Sunday with no screens has settled three seasons. The Tuesday supervision call is a wall stone, six years held. The December week off without email is the coping — placed in 2019, lichened thick enough that nothing crosses it now. The fit was patient.

The carpenter at the end of every day

The end-of-day broom is foundation — twenty-two years against the same workshop floor, lichened so deep the broom knows the corners by heart. The Saturday tool-sharpening is set this winter, still finding its seat. The yearly attic clear-out is coping, autumn after autumn.

The novelist on the slow second book

The 200-words-before-coffee is set this year — placed at the equinox, still bedding in. The Thursday long walk is settled four. The annual fortnight where the manuscript stays closed is coping, the topmost stone that lets the rest of the wall breathe. No glue. Just fit.
