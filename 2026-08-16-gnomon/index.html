Gnomon

Stand a rod upright in the sun. Its shadow is not idle: it swings round through the day, and it stretches and shrinks through the year. Read the swing and you have the hour; read the length and you have the season. One still stick, and the whole turning sky reporting to the ground.

This is the oldest instrument there is — older than writing, older than the wheel used for anything but pots — and it works because the thing it measures never stops moving. The sun climbs the sky through the morning and falls through the afternoon, so the shadow it throws is short and points away from it, swinging from west round to north to east as the day turns. And the sun rides higher in summer than in winter, so the same noon shadow is a stub in June and a long finger in December. A rod that does nothing but stand still turns both of those motions into marks you can read, because a shadow is just the sun's position, drawn on the floor. There is a clean geometry underneath it: the height of the sun above the horizon is set by sin a = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos H — your latitude φ, the sun's tilt for the day δ, and how far the day has turned from noon, H — and the shadow follows straight from that. This is the gnomon, and the discipline of reading it, gnomonics, is where geometry and the calendar first met.

Where the shadow comes from

The shadow is the sun's height, drawn on the floor

A shadow has only two things to say: which way it points, and how long it is. Both come from one fact — where the sun is in the sky — and where the sun is comes from three numbers. Your latitude φ, fixed for a place. The sun's declination δ, how far north or south of the celestial equator it stands that day, swinging slowly between +23.4° at midsummer and −23.4° at midwinter and passing through zero at the two equinoxes. And the hour angle H, how far the sky has turned since the sun stood due south at noon — fifteen degrees for every hour, since the whole sky comes round once in twenty-four.

Put those three into the one equation of the sundial and out comes the sun's altitude — its height above the horizon:

sin a = sin φ · sin δ + cos φ · cos δ · cos H.

That single line holds the whole day. At noon H = 0, its cosine is one, and the sun stands as high as it will get. Toward sunrise and sunset H grows, its cosine falls, and the altitude drops to zero — the moment the shadow runs out to the horizon and the sun slips under it. The shadow's length is then pure right-angle triangle: a rod of height h throws a shadow h / tan a long — short when the sun is high, stretching without limit as it sinks. And the shadow's direction is simply away from the sun: find the sun's compass bearing and the shadow lies on the opposite side, to the degree.

The mark a whole day makes

The tip draws a straight line at the equinox and a hyperbola on every other day

Watch not the shadow but its far tip, and let a whole day run. In the morning the sun is low in the east and the tip is flung far to the west; it swings round and pulls in toward the rod as noon approaches and the sun climbs; then it swings on and stretches out to the east as the sun sinks. The path it traces is not a circle and not an arc drawn by hand — it is a conic section, and which one depends on the day.

On the two equinoxes the tip runs dead straight, a clean east–west line. On every other day of the year it bends into a hyperbola — bowing south of that line all summer, north of it all winter — and inside the polar circles, where the sun wheels round without setting, the tip closes the curve into an ellipse.

The reason is older than the sundial and belongs to Apollonius: through the day the sun rides a slanted circle in the sky, and the rays grazing the rod's tip sweep out a cone. The ground is a flat plane slicing that cone, and a plane through a cone cuts a conic — a line when it is parallel to the cone's edge, which happens exactly at the equinox, and a hyperbola when it is steeper, which is every other day. So a sundial's face, if you scratch in the day-curves for a few dates, becomes a calendar too: the shadow tip tells you not just the hour, from where it sits along the curve, but the date, from which curve it is riding. The old dials call these the declination lines, and the pages of a stone one are the seasons.

The bench

Set the place and the day, then walk the sun across the sky

Looking straight down on the ground, north at the top. The rod stands at the centre; its shadow reaches out from it, and the faint curve is the whole day's path of the shadow tip — the declination line for the date you have set. Move the time and watch the shadow swing and breathe; set the latitude and the day of the year and watch the curve change shape — straight at an equinox, bowed the rest of the year. The readout gives the sun's altitude and bearing, the shadow's length in rod-heights and its compass direction, and which conic the day is drawing. Then press Run the day and hand the sky its motion: the sun walks from sunrise to sunset and round again. The numbers are the real ones: sin a = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos H.

Why keep it

The instrument that made time a place

The gnomon is worth a page because it is the moment a natural motion first became a reading. Nobody built the sun; someone only noticed that a stick's shadow moved in a way that repeated, and thought to scratch marks where it fell. Every clock since is a descendant of that thought — the escapement, the pendulum, the quartz sliver, the atom counted in a lab — each one a steadier stand-in for a shadow, and each one still set, in the end, against the sun the gnomon reads directly.

There is one refinement the bench leaves out, and it is the whole art of the later dials: tilt the rod so it points not straight up but at the celestial pole — north, and up from the horizon by exactly your latitude — and the shadow's swing becomes even, a clean fifteen degrees an hour all day long, so the hour lines can be ruled at equal angles instead of crowded and stretched. That slanted rod is called the style, and the angle you tilt it is your latitude written into brass. A sundial, in the end, is a small model of the Earth's axis, laid on a wall, keeping the one time the whole sky agrees on.
