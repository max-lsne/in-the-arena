cant · noun · /kant/ — the tilt built into a curve, the outer rail raised above the inner so the track leans into the turn

Cant

Raise the outer rail just enough, and the curve pulls down through the floor — not sideways into the passenger.

A train rounding a curve is thrown outward, and there is nothing to be done about the throw: it is the curve, and the speed, and the mass. What the engineer can do is decide which way the floor faces. Lift the outer rail above the inner — that is the cant, the superelevation — and the whole track leans into the turn. Now gravity no longer pulls straight down the passenger's spine; it pulls a little toward the low, inner rail, and that inward lean is spent cancelling the outward throw. Get the tilt right for the speed and the two balance exactly: the resultant of gravity and the curve's pull runs plumb through the floor, the flanges touch neither rail, and a full glass on the table does not spill. The cant is not there to hold the train down. It is there to point "down" in the one direction that turns a sideways force into weight the track already knows how to carry.

Where the balance comes from

One speed makes the side force vanish

Stand a train still on a canted curve and it simply leans inward, its weight leaning on the low rail. Run it round at speed and the outward throw — v²/R, growing with the square of the speed and shrinking with the radius — pushes the other way, out toward the high rail. Somewhere between stopped and flat-out there is exactly one speed where the inward lean of the tilt and the outward throw of the curve are equal and opposite. That is the balancing speed, and at it the net sideways force is not small — it is nothing.

The cant that balances a curve rises with the square of the speed you build it for, and falls as the radius opens out. A sharper, faster curve wants more tilt; a broad, gentle one wants almost none.

So a curve is tuned like an instrument to a chosen speed. The permanent-way engineer picks the speed the line is really run at, works out the tilt that nulls the throw at exactly that speed, and lifts the outer rail by that much — a few centimetres, laid in and taken out gradually over a transition so no wheel meets the tilt as a step. Below that speed the tilt is too much and the train leans in; above it the tilt is too little and the train leans out. The art is that a real line carries more than one speed, so the single tilt it can only be built with must be a bargain struck between them all.

The one bargain

The give that balances one train unbalances another

The gap between the speed a curve is canted for and the speed a given train actually runs has a name and a number. Express it as the extra cant that speed would have wanted — the cant deficiency — and the sideways force the passenger feels falls straight out of it:

Run faster than the balancing speed and you have cant deficiency: the tilt is too little, the train leans out, and the load piles onto the outer rail. Run slower on a high cant and you have cant excess: the tilt is too much, the train leans in, and the load piles onto the inner rail. One curve, one tilt — and every train that is not doing the balancing speed pays in one direction or the other.

Both walls are real, and they are not the same wall. Deficiency is capped by the ride and by safety at speed — leave too much of it and the outer flange grinds, the ride is flung to the high side, and at the edge the outer wheel can climb the rail. Excess is capped by the slow and the stopped train — pile too much tilt under a crawling freight and the inner rail wears, the load shifts to the low side, and a train standing on the curve leans toward the ditch. And the cant itself has a ceiling — raise the outer rail past about 150 mm and the stopped train's inward lean becomes the danger. So the engineer works in a box with four sides, and the fast train and the slow train push on opposite walls at once.

Four rides, dragging-in to thrown-out

From a train sliding down the bank to one climbing the rail

  1. Sliding in. Far too much cant for the speed — a heavy train crawling, or stopped, on a curve built for an express. The tilt hands its whole weight to the inner rail; the ride slumps to the low side, the inner rail is ground away, and a train left standing here leans toward the ditch. The quiet failure — it wears rather than wrecks, but it wears fast.
  2. In balance. Speed and tilt matched: the resultant of gravity and the curve's pull runs plumb through the floor, the flanges touch neither rail, and both rails share the load evenly. This is the speed the curve was tuned to, and the glass on the table does not slide. Nothing is being paid at all.
  3. Running wide. Faster than balance — real cant deficiency. The ride leans out and leans on the outer rail, and the passenger feels a steady tug toward the window. Inside the deficiency limit this is not a fault but the design: the fast train is meant to run a little wide, and the tilt was set knowing it would.
  4. Thrown out. Deficiency past the limit — more outward throw than the flange and the rail can civilly share. The ride is flung to the high side, the inner wheels unload, the outer flange bites, and at the edge it climbs the rail and lets go. The sudden failure, and the reason the deficiency wall is guarded with a speed limit on the post.

Three who read a tilt

The same curve, three trades

The p-way engineer

the tilt

Sets the cant. Picks the speed the line is really run at, works the tilt that nulls the throw there, then splits the difference so the express's deficiency and the freight's excess both sit inside their walls. Ramps it in and out over a transition curve so no train meets the tilt as a step, and never lifts the outer rail past what a stopped train can stand.

The driver

the speed

Reads the tilt with the throttle. Holds the line speed the curve was canted for — too fast and the deficiency tugs the coaches wide and grinds the outer flange; too slow on a high cant and the wheels drag on the inner rail. Feels the balance in the seat: at the right speed the curve barely registers, and that stillness is the cant doing its work.

The inspector

the measure

Trusts neither. Lays a level across the rails and reads the cant against the design, watches for twist where the tilt changes too fast, and for cant that has crept as the ballast settles. A curve that has quietly lost its lift hands every train a deficiency no timetable ever asked for — so the measure, not the memory, is what the speed is signed off against.

The bench

Set the tilt. Find the balance.

One curve, seen end-on: two rails, the outer one lifted, and a vehicle riding the tilt between them. Set the curve's radius, the cant you lift the outer rail by, and the line speed the train runs, then pick the stock — a loco-hauled rake, a multiple unit, or a tilting train, each of which can carry more deficiency than the last before the ride fails. The page works out the balancing speed, the cant deficiency the running speed leaves, the side force the passenger feels, and how much speed is left before the near wall — and lights the balance band on the gauge. It opens on a curve canted too low for the express that runs it: thrown out. Cant it true.

The balance band is the narrow span of deficiency where the ride comes home — enough tilt that the throw is all but cancelled, felt as almost nothing sideways. Lift the cant to lean the train into the turn; hold the speed the curve was tuned for; a broader radius asks for less tilt. Past the outer wall the deficiency throws the train wide and the flange climbs; past the inner wall the excess drags a slow train down onto the low rail. Stiffer stock — a unit, then a tilting train — can carry more deficiency before the ride fails, which is how a curve is run faster without being re-laid.
