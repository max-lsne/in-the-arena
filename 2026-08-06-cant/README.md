# Cant

**A train on a curve is thrown outward, and no track can stop the throw — it is
the speed, the radius, and the mass, and it obeys nobody. What the track can do
is choose which way "down" faces. Raise the outer rail above the inner by a few
centimetres — the *cant* — and the whole road leans into the turn, so gravity no
longer pulls sideways through the passenger but a little toward the low rail,
and that inward lean is spent cancelling the outward throw. Get the tilt right
for the speed and the two vanish together: the resultant runs plumb through the
floor, the flanges touch neither rail, and a full glass does not spill.**

The word is old and plain — *cant*, a tilt, a slope given to something on
purpose. On the railway it is superelevation: the outer rail of every curve is
laid higher than the inner, and the exact amount is not a flourish but a
calculation. There is one speed, for a given radius and a given tilt, where the
inward lean of the cant and the outward throw of the curve are equal and
opposite. It is called the **balancing speed**, and at it the sideways force on
the train is not merely small — it is *nothing at all*. Below it the tilt is too
much and the train leans in; above it the tilt is too little and the train leans
out. The whole craft is that a curve can only be built with one cant, and a real
line carries more than one speed across it.

## The one shape

Express the mismatch between the speed a curve is canted for and the speed a
train actually runs as the extra cant that speed *would* have wanted, and every
consequence falls out of that single number — the **cant deficiency**:

- Run **faster** than the balancing speed and you have deficiency: the tilt is
  too little, the train leans **out**, the load piles onto the outer rail, and
  the passenger feels a steady tug toward the window. A little of this is by
  design — the fast train is *meant* to run wide — but past a limit the outer
  flange grinds, the inner wheels unload, and at the edge the wheel climbs the
  rail and lets go. The sudden failure, guarded by the number on the speed post.
- Run **slower** on a high cant and you have excess: the tilt is too much, the
  train leans **in**, the load piles onto the inner rail, and a train standing on
  the curve leans toward the ditch. The quiet failure — it wears the inner rail
  rather than wrecking the train, but it wears it fast, and it is what caps how
  much cant a curve carrying slow freight can be given.

And the cant itself has a ceiling — lift the outer rail past about 150&nbsp;mm and
the *stopped* train's inward lean becomes the hazard on its own. So the engineer
works inside a box with four walls: the deficiency limit above, the excess limit
below, the applied-cant ceiling, and the fixed geometry of the curve. The fast
train pushes on one wall and the slow train pushes on the opposite one, at the
same time, and the single tilt that must serve them both is a bargain struck
between them.

The page is one curve seen end-on: two rails, the outer lifted, a vehicle riding
the tilt, and the forces drawn from its centre of mass — true gravity, the
track's idea of "down," and the resultant the speed actually makes. Set the
**radius**, the **cant**, and the **line speed**, pick the **stock** — loco-hauled,
a multiple unit, or a tilting train, each able to carry more deficiency than the
last — and watch the resultant swing from plumb to leaning out to leaning in as
the gauge below reads the deficiency against its two walls. The numbers are the
real ones: the balancing speed `√(g·E·R/G)`, the deficiency `G·v²/(g·R) − E`, the
side force `g·E_d/G`, and the deficiency and excess limits taken from permanent-way
practice.
