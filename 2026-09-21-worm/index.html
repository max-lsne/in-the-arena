worm · noun · /wəːm/ — the screw of a worm-and-wheel gear, whose single spiral thread meshes with the teeth of a wheel set across it; one turn of the worm advances the wheel by a single tooth, so a fine thread gives great reduction and, if fine enough, a drive the wheel cannot turn back; from Old English wyrm, a serpent — for the coil of the thread

Worm

A screw that turns a wheel with great force, and — cut fine enough — will not be turned back.

A worm is a screw laid against a toothed wheel so its spiral thread drives the teeth. Turn the worm and the wheel creeps round, one tooth per turn, slow and immensely strong: a single-thread worm on a forty-tooth wheel is a forty-to-one reduction in one small mesh. Then try it the other way — push on the wheel, try to drive the worm — and if the thread is fine enough nothing moves. The load cannot back-drive it; the gear holds itself, no brake, no pawl. That self-lock is the worm's whole reputation — a hoist that stays where you leave it, a tuning peg that keeps its string — and it is not free. It is bought, in full, from efficiency: the finer the thread that locks it, the more of every turn you put in is lost to friction as heat. One angle sets both.

Where the hold comes from

A thread is a ramp wrapped round a shaft

Unroll one turn of the worm's thread and it is a straight ramp: it climbs a height — the lead, how far the thread advances in one turn — across a length, the circumference. The angle that ramp makes with the flat is the lead angle λ. Every tooth of the wheel rides on that ramp, and the whole behaviour of the gear is the old, plain question of a block on an incline: will it slide, or will friction hold it?

A worm gear is a block on a ramp, wrapped into a circle. Drive the worm and you push the block along the ramp; push the wheel and you ask the block to slide back down. Friction answers, and the answer depends only on how steep the ramp is.

Friction sets a limit angle of its own — the friction angle ρ = arctan μ, where μ is the coefficient of friction at the mesh. A block on a ramp shallower than ρ will not slide however hard gravity pulls down the slope: the friction cone swallows the weight. Steeper than ρ and it slides. That is the entire self-locking rule, read straight off the incline: the worm holds the load — the wheel cannot drive it back — exactly while λ ≤ ρ.

So a worm is not self-locking because it is a screw, or because it reduces; a coarse, steep-thread worm hands the load straight back to your hand the moment you let go. It is self-locking only when its thread is cut shallower than the friction can hold, and the shallower you cut it the more surely it holds — and the more of the drive it burns getting there.

The one relation it embodies

One angle, two jobs

Take the lead angle λ of the thread, the friction angle ρ = arctan μ at the mesh, and a single-start worm on a wheel of z teeth. Idealise it as a square thread and the whole of it is three short lines:

  • The drive. Turn the worm and the wheel turns at a reduction of z : 1 — one tooth per turn — with an efficiency η = tan λ ⁄ tan(λ + ρ). It falls away as the lead grows fine: at the self-lock boundary it is already below one half, and below that boundary most of the work is heat.
  • The hold. Try to drive it backward, from the wheel, and the efficiency is η′ = tan(λ − ρ) ⁄ tan λ. When λ ≤ ρ that is zero or less: the load cannot move the worm, and the gear holds itself. When λ > ρ the load overhauls — it runs the worm backward and drops, unless a brake holds it.
  • The trade. The two share one number. Cut the lead fine — λ well under ρ — and you buy a firm self-lock and a high reduction, and pay with an efficiency near zero. Cut it steep and you buy efficiency, and pay by losing the hold. There is no lead that is both free and self-locking; the boundary λ = ρ is the best either can do.

So one angle does two opposite jobs, and setting it is the whole of the craft. A holding worm — a hoist, a jack, a peg — is cut deliberately shallow, under the friction angle, and its low efficiency is not a fault but the price of the lock, carried away as heat the housing must shed. A worm cut for power, to move a load and not hold it, is cut steep and above the friction angle, and given a brake to hold what it will no longer hold itself.

The worm gives you the hold or the efficiency, and charges the same coin for both: the lead angle. Ask it to hold without a brake and it will, and keep more than half of your work as heat for the favour.

Four leads, from a buried lock to a load that runs back

Along the one angle

  1. Buried. The lead is far under the friction angle — it holds the load like a wall, but the reduction is enormous and the efficiency near nothing: almost every turn you put in comes out as heat, and the gear runs hot. A firm lock, dearly paid for.
  2. Holds. The lead sits just under the friction angle. The worm holds the load with no brake, at the highest efficiency a self-locking lead allows — still under half, but the most the hold will give. This is where a holding worm is cut.
  3. Creeps. The lead has just passed the friction angle. The hold is gone: the load overhauls, but slowly — it creeps the worm backward, and a light brake or a touch more friction holds it. The edge of the cliff.
  4. Runs back. The lead is well over the friction angle. Efficient forward, and no hold at all: let go and the load runs the worm backward and drops. Fast, free, and useless as a brake — this worm must be braked to hold anything.

Who reads it, and for what

The same angle, three ways

The hoist-builder

wants the hold

Cuts the lead under the friction angle so the winch stays where it stops — no ratchet, no band brake, the gear its own lock. Pays in efficiency and cooling, and counts it cheap against a load that never runs away.

The gear-cutter

wants the power

For a drive that must move, not hold, cuts the lead over the friction angle — often a multi-start worm, steeper still — for efficiency, and fits a brake to hold what the gear no longer will. Reads λ against ρ and chooses which side to sit.

The luthier

wants it to stay put

Sets a worm to each tuning machine so the string's pull cannot back-drive the peg: the note holds. A fine lead, deep in the self-lock — efficiency no concern where the load never turns the gear more than a hair.

The discipline in the word

A machine that will push, but not be pushed

The worm is one of the few machines whose limitation is its point. A gear that could be driven from either end would be no good as a hold; the worm's refusal to be back-driven is exactly what makes it a brake, a lock, a thing that stays. And that refusal is not stubbornness of metal but a plain fact of the ramp: cut shallower than the friction can hold, a slope will not let its load slide back. The discipline is to see that the hold and the loss are the same fact seen twice, and to set the one angle knowing you cannot have both — to decide, before cutting the thread, whether this worm is to hold or to move.
