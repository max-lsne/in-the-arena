A towpath is the worn ribbon of stone beside the canal. A horse walks it at three miles an hour. A rope runs from its collar down to the bow of a narrowboat carrying forty tons of coal. The horse is not strong enough to lift the boat. But the rope is taut, and the boat moves, and at the end of the day it is twelve miles further along than it was at dawn. That is the whole thing.

Most productivity asks what the top speed is. The top speed is rarely the bottleneck. The bottleneck is the long stretch of canal between the wharfs, where nothing interesting happens and the wind is not at your back. A sprint over a single mile loses, every time, to a horse kept walking the whole twelve. The barge does not care which day it was hauled. The barge only cares that the rope stayed taut.

A towpath is a low pace held for a long time. Three miles an hour, six hours a day, six days a week. Nothing heroic. The horse is not stronger than yesterday. The boat is not lighter. The wharf is not closer than it was at dawn — except that it is twelve miles closer, and twelve miles is the day's haul.

How it works

  1. 01

    Tie on a small rope

    Pick one piece of work and tie a line to it. The smallest sustainable pull — a paragraph a day, an evening's hour, the next chapter — not the biggest you can manage on a good day. The rope only works if it stays taut on the bad ones too.

  2. 02

    Pick the boat

    Skiff for the small light work. Wherry for the small working work. Narrowboat for the long-haul of a year or two. Barge for the decade-long thing. The boat decides the pace; the pace is always the same three miles an hour.

  3. 03

    Walk the path

    Cast off, in tow, steady haul, made fast. Most of the work happens in the steady haul, where there is nothing to report. No story, no headwind, no breakthrough. Just the rope taut and the path under the horse's feet.

  4. 04

    Tie up at the next pound

    Every day's haul ends at a bollard. Twelve miles closer than dawn. Tie up. Rest the horse. Tomorrow the rope goes back on. The barge does not have to know whether you are heroic; it only has to keep moving at three miles an hour.

The four boats

  • Skiff. Small light work that sits high in the water — a daily note, a single push-up, the short letter. Easy to pull. Easy to forget you are pulling.
  • Wherry. Small working work — a chapter to be drafted, a garden corner to be cleared, an instrument practised a half hour at a time. Moves at three miles an hour without fuss.
  • Narrowboat. The year-or-two haul — a novel, a thesis, a slow recovery, a course finished evening by evening. Will not move without the rope.
  • Barge. The heaviest, slowest, longest hauls — the decade-long works, the rebuilt house, the second career. The pace is the same three miles an hour. The barge does not know it is a barge.

Who walks a towpath

The translator at the long Russian novel

Eight hundred and ninety pages of the original on the desk. A page a day, six days a week, on a corner of the kitchen table after the children are in bed. Three years and four months by the rough count. The page does not care which evening it is hauled. The page only cares that it is hauled today.

The carpenter restoring the old workshop

An hour before breakfast, every day since the autumn. A floorboard sanded. A length of beading mitred. The pile of off-cuts has grown a foot taller every month. By next spring the room will be a workshop again. No single morning has done much; every morning has done the same small thing.

The runner training for the spring marathon

The plan is on a sheet of paper pinned beside the back door. Five miles on Tuesday. Seven on Thursday. Long-slow on Saturday. The fast days are not faster than last year. The slow days are slower than last year. By April the marathon is twenty-eight weeks closer than it was in October.

The researcher building the corpus

One transcription a day, since the second winter of the grant. The corpus is at four thousand, two hundred and sixteen entries. The work has no breakthrough, no chapter, no flash. There is only the day's transcription, added quietly to the end of the file. The corpus is the rope's record.
