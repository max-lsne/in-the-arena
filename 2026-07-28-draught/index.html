draught · noun · /drɑːft/ — the unseen pull of air that makes a fire breathe

Draught

A fire does not take air. Air is pulled through it — by a column of its own hot breath, standing in the flue.

Burning heats the gas in the flue, and hot gas is lighter than cold. So the flue stands a tall column of light gas inside the heavy cold air of the day, and the heavy air, pressing in beneath, lifts the light column up and out the top. That lift, felt at the grate as a pull of fresh air through the fire, is the draught. You never see it. You see only what it fails to do: the smoke that rolls back into the room, or the heat that goes straight up the chimney and is gone.

Where the pull comes from

A light column falling upward

The word gathers a family of pulls — the draught of an ox is the load it hauls; the draught of a ship is how deep the water pulls her down; a draught of ale is what you pull from the cask. Under all of them is one idea: a drawing, a pull you know by its effect and never by its face. The draught of a chimney is the plainest of the lot.

The taller the column and the greater the difference in weight between the flue gas and the day, the harder the lift: pull ≈ height × (how much lighter the hot gas is than the cold air outside).

This is why the same chimney that roars in January can sulk and smoke in October, and why the fault is almost never the fire. A cold flue on a mild day is barely lighter than the air around it, and barely pulls at all. A hot flue on a frosty morning stands a heavy day on a very light column, and pulls hard. The fire has not changed. The day has, and with it the weight of the air the flue must lift its column through.

The one rule

The pull must match the fire's appetite

No less — and, just as much, no more.

Too little and the smoke the fire makes is faster than the flue can carry it. It has nowhere to go but back through the only other opening: the room. The fire smoulders in its own exhaust, tar lays down in the cool flue, and the house smells of last night's fire.

Too much and the draught drags air through the grate faster than the fuel can use it. The fire burns bright and burns out, and the heat you lit it for is pulled up the flue and thrown at the sky before the room ever feels it. A roaring fire is not a generous one — it is a leak.

You cannot make more heat by building a taller chimney. A taller chimney makes more pull, and past a point the extra pull is simply heat leaving faster. Height sets the draught; fuel sets the heat; the two are not the same lever, however much they feel like it on a cold night.

Between too little and too much is a band — enough pull to clear the smoke cleanly, not so much that it robs the room. The damper, a flap in the throat, is how you give back the draught you don't need: close it part way and the pull spends itself against the added resistance instead of dragging your heat out the top. You are not making less heat. You are keeping the heat you made.

Four flues, worst to true

From smoke in the room to a fire you forget about

  1. Spills. The pull is below what it takes to clear the fire's own smoke. The plume can't get up the flue, so it rolls out of the opening and into the room. Cold flue, mild day, a bore too wide to warm through — a fire that smokes the moment you shut the door.
  2. Sluggish. Enough draught to limp, not enough to burn clean. A low, lazy, yellow flame; smoke that leans rather than lifts; a flue slowly furring with the tar a cool, slow draught lays down. It works, in the way a thing works that you have to nurse all evening.
  3. Draws clean. The pull matches the appetite. Air comes through the grate steadily, the flame stands up and burns bright, the smoke goes where smoke should go — and you stop thinking about the chimney at all, which is the only sign a chimney is right.
  4. Roaring. Far more pull than the fire can use. It burns hard and fast, the fuel gone in an hour, and the heat you wanted in the room is dragged glowing up the flue. Sound, in that nothing is failing — and wasteful, in that the chimney is warming the sky with your fuel. Close the damper: the roar is heat you are allowed to keep.

Three who read a draught

The same pull, three trades

The chimney sweep

the flue itself

Knows a sluggish draught by the tar it leaves — a cool, slow pull that never dries the flue, and the creosote that furs and one day catches. Clears the bore so the column can warm through and the pull come back. A clean flue is a fast one.

The stove fitter

the appliance

Sizes the flue to the stove and the stove to the room, then hands over the one control that matters: the air. Sets the draught with height and bore so the damper has somewhere useful to sit — throttling a pull that is generous, not begging one that isn't.

The founder

the furnace

Wants draught past what any hearth needs, to force air through a deep bed of coke and melt iron. Builds the stack tall and runs it hot, because the melt is set by how hard the day's air can be pulled through the burning fuel — the same rule, wound all the way up.

The bench

It opens on a tall old flue roaring on a frosty night — throwing its heat up the chimney. Throttle it.

The clean band is the span of flue-gas velocity where the fire draws right — fast enough to clear the smoke, slow enough not to drag the heat out with it. Raise the flue or feed the fire to pull harder; close the damper to give back a pull that's too strong.
