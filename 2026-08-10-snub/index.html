snub · verb · /snʌb/ — to check a running line by taking a turn round a post, letting the friction of the wrap do the holding a bare hand never could

Snub

A line under tons of load can be held by a finger — if it takes a few turns round a post first. The turns do not add to your pull. They multiply it.

Loop a rope once round a bollard and haul on the tail, and the post carries some of the load for you. Loop it again and the post carries far more — not twice as much, but the same share again, of what is left. Each wrap sheds the same fraction of the tension into friction against the wood, so the load falls off geometrically as it runs round the post, and the little that reaches your hand is what remains after a compound discount taken turn after turn. This is the capstan equation, and it is one of the oldest exact results in mechanics: the tension on the loaded side and the tension you must hold on the slack side stand in the ratio eμθ, where θ is the whole angle the rope wraps and μ is how the rope grips the post. It does not depend on how thick the rope is, or how fat the post — only on the angle turned and the grip. Euler wrote it down; every deckhand knew it in the hand. Three turns of hemp on an oak bitt will hold a barge against the tide on two fingers. And the same exponent that lets a finger hold her means a finger can never haul her back: friction does not care which way you pull.

Where the grip comes from

Each wrap takes the same bite, so the bites compound

Follow the rope round the post from the loaded side to your hand. Over any small slice of the wrap, the rope presses inward on the post — that is what a curved rope under tension does, it squeezes whatever it bends around — and the post presses back. Friction on that pressed slice can hold back a small part of the tension, and the most it can hold is a fixed fraction of the tension passing through: the same fraction on every slice, because a slice further along is under less tension and presses less hard, in exact proportion. So the tension does not drop by a fixed amount per degree of wrap. It drops by a fixed percentage per degree, and a quantity that loses the same percentage over and over falls away as a decaying exponential.

Add the slices up around the whole wrap and the discount compounds into a single clean law: the loaded tension is the tension in your hand, times e raised to the friction times the wrap angle. Nothing about the rope's size survives. Only the angle and the grip.

The exponent is the whole story. A half turn of ordinary rope on wood multiplies your hand by something near three; a full turn, near nine; three full turns, near seven hundred. The numbers run away because the wrap sits in the exponent — every extra turn does not add a constant, it multiplies again by whatever one turn was worth. This is why the capstan and the winch and the sailor's cleat all work by the same trick, and why a tow of a thousand tons comes down, in the end, to a few coils and a hand on the tail.

The one bargain

Turns hold what strength cannot — and hold it both ways

The bargain the wrap offers is lopsided, and knowing which way it leans is the whole of the craft. To hold a load, friction is your friend: it is already fighting the load's pull, and it will keep fighting up to eμθ times whatever you supply. So a hand you could never haul with becomes a hand that holds anything, if you first spend a few turns. That is why you snub before you strain — you take the turns first and let the post take the ship, and only then put your hand to the tail to trim the last of it.

But friction does not know the difference between holding and hauling. The same wrap that multiplies your hold multiplies the load's grip on the post against you. To haul the line in over the wrap you must overcome the friction, not borrow it — the exponent that was worth seven hundred to the holder is worth seven hundred against the hauler. So you never pull a heavy line in over the turns. You surge it: ease the tail a hair, let the whole load slip round the post a little, and check it again.

Between holding fast and surging lies the edge the line lives on. Give the tail a shade less than the load's slack-side demand and nothing happens — the wrap holds, the reserve unspent. Give it a shade less than that and the line renders: the load overhauls your hand, the rope runs round the post in a rush, and if you are lucky you have another turn to throw and if you are not you have a rope burn and a barge adrift. The margin between fast and running is not force. It is turns. When a line is close to rendering you do not haul harder. You throw another turn, and the exponent does the rest.

Four lines, held to running

From a finger to spare, to a rope run out

  1. Held on a finger. The wrap is generous — turns to spare, a good grip — and the tension has fallen so far round the post that what reaches the tail is a nothing you could hold in your sleep. This is three turns on a bitt against a moored boat: the whole load buried in the post, the tail idle, and you free to make it fast or walk away. There is slack in every wrap.
  2. Held with a hand. Fewer turns, or a heavier load, and the tail now wants a real hand — a firm pull, arms set, but a pull a person can give and keep giving. The line holds and holds honestly, the reserve small but real. This is the working state of a hand on a fall: taking the strain, tailing the winch, trimming the sheet, the post carrying the bulk and the hand carrying the rest.
  3. On the verge. The tail demand has climbed to the very edge of what the hand can give. The line still holds — barely — but the whole reserve is spent, and a gust, a swell, a sea lifting the bow is all it would take to break the grip. A hand here does not relax and does not haul; it holds dead still and calls for another turn before the sea calls first.
  4. Rendered. The load has beaten the wrap: the slack-side tension the load demands is more than the hand can hold, so the rope runs round the post, paying out in a rush, friction screaming and smoking on the wood. Not broken — the rope is whole, the post is whole. It simply ran, because there were not turns enough to bring the load down to a hand. One more turn, taken in time, and it would never have started.

Three who read a line

The same wrap, three trades

The deckhand

the turns

Throws the turns and tails the line. Knows in the hand what the equation says on paper — that the first turn is worth more than the tenth pull, that you snub before you haul, that a line close to running wants a turn and not a heave. Takes the turns with the lay of the rope, keeps them from riding one over another into a jam, and when the load must move, surges it a hair at a time rather than fighting the wrap. Never stands in the bight.

The rigger

the grip

Chooses the rope and the post, and so chooses μ. Knows tarred hemp on rough oak grips harder than wire on a greased sheave, that a wet line and a dry line are two different tools, that a fair lead into the wrap earns its keep. Sizes the bitt not for strength — the numbers are trifling, the post barely feels the ship — but for turns: enough girth to take the coils it will need, fairly led, so the exponent is there when the hand asks for it.

The engineer

the number

Never touches the rope; writes the exponent. Reckons the hold not in the rope's strength but in eμθ — the ratio the wrap alone can supply — and reads the margin as the turns between the load's demand and the hand's reach. Knows the law is Euler's, that it ignores the rope's size entirely, and that its whole force lives in putting the wrap up in the exponent where a small change in turns is a large change in hold. Measures the snub in turns, not tons.
