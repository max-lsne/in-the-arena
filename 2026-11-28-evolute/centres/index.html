evolute · noun · the curve traced by the centre of curvature of another curve as the point runs along it — equally, the envelope of that curve's normals

Centres

The second curve an ellipse keeps — the track every best-fitting circle's centre rides.

At each point of a curve there is one circle that hugs it best — the osculating circle, the circle of curvature — and its centre sits a short way in along the normal, the line dropped square to the curve. Walk a point round an ellipse and that centre travels too, and the curve it writes is the evolute: a four-cusped star, a stretched astroid, coming to a point at the four places the ellipse is most and least sharply bent. The same curve answers to a second description that looks unrelated and is the very same thing — it is the envelope of all the normals, the one line every normal just touches and none of them crosses, exactly as yesterday's reflected rays wrapped a caustic. Nothing here is drawn from a formula — the centre is read off the curve's own bend, and the normals are a real family you can watch crowd onto the curve the centres trace.

The bench

One ellipse, and the curve its centres ride

The gold curve is the ellipse, held still. A point runs round it; at that point sits the steel normal, dropped square to the curve, and the best-fitting circle that hugs the ellipse there, its centre pinned a radius of curvature in along the normal. Drop a normal at every point and you get a whole family of lines — and the copper curve they all lean against, the one none of them crosses, is the evolute. It is also, point for point, the track of those circle-centres. The page draws it both ways and they land on the same line: the evolute is where a curve keeps its curvature written down.

Why the centres make a curve, and why it is the same as the envelope

One curve, reached two ways

  • At a point of the ellipse the curvature says how sharply it bends — the reciprocal of the radius of the circle that hugs it best. That osculating circle's centre of curvature lies on the inward normal, one radius of curvature in. It is read straight off the curve's first and second derivatives; no equation of the evolute is needed to find it.
  • Let the point run all the way round. The centre of curvature moves with it, and the curve it traces is the evolute. For an ellipse that curve is a four-cusped astroid, stretched along the axes — and it is pinched to a cusp at the four vertices, the ends of the two axes, where the curvature is momentarily steady and the centre pauses and reverses.
  • Now forget the circles and keep only the normals — one straight line dropped square to the ellipse at every point. A family of lines that turn as they slide has an envelope, the curve each line just touches and none of them crosses. The envelope of the normals is the evolute: each normal is tangent to it exactly at that point's centre of curvature. The copper curve is drawn here as the meeting of adjacent normals — the same way the caustic was drawn as the meeting of adjacent rays.
  • So the two constructions are one. Track the centres and you get a curve; wrap the normals and you get a curve; they are the same curve, because the place a normal touches its envelope is precisely the centre of the circle that hugs the curve there. A single idea, approached from the bend of the curve on one side and the crowding of its normals on the other.
  • Flatten the control toward a circle and the evolute shrinks: every normal of a circle passes through its centre, so the whole family wraps nothing but that one point, and the four cusps rush together into it. A circle is the one curve whose evolute is not a curve at all — it has no bend to spare.

The evolute is where a curve keeps its curvature — not a shadow cast over it but the record, drawn out as a line, of how hard it was turning at every step.
