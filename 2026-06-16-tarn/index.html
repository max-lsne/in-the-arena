A tarn is a small mountain lake left in a hollow the glacier scoured out ten thousand years ago, cupped in scree and sedge above the treeline, fed by snowmelt and a slow seep through the moss. In a dry August the lower pools may go. The high tarns stay. By September the water is dark enough to lose a stone in. The hill keeps it through the winter and gives it back to the beck next May.

Most of the year's water comes and goes. The becks rise after rain and are stone-dry in two days. The puddles in the lane last a morning. The cattle trough wants topping up each week. Even the deeper pools in the wood lose a foot a fortnight in a dry July, and by the second week of August half of them are mud cracked into hexagons.

A tarn is the other thing. A tarn is what holds. The snow falls on the fell in November and melts into the bowl in May, and is still there in August when every roadside ditch is bone-dry stone. A tarn is what a hill keeps for itself when no one is asking, and gives back to the beck the next May, and the May after that, and the May after that.

How it works

  1. 01

    Find the bowl

    The bowl is the hollow the glacier left ten thousand years ago — high enough that nothing taller than juniper will hold, low enough to catch the runoff from the ridge above. The corner of the desk that gets the morning light. The same chair, the same hour. The hollow that catches the meltwater for you.

  2. 02

    Catch the first drop

    Today, catch the first drop. Not a tarn. Not a pool. One drop from the snowfield above. A line of the morning entry, a single open-string scale, the half-page note that is the only note. The sun will evaporate it by noon. That is fine. There is no tarn yet.

  3. 03

    Hold it through the dry summer

    And the dry summer after. The first August is the test — the lower pools go, the becks dry, and the question is whether the bowl is deep enough to hold. The first hold is a pool. The fifth is a small tarn. The fifteenth is a year-round tarn the parish maps with a name on it.

  4. 04

    Become a cirque

    One year the bowl has a string of tarns at three heights with a beck running out under the col, and the next fell can see it from the ridge. The work is held by a landscape, not a season. The water is a watershed. The hill keeps it for you whether you walk up or not.

The four levels

  • Drop. A single drop off the rock above — the first half-page, the empty notebook with one entry, the line of code that is the only line. Could evaporate by noon.
  • Pool. A small gathered pool, days deep, summer-fragile — the first complete draft, the first ten weeks of practice carried unbroken. Real, but the next dry August might lose it.
  • Tarn. A proper mountain lake, year-round, twenty feet deep in the middle — a body of work patient hands have built up over years. Will not blow away in a single bad season.
  • Cirque. The full glacial bowl with three tarns and a string of feeder pools and the beck running out under the col — a lifework, a landscape, a watershed visible from the next fell.

Who walks up to the same bowl

The cartographer at the parish map

Forty years of the same parish map, redrawn each February in the back of the schoolhouse. The cathedral tarn at the head of the cirque has been on every edition since 1986. The young walker's drop in the upper bowl went on the 2026 edition this March.

The fell-walker at the morning tarn

Twenty-eight summers of the same walk to the same tarn — up the long ridge before breakfast, back by the col before the rain. The tarn is the same colour in July and in January. The walker is not the same. The tarn does not mind.

The shepherd at the high water

Forty summers of watering ewes at the upper pool below the cliff. The pool went dry once in 1976 and never quite the same since. The high tarn never has. The ewes know the way. The shepherd lets them go ahead.

The translator at the same desk

Two pages of the slow Italian every morning since 2012. A tarn now in the corner of the desk, fifteen summers deep, year-round, dark enough to lose a stone in. Will not blow away in a dry August. The hill keeps it.
