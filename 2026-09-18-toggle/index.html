toggle · noun · /ˈtɒɡ(ə)l/ — two rigid links joined by a central pin, or knee; a push on the knee toward the straight line of the links drives their far ends apart with a force that grows without limit as the links come straight, and, carried a hair past straight against a stop, holds itself shut under the load; from an old nautical toggle, a crosspin through an eye

Toggle

A joint that pushes hardest at the moment it has almost stopped moving.

Pin two stiff links end to end at a middle knee, fix one far end and let the other bear on the work, and you have a toggle. Push the knee sideways, toward the straight line the two links want to make, and the links straighten — driving their far ends apart. Because the push turns a wide angle into a shrinking one, a light shove on the knee becomes a heavy thrust at the ends, and the closer the links come to straight, the more the thrust runs away: at the dead centre, where they are in line, it is unbounded, and the travel it delivers has shrunk to nothing. Carry the knee a hair past straight against a stop and the trick turns to holding: now the load, pushing back, drives the knee harder into the stop instead of folding it — the joint locks itself shut with no catch. The knee angle is the one dial, and it sets the thrust, the travel, and whether the joint drives or merely holds.

Where the force goes

One knee, a runaway lever

A toggle is a lever whose ratio is not fixed but sweeps as it works. Read the two links as a shallow > lying against a straight line, each making an angle θ with it. A push P on the knee, square to that line, has to be carried away along the two links as compression — and geometry sets the trade. When the links are steep (a wide, open toggle) the push slides them apart easily and little is passed on. As they flatten toward straight, the same sideways push has to shove ever harder along their length to move the knee any nearer the line, and that long-ways force is what reaches the ends. The flatter the toggle, the more the push is multiplied.

A toggle trades travel for force along its whole stroke: it opens with a large, quick motion and little push, and closes with a vast push and almost no motion. All the force it will ever make is banked into the last hair of its travel.

That is why a toggle is the mechanism of the last squeeze — the final bite of a clamp, the coining blow of a press, the crush of a jaw on stone. It gives its enormous thrust exactly where a screw or a plain lever runs out of grip: at the end, against a load that has stopped yielding. And it gives the second thing a plain lever cannot — the power to hold. Take the driving push away with the knee just past dead centre, and the load, straining to open the joint, can only press the knee harder against its stop. The very geometry that made a small push into a huge thrust now makes the joint its own latch. Read the joint at rest and you find two links in pure compression, a knee pin carrying their thrust, and a stop taking a force the load can never overcome.

The one relation it embodies

Force against the angle

Let each link make an angle θ with the straight line through the two far pins, and let the push on the knee be P. Balance the knee, ideal and frictionless, and the whole of it is three short lines:

  • The thrust. The push is shared into compression along the two links and reappears at the ends as a thrust Q = P ⁄ (2·tan θ). So the mechanical advantage is Q ⁄ P = 1 ⁄ (2·tan θ) — one at θ ≈ 26.6°, and climbing without limit as θ → 0. Wider than that and the toggle is a mechanical disadvantage: it delivers less than it is given.
  • The link. Each link carries a compression S = P ⁄ (2·sin θ), and this is the force the knee pin passes and the link must not buckle under. It too runs away as the toggle straightens, a touch faster than the thrust, so the parts feel the closing long before the work does.
  • The travel. Force is never free. What the toggle gains in thrust it loses in motion: the ends advance only 2·tan θ for each step of the knee, so at the dead centre the thrust is unbounded and the delivered travel is nil. A toggle cannot both push hard and move far; the angle chooses which.

So the knee angle is the one dial, and it sweeps between two useless ends. Open it wide and the toggle idles — a lot of travel, no advantage, the thrust less than the push. Bring it toward straight and the advantage climbs, the travel shrinks, and somewhere in the middle is the working bite. Push it onto the dead centre and the mechanism takes charge: the thrust and the link force spike toward the parts' limits, a hair of extra push does great harm, and the joint, carried just over, holds itself with no hand on it at all. Between idle and overload is the angle a fitter sets — flat enough to bite, open enough to keep the links and pins inside their limits and the stroke under control.

The toggle's whole gift, and its whole danger, is the same fact: its advantage has no ceiling. It will make any force you ask of it, and break any part you forget to size for that force.

Four states, from the angle that bites to a part past its limit

From idle to overstressed

  1. Bites. A working angle: the links are flat enough to multiply the push into a real thrust, yet open enough that every part — the pins in shear, the links in buckling, the frame in tension — sits inside its limit and the stroke stays under the hand. This is the toggle the fitter sets: strong at the close, and safe.
  2. Slack. The toggle is too far open. The links stand steep, the advantage falls below one, and the joint delivers less force than the push it is given — all travel and no bite. Safe, and useless: a toggle worked here is only an awkward, force-losing link, not a multiplier. Bring the knee toward straight.
  3. Locked. The knee is on or over the dead centre. The advantage has run away — a light push makes a colossal thrust, the travel has all but vanished, and the joint holds itself shut against the load with no catch. Priceless as a latch; treacherous as a drive, because the parts are near their limits and the smallest extra push has nowhere to go but into breaking something.
  4. Overstressed. The thrust or the link force has carried a part past its limit — the knee pin shears, a link buckles, or the frame tears at its anchor. Which gives first depends on the mechanism, and no angle relieves it: opening the toggle only trades the overload for idleness. To save it you must ease the push, not the angle.

Three who read the same knee

The same toggle, three trades

The machinist

the clamp

Knows a toggle by its snap. Sets a hold-down clamp so the knee just crosses centre as the handle comes down, feels it go hard and then over — and trusts it to stay clamped with the handle let go, because past centre the work only jams it tighter. Never leaves the knee short of centre, where the clamp needs a hand to hold it, nor drives it far over, where the little links take the whole load and buckle. The bite is set by where the stop falls, not by how hard the arm pulls.

The pressman

the blow

Uses the toggle to coin: a flywheel swings the knee down through bottom dead centre and the ram delivers a thrust no screw of that size could, exactly at the bottom of the stroke where the work resists most. Times the die to meet the ram at the dead centre and no sooner, because a die set a hair high meets the runaway force and the frame, not the blank, takes the coining. Reads the tonnage off the angle, and never off the arm.

The engineer

the plate

Draws the thrust as P ⁄ (2·tan θ) and sees at once that it has no ceiling, so sizes not for the push but for the angle: the pins in double shear, the links against buckling, the frame in tension. In a stone crusher makes one link a sacrificial toggle plate, cut to give first, so a shard too hard folds a cheap plate instead of cracking the frame. Sets the closed angle to bite with a margin, and puts a stop where the knee must not pass.
