flitch · noun · /flɪtʃ/ — a slab of timber sawn the length of a log; and a steel plate bolted between two such timbers to make one stiffer, stronger beam — a flitch beam; Old English flicce, a side of bacon, a thing cut in a long slab

Flitch

Fasten two materials so they cannot bend apart, and they must bend together.

A timber beam is cheap, warm, and light, and it sags. A steel plate is stiff and strong and dear, and on its own it is a floppy thing on edge that wants to buckle. Bolt the plate between two timber leaves — a flitch beam — and something the trade has trusted for a century happens: the sandwich is forced to deflect as a single member, so the two materials share one curvature. From that one fact the whole beam follows. The load splits between wood and steel in proportion to their stiffness, not their strength; the timber gives the steel a fair face to work against and keeps it from buckling; and the pair carries more, and sags less, than the sum of the parts pretending to act alone. The only sizing dial is how thick a plate you slip between the leaves.

Where the load goes

Bonded together, they share one curvature

Bending a beam bends it to some radius R: the fibres above the middle stretch, the fibres below squash, and the strain at a height y off the neutral axis is simply y ⁄ R. Now fasten a steel plate flat between two timbers, tight enough that they cannot slide past each other. They can no longer take up their own separate radii — a bolt through all three forbids it — so they take up one radius together. That is the whole trick, and it is a geometric fact, not a material one.

Strength is shared by strength; but stiffness decides who carries the load. Two materials made to bend as one split the moment in exact proportion to how hard each resists bending.

Because the strain at a fibre is the same for both, the stress is not: stress is stiffness times strain, so at any height the steel carries E_s ⁄ E_w times the stress the wood carries there — call that ratio n, and for steel on softwood it is about twenty. The steel works twenty times as hard as the wood beside it, which is exactly why a thin plate does so much. Slice the plate thicker and you raise the pair's stiffness, so the same load bends it less and every fibre relaxes — but you do not change n. The ratio between the two stresses is set the instant you choose the two materials; the plate thickness only sets how far from their limits both of them run.

The one relation it embodies

Load splits by stiffness; one material reaches its limit first

Take two timber leaves and a steel plate, all of depth d, bent about the strong axis. Each material has a flexural rigidity E·I — its stiffness E times its second moment of area I — and because they share one curvature 1 ⁄ R, the applied moment M divides between them and the pair behaves as one member of rigidity ΣEI = E_w I_w + E_s I_s:

  • The share — the moment splits as the rigidities do, M_s ⁄ M_w = E_s I_s ⁄ E_w I_w, and the pair bends to 1 ⁄ R = M ⁄ ΣEI. Add plate and ΣEI climbs, so under a fixed load the curvature — and the deflection δ = W L³ ⁄ 48 ΣEI — falls.
  • The two stresses — at the extreme fibre y = d ⁄ 2, the wood carries σ_w = E_w (d ⁄ 2) ⁄ R and the steel σ_s = n·σ_w, with n = E_s ⁄ E_w. Thicken the plate and both fall together, in fixed ratio; the plate never changes which one is closer to its limit.
  • Who gives first — the wood reaches its bending strength f_w when σ_w = f_w; the steel yields when σ_s = f_y, i.e. when σ_w = f_y ⁄ n. So the timber governs if n·f_w < f_y and the steel governs if it does not — a property of the pairing, decided before a single bolt is driven.

So the plate thickness is the one dial, and it does two honest things at once: it raises the load the pair can carry and it cuts how far the pair sags. What it cannot do is change the character of the beam. A soft timber under a hard steel will always be the one that yields at the fibre; a strong timber under a mild steel will always feel the plate reach its limit first. You choose that when you choose the two slabs. After that, you are only choosing how much margin to buy.

Nothing here makes the wood stronger or the steel stronger. It only forces the two to keep the same curvature, and lets stiffness hand each of them exactly the share of the load it is fit to hold.

Four states, from a plate that earns its steel to one that never will

From balanced to overstressed

  1. Balanced. The thinnest plate that brings the timber, the steel, and the sag all inside their limits at once. The wood carries what wood is good at, the steel makes up the rest, and no slab is doing work another could do cheaper. This is the flitch the builder cuts — the beam earns every millimetre of plate in it.
  2. Over-plated. Safe, and safe with room to spare — the stresses and the sag sit well under every limit. But the plate is thicker than the load asked for, and steel is the heavy, dear part of the beam. It works; it is simply more beam than the span needed, and a good eye reads that as waste, not virtue.
  3. Sagging. Strong enough — nothing is anywhere near breaking — and yet the beam bends past what a floor or a lintel can live with, past the old serviceability line of a span in three-hundred-and-sixty. Doors bind, plaster cracks, the floor feels alive underfoot. The commonest reason a sound timber gets a flitch at all: not weakness, but bounce.
  4. Overstressed. The extreme fibre — of the wood, or of the steel, whichever the pairing hands the limit to — has run past what it holds. Pushed here the beam does not merely deflect; it breaks, in the material that gave first. The plate is too thin, or absent, or the load simply too great for this section to carry safely at all.

Three who read the same beam

The same sandwich, three trades

The builder

the bolts

Slips the plate between the leaves and knows the whole thing is a lie until the bolts are tight — that the sandwich only acts as one member if the fasteners carry the horizontal shear trying to slide the faces past each other. Staggers the bolts along the span where that shear runs highest, near the supports, snugs them, and drills the timber, not the steel, oversize for the seasonal move of the wood.

The carpenter

the sag

Cares less that the beam is strong than that it is quiet. Has felt a floor that passes every strength check still tremble when you cross it, and reaches for a flitch not to stop a break but to stop a bounce — a stiff plate to pull the deflection back under the line the eye and the plaster can accept. Sizes to the sag first and finds the strength comes free.

The engineer

the ratio

Reads the pair as one section through the modular ratio n: transforms the steel into n times its width in equivalent timber, works the homogeneous beam that results, then untransforms the steel's stress by multiplying back. Checks the fibre that governs, checks the deflection, checks the bolts against the shear flow VQ ⁄ I, and adds nothing the arithmetic did not ask for.
