windlass · noun · /ˈwɪndləs/ — a drum turned by a lever or crank to haul a load, held between heaves by a pawl in a ratchet; from Old Norse vindáss, winding-pole — for the pole you wind, and the little tooth that keeps what you wound

Windlass

You heave it round a stroke at a time, and the pawl keeps every notch you win — so a back-and-forth becomes a one-way climb.

A windlass is a drum with a load on a line, and a pawl — a small pivoted tooth — dropped into a ratchet wheel on its shaft. You haul the drum round a heave at a time; between heaves the load would run it straight back down, and the pawl catches the next notch and holds. That is the whole trick, and it is a deep one: the machine is a rectifier. It takes a motion that goes both ways — your arm, back and forth — and passes only the useful half, banking each heave and giving none of it back. But it banks in whole notches, and the scrap of a heave that falls short of the next notch is lost motion, handed back at the top of every stroke; and the pawl holds only so hard, so load it past the tooth's grip and it overhauls — the weight beats the pawl and runs the drum back down.

Where the one-way comes from

A tooth that passes one way and jams the other

Look at the rim of the ratchet: the teeth are not symmetrical. Each has a gentle ramp on one side and a steep face on the other. Turn the wheel the winding way and the pawl's tip rides up each ramp and drops off the tip into the next hollow — click — offering no resistance. Turn it the other way and the pawl's tip meets the steep face square-on and jams: it cannot climb a wall. One shape, two behaviours. The wheel is free one way and locked the other.

That asymmetry is the entire idea. A symmetric tooth would hold both ways or neither; the raked tooth is a valve for rotation — it lets motion through in one direction and stops it dead in the reverse.

So your heave and the load's pull, which are opposite, meet the tooth differently. The heave rides the ramps and turns the drum; the load meets the faces and is held. You put work in on the forward stroke, let go, and the pawl banks it. Come back for another heave and it banks again, onto the first. The drum climbs, notch upon notch, and never — while the pawl holds — gives a notch back. A rower's oar, a saw, a file, an arm on a crank: all of them go both ways, and the ratchet keeps only the way that pays.

And here is the catch, in the same tooth. The pawl banks whole notches only. If your heave carries the wheel two notches and a bit, the two notches are kept and the bit is not — the pawl has not yet reached the third tooth, so at the top of the stroke that fraction slides back to the last notch it holds. That returned fraction is lost motion. A coarse tooth, with big notches, can lose almost a whole notch of heave each time; a fine tooth loses next to nothing — but a fine tooth is a small tooth, and a small tooth lets go under far less load.

The one relation it embodies

Bank the whole notches; hold to the tooth

Take the stroke you swing (an angle), the tooth's pitch p = 360 ⁄ N for a wheel of N notches, and the back-load L the weight puts on the drum. Idealise the ratchet as rigid and the whole of it is two lines:

  • It banks whole notches. banked = p · ⌊stroke ⁄ p⌋. A heave advances the drum by as many whole notches as it covers; the remainder, stroke − banked, is lost motion, given back when you reach the top and the load settles onto the last held tooth. Swing shorter than one notch and you bank nothing at all.
  • It holds to the tooth. The pawl holds the load so long as L ≤ H, the tooth's capacity. A steeper, coarser tooth has a larger H; a fine tooth a smaller one. This is the price of the fine resolution: what wastes little, holds little.
  • Past the tooth, it overhauls. L > H and the pawl can no longer hold the face: the weight overhauls it and drives the drum backward, and the height you banked runs back down. The rectifier has failed — the reverse half of the motion is getting through.

The two limits pull against each other, and the cut of the tooth is where you settle them. Cut it fine and you keep almost every degree you swing, but you dare not hang much on it. Cut it steep and you can hold a great weight, but you throw away a slice of every heave. There is no tooth that wastes nothing and holds everything — the ratchet makes you choose.

A ratchet is a one-way valve for motion: it banks the forward half and jams the reverse. Everything good about it and everything that fails is in that one raked tooth — the resolution you keep, the load it holds, and the wall past which it lets go.

Four states, from a clean bank to a runaway drum

Along the one shaft

  1. Banking. A good heave on a tooth that holds. The stroke covers whole notches, the pawl catches, and the weight sits well inside the tooth's grip — every notch kept, the drum climbing and never slipping. The plain, happy case the windlass runs in.
  2. Short of a notch. The heave is real but too small — it swings less than one notch, so the pawl never reaches the next tooth and the whole stroke is lost motion, given back. You work and the drum does not climb. Lengthen the heave, or cut a finer tooth.
  3. At the edge. The weight has crept up near the tooth's capacity. The pawl still holds, but only just; a little more load, or a knock, and it lets go. The cliff's edge, where a wise hand eases off or cuts a steeper tooth before hanging any more.
  4. Overhauling. The weight beats the pawl. The steep face shears past the tip, the drum runs backward, and the banked height pours back down — the rectifier failing, the reverse half of the motion getting through. Ease the load under the hold, or the whole climb is undone.

Who leans on the one-way tooth

The same pawl, three trades

The hoist-hand

wants to rest without losing height

Hauls a load up a heave at a time and trusts the pawl to hold between strokes — so a lift too heavy to raise in one pull is raised in fifty, and never slips back while hands are moved. Cuts the tooth steep enough to hold the load, and lives with the lost motion.

The clockmaker

wants the wind kept, not the run

Puts a click — a tiny pawl — on the winding arbor, so the key winds the mainspring tight and cannot let it whirl back through your fingers. The same one-way tooth, sized to a spring instead of a ship's anchor; the whole of a wound clock rests on it.

The physicist

wants to know what a ratchet cannot do

Asks whether a tiny ratchet could bank the random jiggle of heat into steady work, and finds it cannot: warm the pawl and it bounces off its own tooth as often as it catches. A rectifier needs a real bias, not just a preferred shape — the deepest lesson the tooth teaches.

The discipline in the word

Keep the half that pays

The windlass is a lesson in how to make progress out of a motion that goes both ways. Your arm cannot only push; it must return to push again, and on its own that return would give back everything the push won. The pawl is the discipline that keeps the push and refuses the return — and the whole climb, however great, is only ever the sum of small kept heaves. But it keeps in whole notches, so the scraps are lost; and it keeps only to the strength of one tooth, so a load past that undoes the lot. The craft is to size the tooth to the work: fine enough to waste little, steep enough to hold what you hang, and never to trust a climb to a pawl you have loaded to its edge. To ratchet is to decide, in the cut of one tooth, exactly how much you will keep and exactly how much you can hold.
