freewheel · noun · /ˈfriːwiːl/ — a hub that drives its wheel when turned forward and runs on freely when the drive stops, on spring pawls riding a ratchet ring; the mechanism that lets a bicycle coast

Freewheel

It drives when you pedal and slips free when you stop — the same one-way tooth, made into a clutch.

A freewheel is a hub carrying a ring of ratchet teeth, with spring-loaded pawls that ride on it. Drive the ring forward and the pawls catch a tooth and carry the wheel round with you; stop driving, and the ring runs on ahead of the pawls, which skate over the teeth — click, click — offering nothing. That is the coast. It is the same rectifier as any ratchet, but read as a clutch: it engages the forward half of your effort and lets the reverse — the wheel outrunning the crank — slip clean away. Two things bound it. Between the teeth is free play: a push shorter than one tooth's pitch catches nothing, and that pitch is the engagement lag you feel before the drive bites. And the pawls carry only so hard, so a grade past their strength makes them skip — shearing over the teeth, the drive lurching and slipping.

Where the coast comes from

Pawls that catch forward and skate back

The hub ring has the same raked teeth as any ratchet — a gentle ramp one way, a steep face the other — but here it is the relative motion of ring and pawl that matters. Drive the crank faster than the wheel is turning and the pawls' tips meet the steep faces and catch: the crank carries the wheel. Let the wheel outrun the crank — freewheeling downhill, or simply coasting — and the teeth stream past the pawls the easy way, lifting them over each tip. click. The wheel is coupled to your legs one way and free of them the other.

That is why you can stop pedalling and keep rolling, and start again and instantly bite. The freewheel is a valve for drive: forward effort passes through, the coast slips past it.

And the lag lives in the same teeth. The pawls only catch at a tooth, so when you begin to drive after a coast, the crank must first take up the free play — turn until a pawl meets the next face — before anything happens at the wheel. On a ring of few big teeth that dead spot is long: you feel the crank move and the bike not. On a ring of many small teeth it is almost nothing: the drive bites the instant you push. Riders count this as a ring's engagement points, and pay for more of them — because the same fineness that kills the lag makes each tooth, and each pawl's bite on it, smaller.

Which brings the second limit, in that smaller bite. A pawl carries the drive only so long as the load on it stays under what the tooth-face can bear. Stamp on the pedals from a standstill, or wear the ring down, and the load beats the pawls: they skip, shearing up and over the teeth instead of holding, and the drive slips with a sickening lurch. A coarse ring, with big teeth, carries a hard stamp; a fine ring skips sooner. The pickup you gain, you pay for in the load you can put through.

The one relation it embodies

Engage in teeth; carry to the pawls

Take the push you drive (an angle at the hub), the ring's pitch p = 360 ⁄ N for N teeth, and the load L the grade puts on the drive. Idealise the ratchet as rigid:

  • It engages in whole teeth. driven = p · ⌊push ⁄ p⌋. A push carries the wheel by as many whole teeth as it covers; the remainder is free play. The pitch p is the engagement lag — the crank you turn before a pawl catches. Push shorter than one tooth and nothing is driven.
  • It carries to the pawls. The pawls carry the drive while L ≤ H, their capacity. A steeper, coarser ring has a larger H; a fine ring a smaller one — the price of quick pickup.
  • Past the pawls, it skips. L > H and the pawls shear over the teeth: the drive slips. The rectifier lets go — your effort no longer reaches the wheel, and it lurches.

The two limits pull opposite ways, and the cut of the ring is where you settle them. Fine gives instant pickup but skips under a hard stamp. Coarse takes a big stamp but wastes a long dead spot before it bites. There is no ring that picks up at once and never skips — the freewheel makes you choose your compromise, and a rider chooses it by the riding.

A freewheel is a one-way clutch: it engages the drive and frees the coast. The pickup you feel and the skip you dread are the two faces of one tooth — how soon it bites, and how hard it can bite before it lets go.

Four states, from a clean drive to a slipping ring

Along the one hub

  1. Driving. A good push on a ring that carries. The push covers whole teeth, the pawls catch and drive the wheel, the grade sits well within their carry, and when you stop it coasts clean. The plain, happy case a freewheel lives in for miles.
  2. Short of a tooth. You turn the crank but less than one tooth's pitch — inside the free play — so no pawl reaches the next face and the wheel does not move. The dead spot after a coast, felt worst on a coarse ring; push through it, or fit a finer ring.
  3. At the edge. The grade has crept up near the pawls' carry. They still hold, but only just; a harder stamp, a worn tooth, and they let go. The ridge before a slip, where a rider eases the effort or drops to an easier gear.
  4. Skipping. The load beats the pawls. They shear over the teeth, the drive slips, and your effort pours into a lurch instead of the wheel — the clutch failing under a stamp it cannot carry. Ease off, or fit a ring cut to take it.

Who leans on the one-way clutch

The same pawl, three trades

The track racer

wants the drive, hates the lag

Fits the ring with the most engagement points they can, so the pedals bite the instant they stamp out of a corner — and accepts a ring that must be kept fresh, because the same fine teeth that kill the dead spot are the first to skip under a full sprint.

The tourer

wants a ring that never lets go

Runs a coarse, deep ring that will carry a loaded bike up a wall of a hill and never skip, and lives happily with the longer pickup — a moment's dead crank after a coast is nothing set against a drive that holds under everything they can put through it.

The millwright

wants a shaft that drives one way only

Uses the same one-way clutch as an overrunning coupling — a starter that drops away once the engine runs faster than it, a conveyor that cannot be driven backward. The bicycle's coast, put to work wherever a shaft must take drive from one side and refuse it from the other.

The discipline in the word

Take the drive, free the coast

The freewheel is the ratchet's gentler lesson: not only to keep the half that pays, but to let the other half go clean. A fixed wheel drags your legs round on every descent; the freewheel lets the wheel run ahead and asks nothing of you until you ask something of it. That is the grace of a one-way clutch — it couples when there is work to pass and uncouples the instant there is not. But the coupling is granular, so there is always a moment of free play before it bites, and it is only as strong as one pawl on one tooth, so a load past that shears it loose. The craft is to cut the ring to the riding: fine for the racer who cannot spare the lag, coarse for the tourer who cannot risk the skip, and to know that the quickness and the strength are bought from the same purse. To freewheel is to choose, in the pitch of one tooth, how soon your drive bites and how hard it can bite before it slips.
