Turnstile

One through a push, and no way back — the ratchet's whole job made plain: a crowd turned into a count.

A turnstile is a ratchet you walk through. Its rotor carries a ring of stops, and a pawl drops behind each, so the arm turns one way and jams the other: push it forward and it lets you — and only you — through, a person a push; push it back and it will not budge. Everything a ratchet is, is here with nothing else attached — no load to lift, no wheel to drive, only the one-way count itself. It takes a crowd, which mills both ways, and yields a tally, which only climbs. Two things bound it. Between the stops is slack: a shove short of the next stop passes no one and the arm springs back, and the spacing of the stops is how fine the count is. And the pawl bars the return only so hard, so lean on it enough — a press of bodies, a barge, someone forcing an exit — and the stop is overpowered and the tally itself is driven backward.

Where the count comes from

A gate that opens one way and stands the other

The rotor's stops are the same raked teeth as any ratchet — an easy way and a jammed way. Come at the arm from the front and your push turns the rotor, the pawl riding up its stop and dropping behind the next: you pass, click, and one is added to the count. Come at it from behind, or try to back out the way you came, and the pawl meets the steep face of the stop and holds: the arm is a wall. The gate is open to the crowd going one way and shut to it going the other, and that asymmetry is the whole machine.

A turnstile does not measure how many people there are; it measures how many have passed. It converts a milling, reversible crowd into an irreversible number — the one thing a ratchet is for, with the lifting and the driving stripped away.

And it counts in whole stops. A stop passed is a person through; a shove that does not reach the next stop passes no one, and the sprung arm returns to where it was. So the spacing of the stops is the grain of the count: a rotor of few, wide-spaced stops needs a full committed push to admit one and can miss a half-hearted one; a rotor of many close stops registers the smallest advance and is ready at once. It is the same trade every ratchet makes — resolution against the size, and so the strength, of each tooth.

Because the strength is the second limit. The pawl bars the return only up to the force its stop can bear. A single person leaning is nothing; a crowd pressed against a locked turnstile, or a determined barge, is not. Load the stop past what it holds and it is forced — the pawl shears over, the rotor turns the wrong way, and the count that should only ever rise is driven down. A rectifier is only as one-way as its jammed side is strong, and a turnstile is only as honest as its pawl.

The one relation it embodies

Admit in stops; bar to the pawl

Take the push a person gives the arm (an angle), the rotor's spacing p = 360 ⁄ N for N stops, and the back-push L pressing the stop the wrong way. Idealise the ratchet as rigid:

  • It admits in whole stops. admitted = p · ⌊push ⁄ p⌋. A push turns the rotor by as many whole stops as it covers — that many through; the remainder is slack, sprung back. Push shorter than one stop's spacing and no one passes at all.
  • It bars to the pawl. The pawl bars the return while L ≤ H, the stop's strength. A steeper, coarser rotor has a larger H; a fine one a smaller one — the price of a ready, fine-grained count.
  • Past the pawl, it is forced. L > H and the stop is overpowered: the rotor turns backward and the count is driven down. The one-way gate has failed — the crowd is getting through the wrong way.

The two limits pull against each other, and the cut of the rotor settles them. Fine gives a ready count that catches every step, but each small stop is barged by less. Coarse stands against a hard press, but needs a full push for each one and misses the timid. No rotor is both perfectly ready and perfectly firm — the turnstile makes you choose how fine a count you want and how hard a crowd it must stand.

A turnstile is rectification with nothing else attached: it passes the crowd one way and bars it the other, and a count that only climbs is the proof. Its readiness and its firmness are the two faces of one stop — how small an advance it will register, and how hard a shove it will refuse.

Four states, from a clean count to a forced gate

Along the one post

  1. Counting. A committed push on a rotor that holds. The push passes whole stops, the pawl bars the return, the back-push sits well within the stop's strength, and each person is tallied and cannot be un-tallied. The plain, honest case a turnstile keeps all day.
  2. Short of a stop. A shove that does not reach the next stop — inside the slack — so the pawl never catches, the arm springs back, and no one is admitted though someone pushed. The half-hearted attempt a coarse rotor misses; push in full, or fit a finer rotor that catches the smallest step.
  3. At the edge. The back-push has crept up near the stop's strength. The pawl still bars the return, but only just; a harder press, a heavier barge, and it gives. The moment before a crowd defeats the gate, where a steeper rotor — or fewer bodies against it — keeps the count honest.
  4. Forced. The back-push overpowers the stop. The pawl shears over, the rotor turns the wrong way, and the count that should only climb is driven back — the one-way gate beaten, the tally no longer true. Ease the press under the hold, or fit a rotor cut to stand it.

Who leans on the one-way count

The same pawl, three trades

The gatekeeper

wants an honest headcount

Sets a turnstile at the mouth of the ground so the count on the meter is exactly the count that passed — one a push, no way back, no double-tallies. Cuts the rotor strong enough that a match-day crush cannot barge it and drive the number false.

The meter-maker

wants motion made into a number

Builds the same one-way tooth into a gas meter, an odometer, a tally counter: a train of ratchets that lets the register climb with every turn and never run back, so the reading is the running sum of everything that has passed and forgets nothing.

The archivist of record

wants a ledger that cannot be un-written

Wants what a turnstile wants — an entry that, once made, stands: a stamped serial, a sequence that only increments, an append-only log. The turnstile is the mechanical ancestor of every count that may be added to and never quietly rolled back.

The discipline in the word

A number that only goes up

The turnstile is the ratchet reduced to its idea. Strip away the drum and the hub, the load and the drive, and what is left is the bare act of rectification: taking something that goes both ways and keeping a record that goes only one. That record — the count — is the point. It is how a ground knows its crowd, a meter its throughput, a ledger its history: not by holding the present, but by refusing to forget the past, adding each event and un-adding none. But the record is granular, so it can miss what does not push past a stop; and it is only as trustworthy as its pawl, so a force past that strength can drive it false. The discipline is to cut the gate to the crowd — fine enough to catch every true passage, strong enough that no press can barge it — and to remember that a count is only ever as honest as the one-way tooth that keeps it. To turn a stile is to insist that some things, once passed, are passed for good.
