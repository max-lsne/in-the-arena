# Vernier

**By eye you cannot read past the marks on a ruler — the space between them is
a guess. So lay a second, shorter scale alongside the first, and cut it so that
ten of its divisions fill the space of nine of the main ones. Now each little
division falls short of a big one by exactly a tenth of a big division. Slide
the little scale along and its marks drift out of step with the big marks, then
one by one fall back into step; at any position exactly one little mark stands
square against a big mark while its neighbours miss, above and below, by a
growing tenth. Count which mark it is and you have the tenths — the fraction the
eye could never split, turned into a plain question of *which line is which*.
This is the vernier, set down by Pierre Vernier in 1631, and its rule is exact
and asks no arithmetic of the hand: the smallest step it can read — the least
count — is one main division divided by the number of divisions on the vernier.
Ten divisions read tenths; fifty read fiftieths. You buy the resolution with
nothing but a second row of ticks.**

The word *vernier* is the instrument-maker's, worn smooth from a name: the short
sliding scale on a caliper, a sextant, a barometer, a theodolite — any place a
coarse scale must be read finer than its marks allow. To *read the vernier* is to
stop guessing the fraction and go looking for the one line that agrees.

## The one shape

Two rhythms, nearly the same, beat slowly against each other, and the beat is
made large enough to read. The main scale steps by its division; the vernier
steps by a shade less, because N of its divisions were packed into N−1 of the
main ones. So each vernier mark lags its neighbour by one part in N more than the
last, and the lag climbs like a staircase — one least count per mark — until it
comes all the way round to a whole division and the scales fall back into line.

- The **least count** is D/N: the main division over the number of vernier
  divisions. It is the smallest gap the instrument resolves, and the doubt in an
  honest reading is half of it.
- The **reading** is a sum with no interpolation in it. The main scale gives the
  whole and the coarse part — the last mark the vernier's zero has passed. The
  vernier gives the fine part — the number of the mark that lines up, times the
  least count. Add them.
- The disagreement between the scales **crawls N times slower** than the slide,
  so a shift too small to see in the jaws becomes a coincidence that marches a
  full mark down the vernier. The instrument is a lever made of counting: it
  magnifies a fraction into an alignment you can point at.

The bargain is that resolution is bought with range, and read for free. More
vernier divisions mean a finer least count — but a longer scale whose true
coincidence is crowded by near-misses, harder to tell apart. You trade the ease
of the reading for its fineness, tick for tick; there is no free precision, only
precision moved from the length of your attention onto the length of the scale.

The page is one caliper, read the way the eye reads it: open the jaws to a size,
and the vernier slides with them while the bench lights the one mark that comes
square against the main scale. Set the **jaw opening** and the number of
**vernier divisions**, and it reads back the whole, the coinciding mark, the
least count, the doubt, and the magnification — lighting the coincidence green
and showing, at fifty divisions, exactly how close the neighbours crowd. The
numbers are the real ones: the least count is D/N, the reading is the passed main
mark plus the coinciding mark times the least count, and the coincidence walks N
times faster than the jaws, after Vernier.
