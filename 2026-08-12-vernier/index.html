vernier · noun · /ˈvɜːnɪə/ — a short second scale laid alongside the first, its divisions a fixed hair shorter, so the one mark that lines up reads the fraction between the main marks

Vernier

By eye you cannot read past the marks on a ruler. Lay a second scale beside it whose divisions are a hair too close together, and one pair of marks will line up. Which pair is the fraction you could not split.

Put ten divisions of the little scale into the space of nine divisions of the big one. Now every little division is short of a big one by exactly a tenth of a big division. Slide the little scale along and its marks drift out of step with the big marks and then, one by one, back into step — and at any position exactly one of its marks stands square against a big mark while all the others miss, above and below, by a growing tenth. Count which mark it is and you have the tenths: the little scale has turned a fraction too fine for the eye into a plain question of which line is which. This is the vernier, after Pierre Vernier, who set it down in 1631, and its rule is exact and needs no arithmetic in the hand: the smallest step it can read — its least count — is one main division divided by the number of divisions on the vernier. Ten divisions read tenths; fifty read fiftieths. You buy the resolution with nothing but a second row of ticks.

Where the resolution comes from

Two rhythms, nearly the same, beat slowly against each other

Set the two scales side by side with their zeros together. The main scale steps by its division D; the vernier steps by a shade less, D minus one part in N, because N vernier divisions were packed into N−1 main ones. So each vernier mark falls a little further behind its neighbouring main mark than the one before — one part in N further, then two, then three. The gaps between the pairs are a staircase, rising by the same small step D/N at every mark. That step is the whole instrument. Somewhere along the row the accumulated shortfall comes all the way round to a full main division and the two scales fall back into line; that mark reads zero, and the count restarts.

So the mark that coincides is not luck. It names the number of least-counts by which the vernier's zero sits past the last main mark — reading the fraction off a row of clean yes-or-no alignments instead of guessing a length by eye.

It is the same trick as two musical notes a beat apart, or two gratings laid at a whisker of an angle throwing a broad moiré band, or a strobe that freezes a spinning wheel: two rhythms almost but not quite matched, and their slow disagreement made large and legible. The disagreement here crawls along the scale N times slower than you slide — so a shift too small to see in the jaws becomes a coincidence that marches a full mark down the vernier. The instrument is a lever made of counting.

The one bargain

Resolution is bought with range, and read without arithmetic

The bargain is plain once you see it. Give the vernier more divisions and each one is shorter, the least count D/N smaller, the reading finer — fifty divisions split a millimetre into fiftieths. But the vernier is now longer and the coincidences harder to tell apart, because the neighbours of the true one miss by only a fiftieth and the eye must judge which of three near-aligned marks is squarest. You trade the ease of the reading for its fineness, tick for tick. There is no free precision; there is only precision moved from the length of your attention onto the length of the scale.

And the reading itself asks nothing of you but to look. The main scale gives the whole and the coarse part — the last mark the vernier's zero has passed. The vernier gives the fine part — the number of the mark that lines up, times the least count. Add them. No interpolation, no thou'-splitting by eye, no arithmetic beyond a sum: the instrument has already done the division by turning a fraction into a coincidence you can point at.

The cost hides in the same place the gift does. A worn or canted vernier makes several marks look nearly square at once and the reading grows a doubt of half a least count either way — which is exactly why the finer scale, whose neighbours crowd closest, is the one that punishes a careless eye most. Read the coincidence honestly and the vernier is good to half its least count. Wish a coincidence into being and it will oblige you with a wrong one.

The bench

One caliper, read the way the eye reads it

Open the jaws to a size. The vernier slides with them, and the bench marks the one line that comes square against the main scale — read the whole off the main scale, the fraction off that mark, and add. Change how many divisions the vernier carries and watch the least count, and the doubt, shrink and sharpen. The numbers are the real ones: least count is D/N, the reading is the main mark the vernier zero has passed plus the coinciding mark times the least count, and the coincidence walks N times faster than the jaws.

The vernier is a small confession about measurement: the eye cannot divide a mark, but it can tell, with perfect confidence, which of two lines is straighter against a third. Vernier's whole invention was to hand the eye only the question it can answer, and to arrange the geometry so that answer is the fraction. Every dial caliper, every micrometer's thimble, every sextant's drum keeps the same bargain: turn a hard judgment of length into an easy judgment of alignment, and let a second scale, a hair out of step, do the dividing.
