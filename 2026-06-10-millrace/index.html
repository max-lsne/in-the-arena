A millrace is the channeled length of water that brings the stream to the mill wheel. It is not the river. It is not the wheel. It is the small straight cut between them, half a mile of dressed stone and patient slope. The river runs anywhere. The water in the race is already chosen — gathered above the mill, dropped one inch in twenty, delivered exactly to the paddle. That is the whole thing.

The river runs anywhere
and turns no wheel.

Most days the water is in the river. The river is wide and quick and goes wherever the country slopes. It powers nothing. It only goes. There is plenty of water and none of it is doing anything. A field of attention with no channel cut through it.

A millrace is a small straight cut. Half a mile of dressed stone with a patient slope. It takes very little water out of the river — a thread, no more — and that thread, on its own, turns the wheel. The work is not in finding more water. The work is in cutting the channel and keeping it clear.

How it works

  1. 01

    Find the spring

    The spring is the wide unfocused water of the day — what you have before you have chosen. Time, attention, any patience left over from yesterday. The spring does not know what it is for. That is the spring's job: to be plenty, to be unsorted.

  2. 02

    Cut the race

    The race is the small straight channel. A piece of work, named and ready, with a stone wall on each side. Half a mile is plenty. The race is the part you cut once and then keep clear — not the part you rebuild each morning.

  3. 03

    Open the sluice

    The sluice is the gate at the head. You raise it and the water in the head falls onto the wheel, paddle by paddle. The wheel begins to turn. Nothing dramatic. The race is full, and the work is being done at the speed of the slope.

  4. 04

    Let the tailrace go

    The water that has turned the wheel goes back to the river. The day's work, done, flows out under the mill and disappears downstream. Tomorrow the spring will be there again, and the race, and the wheel, and the same patient inch of slope.

The four cuts

  • Trickle. The smallest cut — a finger's width of water, enough for a quern. The daily note, the half-hour walk, the small letter. Easy to dig. Easy to forget you dug it.
  • Leat. A short upland channel of bare stone — a chapter to be drafted, an instrument practised, a garden corner cleared. Runs straight and quiet without fuss.
  • Race. The full dressed-stone millrace — a year-long work, a novel, a slow recovery, a course finished evening by evening. Cut once and kept clear.
  • Penstock. The high-pressure pipe down the mountain — the heaviest, slowest, longest cuts. The decade-long works. The pressure is the same patient inch of slope, multiplied by many miles.

Who cuts a millrace

The mathematician at the long proof

Four hundred pages of scratch in a buckram notebook, six years in. An hour before dawn, before the coffee, before the post. One lemma a fortnight. The spring is wide; the race is half a mile and runs straight; the wheel turns. By the autumn the proof will have a draft.

The luthier in the back of the shop

One belly carved a month. One back. Two months of varnish coats laid down in single thin strokes after closing. The river is wide — orders, repairs, the till. The race is the small straight cut for the new instruments. The wheel does not care which day.

The doctor reading evening papers

Forty minutes after the children are asleep. One paper a night, summarised in two sentences on an index card. The pile of cards has grown a finger's width thicker each month. No single evening has done much. Every evening has done the same small thing.

The composer at the long quartet

Sixteen bars a week, drafted in pencil at the kitchen table. The spring is wide — teaching, students, the weekly broadcast. The race is sixteen bars. The penstock at the back of it all is the symphony, fifteen years out, the head not yet half full.
