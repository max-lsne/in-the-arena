Envelope

Many straight lines, and the one curve they all agree to wrap.

Draw a line, then another a little turned and slid from it, then another — each dead straight, yet the lines all lean against one curve they touch and never cross. That curve is their envelope. Lean a ladder in every position with its feet on two walls and the ladder-lines wrap a four-pointed astroid. Or mark the numbers round a circle and join each to its double — the old times-table trick — and the chords wrap a cardioid; join each to its triple and they wrap a nephroid. These are the same curves light folds onto in the cup next door, and the same a wheel on a wheel draws — reached a third way, not by a rolling point nor by reflected light but by a family of lines. And the envelope is found the honest way: the place each line meets the next.

The bench

A family of lines, and the curve they lean on

The steel lines are the family — a ladder caught in many positions, or the chords of a times-table laid round a circle. Each is straight; none is bent. But where one line lies close to the next they cross, and those crossings crowd along a copper curve that the whole family leans against and no single line steps over. That is the envelope. Like the caustic, it is not drawn from an equation — it is read off the lines, as the point where each one meets its neighbour, which is the same thing as the curve each line is tangent to.

Why straight lines make a curve

A curve with no points of its own, only tangents

  • A family of lines is a rule that gives one line for each value of a parameter — a ladder for each angle, a chord for each point of the circle. As the parameter advances the line turns and slides, and two lines an instant apart cross somewhere close by.
  • Follow that crossing as the two lines draw together: it settles onto a definite point, and the line at that instant is tangent there to the curve those crossings trace. That curve is the envelope — touched by every line of the family, crossed by none. A curve defined entirely by its tangents, never plotted point by point.
  • So it is found with no formula: take two neighbouring lines, mark where they meet, and sweep the parameter round. This page does exactly that — the copper curve is a record of adjacent lines crossing, the same method the cup uses on its reflected rays.
  • The ladder — a segment of fixed length with its ends on the two axes — wraps an astroid, the four-cusped hypocycloid. The times-table of chords — each circle point joined to its m-th multiple — wraps an epicycloid of m−1 cusps: a cardioid at two, a nephroid at three. The same curves the rolling wheel draws and the mirror folds its light onto, arrived at from straight lines alone.
  • Make the multiplier not whole and the chords never return to a line they have drawn: the envelope will not close, and each near-repeat lands rotated, the whole figure precessing — the same drifting a rolling ratio shows when it slips off a whole number.

Three ways to the one curve: roll a point along it, fold light onto it, or lay down the lines it leans on. The curve does not care which — it is only the agreement a family keeps at its edge.
