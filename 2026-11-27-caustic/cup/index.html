Caustic · cup · envelope · 2026-11-27

caustic · noun · the bright curve or surface onto which a family of light rays is folded after reflection or refraction — the envelope of the rays, where they crowd together and burn

Cup

Light thrown into a round mirror, and the bright curve it folds itself onto.

Throw a sheet of parallel rays into a circular mirror and each one bounces off the inside as a chord. The chords cross everywhere — but along one curve they crowd together and none of them cross it: that bright curve is the caustic, the cusped glow you can see burning across the bottom of a coffee cup in the sun. For parallel light it is a nephroid — two cusps, and the very same curve a wheel rolled on a wheel draws. Swap the sheet for a point source and slide it inward: far away the caustic is still the nephroid, and the instant the source touches the rim it sharpens into a cardioid, a single cusp. Nothing here is drawn from a formula — the caustic is found as the envelope of the rays, the place each chord meets the next. Set the source, aim it, and watch the cup fold its light.

The bench

One mirror, and the light it crowds

The gold circle is the mirror, held still. Light arrives — either as a sheet of parallel rays aimed across it, or from a single bright source you can move — and each ray reflects off the inside as a steel chord, running from where it lands to where it strikes the far wall. Draw enough of them and you see the trick of it: the chords pile up along a copper curve and leave it clean on the bright side. That curve is the caustic. It is not laid down from an equation — it is read off the light, as the point where each reflected chord crosses its neighbour, which is exactly what an envelope is.

Why the light crowds onto a curve

A curve the rays all touch and none of them cross

  • Each reflected ray is a chord of the mirror: it leaves the point it lands on, obeying angle in equals angle out about the radius, and runs straight to the far wall. The light as a whole is therefore a family of lines — one chord for every point the incoming light touches.
  • A family of lines, if the lines turn as they slide, has an envelope: a curve that every line of the family just touches, and that none of them crosses. The light pools along it because near the envelope two neighbouring rays meet almost at once — the rays bunch, and bunched light is bright. That bright curve is the caustic.
  • So the caustic can be found with no formula at all: take two reflected rays a hair apart and mark where they cross; do it all the way round and the crossing points trace the curve. That is exactly how this page draws it — the copper curve is a record of neighbouring chords meeting, not a plotted equation.
  • For a circle the answer has a name. Parallel light folds onto a nephroid — two cusps, half the mirror's width — the caustic you see in a sunlit cup. A point source on the rim folds onto a cardioid — one cusp. Both are epicycloids: the same curves a wheel rolled on a wheel traces on the next page over, arrived at from the opposite direction — there drawn by a rolling point, here by crossing light.
  • Slide the source from the rim outward and the cardioid's one cusp opens, the curve swelling through the whole Cayley family of caustics until, with the source infinitely far — parallel light — it settles on the nephroid's two. The mirror never changes; only how far the light starts from sets which caustic it will burn.

The bright curve is not something the light lands on. It is the shape of the light agreeing with itself — the one place every ray, for an instant, is going the same way.
