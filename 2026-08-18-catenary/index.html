In the arena · 2026-08-18

catenary · noun · /kəˈtiːnəri/ — the curve a free, uniform chain or cable settles into when it hangs under its own weight between two points; from Latin catēna, a chain. The word was minted by Huygens; before it, people called the shape the chainette or, wrongly, the parabola

Catenary

Hang a chain from two points and let it settle. It does not fall into just any droop — it takes one exact curve, the same every time, set by nothing but its own weight and where you pinned the ends. That curve has a name and a formula, and turned upside down it becomes the strongest way there is to bridge a gap in stone.

The reason there is only one shape is that the chain has no choice. Each link hangs from the ones beside it, and each must carry the full weight of every link below it, so the chain is steepest where it holds the most — at the ends, where the whole load pulls through — and flattest at the bottom, where it carries only itself. Follow that rule link by link and the curve it forces is not the parabola it resembles. Galileo, looking at a hanging chain, called it a parabola and was wrong; it took most of a century, and then the young Huygens, Leibniz, and Johann Bernoulli in 1691, to show the true curve is built from the exponential — y = a·cosh(x/a), the hyperbolic cosine. This is the catenary, and its one secret, which Hooke saw first, is that a chain frozen and flipped over stands as an arch that carries itself in pure compression.

Where the curve comes from

Each link carries everything below it

Take the chain at its lowest point and look at the piece from there out to some point further along. Three forces hold that piece still. At the low point the rest of the chain pulls it horizontally, with a tension call H — and because the low point is level, that pull is purely sideways. At the far end the chain above pulls it along the curve, at whatever slope the curve has there. And straight down hangs the weight of the piece itself, which is just its length times how heavy the chain is per unit length.

Balance those and something clean falls out. The horizontal pull H never changes along the whole chain — there is nothing sideways to alter it — so the horizontal tension is constant end to end. The vertical part of the tension, meanwhile, has to grow to match the weight hung below it, and the weight below grows with the length of chain, not with how far across you have gone. Written as a slope, that is

dy/dx = (weight of chain so far) / H,

and differentiating once more — because a little more arc adds a little more weight — turns it into the equation whose answer is the hyperbolic cosine. The chain settles to y = a·cosh(x/a), where the single constant a = H / w is the horizontal tension divided by the weight per length: a taut, lightly-loaded chain has a large a and hangs shallow; a slack or heavy one has a small a and plunges. Every catenary in the world is this one curve, scaled.

The curve it is not

A chain is not a parabola — a loaded cable is

The catenary looks so like a parabola that Galileo mistook one for the other, and near the bottom they very nearly agree. But they part at the ends, and the difference is not a flaw — it is a fact about what is doing the loading. A bare chain is loaded by its own length: the deeper and longer the droop, the more weight there is, spread evenly along the curve. That even-along-the-arc loading gives the cosh.

Load the same cable by its horizontal span instead — hang a heavy, level roadway from it, so every metre across carries equal weight and the cable's own weight barely counts — and the curve that results is an exact parabola.

That is the difference between a power line, which droops in a true catenary because it carries only itself, and a suspension bridge, whose main cables pull into a parabola because what they really hold is the flat deck below. The bench draws the matching parabola faintly behind the chain so you can watch the two curves kiss at the bottom and separate toward the ends — the catenary always the fuller of the two, standing a little steeper where the ends take the strain.

The bench

Slacken it, widen it, tilt it — then flip it into an arch

A chain pinned between two anchors, drawn side-on. Pay out more slack and it plunges deeper and pulls its anchors less hard; draw the span wider and the same slack goes taut; tilt one anchor above the other and the low point slides toward the lower end, the way a real chain always finds it. The chain is drawn thicker where it pulls harder — thin and slack at the belly, taut and thick at the ends — and the readout gives the pull at the anchors and at the belly in multiples of the chain's own weight, the steepest slope, and the curve constant a. Why keep it

As hangs the chain, so stands the arch

The catenary is worth a page because it is a shape the world computes for free. No one has to solve the equation for the chain; the chain solves it, instantly and exactly, by the simple fact that it cannot hold a shape in which any link is unbalanced. Hang it and it settles into the one answer. That is the oldest kind of analog computer there is — a problem in the calculus of variations, worked in hemp.

And it has a twin. Robert Hooke, in 1675, hid the whole of arch theory inside a Latin anagram, and when it was unscrambled it read: as hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch. A hanging chain is in pure tension — every part pulls. Freeze that curve, turn it over, and every pull becomes a push: the inverted catenary stands in pure compression, each stone leaning on its neighbours with no sideways bending to crack it. Christopher Wren shaped the dome of St Paul's near this curve; Antoni Gaudí hung weighted strings upside down to design his churches and read the arches straight off the shadows; the Gateway Arch in St Louis is a weighted catenary, thickened at the feet. Flip the chain on the bench and you are doing, in a browser, what Gaudí did with string and lead — reading an arch off the curve of a chain.
