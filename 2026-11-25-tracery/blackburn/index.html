Blackburn pendulum · noun · a bob on a forked string with two different swing periods in two perpendicular planes, tracing a Lissajous figure under gravity alone

Blackburn

A pendulum with two periods, drawing the figure in sand.

Hang a bob from a forked string — one cord from the ceiling down to a knot, then a Y down to the weight. Swing it in the plane of the fork and the whole length swings: a long, slow pendulum. Swing it across that plane and only the short stretch below the knot can move: a quick one. The bob keeps one period one way and another the other, and the path it traces between them is a Lissajous figure — drawn not by electronics but by gravity and a leaking bag of sand. Make the two lengths a square-number ratio and the figure closes; leave them untuned and it slowly turns, because the two periods no longer keep step.

The bench

One bob, two lengths, a figure in the tray

The sand tray is seen from above — a plan view of where the bob rides. Beside it, in elevation, is the forked string: the long drop from pivot to bob swings slowly; the short drop from the fork swings fast. A pendulum's period is T = 2π√(L/g), so the ratio of the two periods is √(Llong/Lshort) — the square root of the length ratio. That square root is why the clean figures want square numbers: four-to-one in length is two-to-one in period, nine-to-four is three-to-two. Between those the figure will not close; it drifts.

A closed figure ready

Drag left or right across the tray to swing it faster or slower; press space to release the bob, R to rake the sand clean. Square-ratio lengths draw a closed figure.

Why a square root hides in it

Two lengths, and the periods they keep

  • A pendulum's period depends only on its length, not on how far or how heavy it swings: T = 2π√(L/g). The forked string gives the bob two lengths at once — the whole cord in the plane of the fork, the stub below the knot across it — so the bob honestly keeps two periods, one per direction.
  • The period ratio is Tshort/Tlong = √(Lshort/Llong) — a square root. That is the whole character of the thing: to get a period ratio of 2:1 you need a length ratio of 4:1, for 3:2 a ratio of 9:4, for 3:1 a ratio of 9:1. The figure wants square numbers because the clock is hidden under a root.
  • When the lengths are square-tuned the two periods are commensurate — they share a common beat — so the bob returns to its start and the figure closes, exactly as the pure figure does with whole-number frequencies. The sand draws one curve, over and over, in the same place.
  • Off a square ratio the periods are incommensurate: the bob never quite comes home, each pass lands a little rotated, and the figure precesses — the same slow turning the ideal figure shows when you detune it, here written in sand. A real tray's drift is the plainest proof the tuning is not exact.
  • This is gravity doing by analogue what an oscilloscope does with voltages: Hugh Blackburn rigged it in 1844, and generations met Lissajous figures first as a sand pendulum on a schoolroom bench — the figure made physical, with a bob for a pen and the floor for a screen.

The bob is not clever. It only knows how to swing — but it is asked to swing to two clocks at once, and the quarrel between them is the figure.
