harmonograph · noun · a drawing machine in which two damped pendulums move a pen in perpendicular directions, tracing a Lissajous figure that spirals inward as the swing dies

Harmonograph

The figure given friction — and the nested rosette it dies into.

Take the two perpendicular oscillations that draw a standing figure and let them slowly die away, as every real pendulum must. The closed curve no longer closes: each loop falls a little short of the last, so the figure spirals inward, loop within loop within loop, and the pen comes to rest at the centre having drawn a whole nested rosette from a single release. Detune the frequencies a hair and the shrinking figure also turns as it dies, winding the loops into a slow helix of petals. It is a Victorian drawing machine — two weighted pendulums, a pen on a gimballed arm, a sheet of paper — and it made by hand, without a motor, the engine-turned curves that guilloché lathes cut by gear.

The bench

One release, a figure that winds down

Press draw and the pen starts at full swing, tracing the figure its frequency ratio would draw — but each pass the envelope (the faint ring) shrinks, and the curve steps inward. The decay is a plain e−d·t: a fixed fraction of swing lost each beat, so the loops crowd ever closer as the pen nears the centre. The whole drawing is made in one go, from one push, and left on the paper — ink, not a trace that fades. Tune the two frequencies near a simple ratio for a clean rosette; nudge one off and the rosette precesses into a wreath.

Why it spirals in

The same figure, losing amplitude a fixed fraction a beat

  • Undamped, the pen obeys the pure figure: two sines at right angles, a closed curve for a rational ratio. Damping multiplies each by a shrinking envelope e−d·t — the signature of friction, which takes a fixed fraction of the swing per unit time, not a fixed amount.
  • So each time around, the curve returns not to its start but to a point scaled a little toward the centre. The closed figure becomes an open spiral of itself: loop nested inside loop, each a touch smaller, converging on the origin where all the energy has gone.
  • Lower the decay and the pen loses less per beat, so it draws more loops before resting — a denser rosette. Raise it and the figure winds to the centre in a few sweeps. The decay alone sets the number of petals the sheet collects.
  • Detune the frequencies off their ratio and two things happen at once: the figure precesses (as in the ideal case) and it shrinks — so the nested loops also rotate, winding into a wreath or a many-pointed star. Almost every famous harmonograph drawing is a simple ratio with a deliberate small detune.
  • The machine is older than the oscilloscope and does the same arithmetic in brass and gravity. Its decaying rosettes are kin to guilloché — the engine-turned interlacing on watch dials and banknotes — which a geared lathe cuts by compounding two rotations, the Blackburn figure's industrial cousin.

Give the perfect figure a little friction and it stops repeating and starts accumulating — the drawing is the record of a single swing spending itself.
