Lissajous figure · noun · the closed curve traced by a point driven by two perpendicular oscillations, its shape fixed by the ratio of their frequencies and the phase between them

Figure

Two sines set at right angles, and the standing curve they write.

Pull a point side to side with one plain sine and up and down with another, and the path it traces is a figure whose shape depends on almost nothing — only the ratio of the two frequencies and the phase between them. When the ratio is a ratio of whole numbers the figure closes and repeats forever: a 1:1 is an ellipse that opens and shuts as you slide the phase; a 2:3 a trefoil; a 3:4 a woven braid. Count how often the curve kisses the top edge against how often it kisses the side, and you have read the ratio straight off the shape — which is exactly how an oscilloscope measures one frequency against another: tune until the figure stands still. Set the two frequencies, slide the phase, and watch the pen draw.

The bench

Two drives, one figure

Along the bottom runs the horizontal drive — a plain sine of frequency nx; up the left runs the vertical drive, frequency ny. The pen sits where the two meet: its shadow falls straight down to the one and straight across to the other. As both drives run, the pen writes the figure in the box. A whole-number ratio makes a closed curve; the left and right edges are touched nx times a cycle and the top and bottom edges ny times, so the figure wears its ratio on its face. Slide the phase and watch the figure breathe; detune a frequency a hair and watch it slowly turn.

Why the shape is only the ratio

A curve that wears its frequencies on its face

  • The pen obeys x = sin(nxt + δ), y = sin(nyt) — two independent oscillations, nothing coupling them but the single clock t they share. The amplitudes only set the size of the box; the shape is carried entirely by the ratio nx:ny and the phase δ.
  • When nx:ny is a ratio of whole numbers p:q, both sines return to their start together after a time 2π·q/ny, and the curve closes — a finite figure retraced forever. Every rational ratio is a different closed figure; the simpler the ratio, the simpler the figure.
  • The curve runs tangent to its box wherever a drive reaches full stretch: it kisses a left or right edge nx times a cycle and a top or bottom edge ny times. Count the touches on two edges and you have the ratio — read straight off the drawing, with no measurement of time at all.
  • Slide the phase and the figure deforms without changing its ratio: a 1:1 passes line → ellipse → circle → ellipse → line, because you are catching the same two sines at a different offset. The phase chooses which cross-section of the figure faces you; the ratio chooses the figure.
  • Detune a frequency by a hair and the ratio turns irrational: the curve never quite closes, so each pass lands at a slightly shifted phase and the whole figure precesses, turning slowly like a wire loop in the hand. On an oscilloscope this drift is the signal that two frequencies are not yet equal — and stilling the figure is how you make them so.

Two of the plainest motions there are, set across each other, and the shared clock braids them into a figure that could not be guessed from either sine alone.
