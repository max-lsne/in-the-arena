involute · noun · /ˈɪnvəluːt/ — the curve traced by the end of a taut string as it is unwound from a fixed circle; the flank shape given to gear teeth so that they hand on rotation at a perfectly even ratio; from Latin involūtus, rolled up, wrapped in, from involvere, to roll into

Involute

Cut gear teeth as plain pegs or triangles and the drive will ripple — the driven wheel racing and dragging within every single tooth, a stutter you can hear as a whine and feel as wear. The cure is not finer teeth but a particular curve, and it is the one a string draws as it unwinds from a circle.

Two wheels are meant to pass rotation between them at a fixed ratio: three turns of the little one for one of the big. But teeth touch at a moving point, and unless their shapes are chosen with care the point pushes off-square — so the far wheel gains speed as a tooth climbs and loses it as the next takes over, faster then slower many times a second. The fundamental law of gearing says exactly what shape avoids this: the line square to both teeth where they touch — the common normal — must cross one and the same point on the line joining the centres, whatever the moment in the mesh. The involute is the curve that obeys that law in the simplest way possible: it makes the common normal not merely pass through a fixed point but lie along a single fixed straight line, so the ratio never wavers — and because that line is set by two circles buried inside the wheels that never change, the ratio holds even if you set the gears too far apart.

The problem the curve solves

A fixed ratio, from teeth that touch on the move

A pair of smooth discs pressed together and rolling without slip would trade rotation perfectly evenly — the contact sits always on the line of centres, and the speeds stay in the ratio of the radii. But discs slip under load; you need teeth. The moment you cut teeth, contact leaves that tidy point and wanders out along a flank, and now the geometry of the tooth, not the disc, decides how hard and how square one wheel drives the next.

Get the flank wrong and the drive is uneven within each tooth. The far wheel is handed a little more angle here and a little less there, so a shaft that should turn like clock hands instead trembles — transmission error, the source of gear whine, of hammering under load, of teeth that wear themselves into an early grave. No amount of care in spacing the teeth removes it; it is baked into the shape of the flank.

The cure is a flank whose contact, wherever it happens to be, always pushes through the same point on the line of centres. Robert Willis named that the fundamental law of gearing; the involute is its neatest answer.

The one relation it embodies

Contact glides along a fixed line

Bury a base circle in each wheel — radius r_b1 in the driver, r_b2 in the driven. Cut every flank as the involute of that circle: the path drawn by a string's end as the string peels off the circle taut. An involute has one defining property — the string, at every instant, is tangent to the base circle and perpendicular to the flank. It is a normal to the tooth that is also a tangent to the circle.

Now let two such teeth touch. The common normal where they meet must be tangent to both base circles at once — and there is exactly one such line between two circles. So contact does not wander over a curve; it slides up and down a single fixed straight line, the line of action, tangent to both base circles and crossing the line of centres at the pitch point. The law is satisfied not approximately but perfectly, and at every instant of the mesh.

Because the contact force runs along that fixed line, the ratio it hands on is fixed too — the base-circle speeds must match, ω₁·r_b1 = ω₂·r_b2, so

ω₁ ⁄ ω₂ = r_b2 ⁄ r_b1 = N₂ ⁄ N₁,

a constant set only by the two base circles and the tooth counts. And here is the quiet miracle: those base circles are cut once and never move. Push the centres apart by a careless millimetre and the pitch circles swell and the pressure angle steepens — the line of action tilts — but r_b1 and r_b2 do not change, so the ratio does not change either. The relation that governs the whole spread is

cos φ = (r_b1 + r_b2) ⁄ C,

with C the centre distance and φ the pressure angle: stretch C and φ opens, but the ratio, riding on the base radii alone, holds dead still.

The bench

Turn the mesh, reshape it, then pull the centres apart

Press Turn to roll the mesh and watch the contact glide the straight line at an even pace. Change the driven teeth to reset the ratio, or the pressure angle to recut the flanks. Then draw centre distance out — or press Spread the centres — and watch the ratio refuse to move while everything else does: the tolerance that made the involute win.

The driver is fixed at twelve teeth; give the driven wheel more and the ratio deepens, its base and pitch circles growing to suit. The ratio is exactly N₂ over N₁, whatever the flank shape or the spacing.

The nominal angle the line of action makes to the wheels' common tangent, and so how steeply the flanks lean. The old 14½° gives slender teeth and a gentler push; the modern 20° and 25° give stubbier, stronger ones. It recuts the base circles on the spot.

Nominal is the snug distance where the pitch circles just kiss. Draw it out and the wheels part: the pitch circles swell, the pressure angle steepens, the teeth take up a little backlash — and the ratio, set by the untouched base circles, does not stir. That immunity is the involute's whole advantage.

Why keep it

A ratio that will not drift

The involute is worth a page because it turns a demand into a shape. The demand — that two wheels trade motion at one unwavering ratio — is a statement about every instant of a mesh, an infinity of conditions; the involute meets all of them at once with a single curve, the one a string draws unwinding from a circle. Leonhard Euler set the theory down in the 1760s, and Philippe de la Hire had seen the essential trick a lifetime earlier, but the shape needs no theory to work: it satisfies the law by construction, the way a hanging chain finds the catenary without solving for it.

Its rival, the cycloidal tooth, transmits an even ratio too — but only if the centres are set exactly right, for its contact curve depends on the spacing. The involute does not care. Cut the base circles and the ratio is fixed for good; a worn bearing, a rough casting, a shaft a hair out of place all change the pressure angle and the backlash and leave the ratio untouched. That single tolerance is why nearly every gear you will ever meet — in a watch, a mill, a car's box, a rocket's turbopump — is an involute, and the cycloid kept only to the odd clock and the pump. It keeps company with the other machines that hold one thing still while another runs loose: the fusee that holds a clock's drive level as its spring dies, the governor that holds an engine's speed as its load swings, the escapement that holds a beat as the push behind it fades. Turn the mesh on the bench, then pull the centres apart, and watch the ratio sit unmoved: that refusal is the involute, and it is only a string, unwound from a circle.
