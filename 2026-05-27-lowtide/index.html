Retros look at the meeting.
Lowtide looks at the shore.

A retro is a meeting where six people sit in a circle and answer the same three questions. It produces a document no one reads on Monday. It is not a bad thing. It is just not the same thing as the walk you took alone on Friday afternoon, when the office had gone quiet, and you noticed three things you would never have written on a sticky note.

Lowtide is for that walk. The week is a tide; on Friday it pulls back; the shore tells the truth. Three kinds of finds. Four states of keeping. A page that reads as honest as the strand line itself, and no honester than that.

How it works

  1. 01

    Wait for the water to pull back

    Once a week, late on a quiet afternoon. Not Monday. Not in a meeting. The walk only works when the room has gone empty enough that the small things start to show.

  2. 02

    Walk the strand

    Three to ten things. Finds, strandings, shells. The smallest count that still reads honest. If you cannot see ten, do not invent ten.

  3. 03

    Pick up only what you'll carry

    A find can be noticed, picked up, kept, or returned. Most things get returned. The basket is small on purpose; what stays in it is the work for the week ahead.

  4. 04

    Let the tide come back in

    By Monday the shore is under water again. The page does not have to outlast the walk. The point was the walk; the page is just where you set the shells down.

The three things a tide leaves

  • Find. Something useful uncovered. A note in a margin, a question half-answered, a thread you had lost. Worth picking up. Most of the value of the walk is in this column.
  • Stranding. Something the week left high and dry. A thread you had hoped would come in further. Worth deciding: drag it back to the water, or leave it for the gulls.
  • Shell. Small and bright and not useful. A pleasant exchange, an unexpected sentence, a kindness that has nowhere to go. Worth keeping anyway.

Who walks the shore

The bookbinder on a Friday in May

The finds are two margin notes in a 1908 ledger that suggest the previous binder used a thread the supplier no longer lists. The strandings are the catalogue copy that has been on the bench three weeks. The shells are the postcards from a customer in Halifax. The shore is the bench by the window at four.

The clinic director at the end of a long week

The finds are two patient notes that point to the same supply problem from different ends. The strandings are the hire that was supposed to start Monday. The shells are the thank-you card from the family in room six. The shore is the bench by the side door after the last patient leaves.

The teacher on a Friday in late spring

The finds are three short essays better than the prompt asked for. The strandings are the unit on long division that the slow class never reached. The shells are the small drawing one student left on the desk. The shore is the empty classroom after the buses have gone.

The freelance editor at the end of a project

The finds are two paragraphs the writer should keep when everything else is cut. The strandings are the chapter neither of you finished arguing about. The shells are the line the writer asked you to keep just for herself. The shore is the kitchen table after the manuscript has gone back.
