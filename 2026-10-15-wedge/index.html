wedge · noun · /wɛdʒ/ — a piece tapering to a thin edge, driven between two things or into one to force them apart, lift, split, or hold fast; from Old English wecg, a wedge, a mass of metal — the simple machine that is an inclined plane set moving

Wedge

An inclined plane you drive instead of climb — and the one simple machine that can hold what it lifts.

A ramp lets a weight go up a gentle slope for a gentle push; a wedge is that ramp laid on its side and shoved endwise, so the gentle push becomes a great sideways force at the faces. Split a log, lift a shed corner, seat a machine on folding steel shims, hold an axe-head on its haft — all one shape: two shallow faces meeting at an edge, forcing apart whatever they enter. The shallower the taper the greater the force, and the further the wedge must travel to deliver it — the same trade of force for distance a lever makes. But a wedge keeps a second account the lever never opens. Friction along the faces can grip hard enough that the load cannot push the wedge back out — the wedge self-locks, and stays exactly where it was driven. That grip is the wedge's gift and its whole danger: steepen the wedge for more work and less travel and the grip thins, until, past a line the friction draws, the load spits it out the instant you stop pushing.

Where the force comes from

A ramp shoved endwise

Picture the gentler cousin first. To raise a barrel to a ledge you roll it up a ramp: a small push along a long slope does the work a large lift would, and the ratio is pure geometry — slope length over slope height. A wedge is that same inclined plane, but instead of the load climbing a fixed ramp, the ramp is driven under the load. Give the wedge a taper half-angle α — the angle each face makes with the line you drive along — and every length s you push it in lifts the work only s·tan α. A shallow wedge barely lifts per inch of drive, so it must travel far; in exchange, the force it musters at the face is large.

The wedge creeps a long way to move the work a little — and that is exactly why the little move comes with such force. Distance in, force out: the lever's bargain, carried on an edge.

Idealise the faces as frictionless for a moment and the bargain is exact. The force delivered to the load is cot α times the force you drive with: a 10° wedge multiplies by about six, a 5° wedge by eleven. That is the wedge's ideal mechanical advantage, and it is bought back, to the last foot-pound, in travel — you push cot α times as far as the load moves, so the work in equals the work out and nothing is made. A wedge, like every simple machine, only spreads a force thin over a long path or gathers it into a short one. But no real wedge is frictionless, and in a wedge, unlike a lever, friction is not a loss to be minimised. It is the second half of the machine.

The one inequality, read three ways

The taper, the friction cone, and what holds

Take the taper half-angle α, the coefficient of friction between the faces μ, and from it the friction angle φ = arctan μ — the steepest slope a surface can hold a resting weight on before it slides. Drive the wedge against a resistance W normal to its face, and the whole of the wedge is three lines read off α against φ:

  • It costs more to drive, less to withdraw, than the taper alone. Pushing a wedge in takes P = W·tan(α + φ) — the taper plus the friction, both fought on the way in. Backing it out takes W·tan(α − φ) — the taper now helping, the friction still resisting. Friction is added going in and subtracted coming out, so the two are never the same price.
  • It self-locks exactly inside the friction cone. When α ≤ φ the withdrawal force W·tan(α − φ) turns negative: the load cannot push the wedge out — you must pull it. The wedge stays where it was driven. This is the one trick no lever, pulley, or gear train can do unaided: hold its own output with nothing holding it. A wedge is a one-way machine made of nothing but an angle and a rub.
  • Efficiency and grip pull opposite ways. The fraction of your work the wedge delivers is η = tan α ⁄ tan(α + φ), and it rises as the wedge steepens — a steeper wedge wastes less in the faces and travels less to do the job. But the holding margin φ − α shrinks as it steepens, and the instant α passes φ the wedge springs back out. So the best wedge is never the shallowest, for all its force, nor the steepest, which will not hold — it is the steepest one still inside the cone, α a hair under φ: the most work delivered, and still held.

Those three are one inequality turned three ways. The friction that makes the wedge grip is the friction that makes it dear to drive; the taper that makes it efficient is the taper that lets it slip. You cannot have a wedge that is cheap to seat, holds like a vice, and comes out easily — the same angle sets all three, and reads differently in each. Choose the account you care about, and the other two are decided for you.

Self-locking is friction standing in for a hand. Trust the grip, and you trust the rub to stay — an oiled face, a polished one, a trace of wear, and the line moves under you.

Four states, from a wedge that holds to one that springs

Around the one edge

  1. Holds. The taper sits well inside the friction cone, α comfortably below φ, with efficiency enough to be worth driving. It bites, it lifts, and it stays — you can let go of it. The gib key, the cotter, the taper pin: a wedge whose whole job is to be driven once and not move again.
  2. On the edge. The taper has crept up near the friction line, α just under φ. It still holds, and holds efficiently — but barely. A film of oil, a polished face, a little wear drops φ toward α and the grip is gone. A wedge that leans on all of its friction has nothing in reserve.
  3. Thankless. The taper is far below the cone, α ≪ φ. The force is enormous and the hold is absolute — but the efficiency η is tiny: you drive it a vast way, most of your work turns to heat in the faces, and it is murder to get back out. Force bought far too dear in distance; a wedge that works, and that you will come to hate.
  4. Springs. The taper has passed the friction line, α > φ. The wedge is no longer self-locking: the load drives it straight back out the moment you stop pushing. A splitting wedge is built to want this — the halves fly, the crack runs. A holding wedge that reaches it is simply ruined.

Who reads it, and for what

The same edge, three ways

The fitter

wants it to hold

Drives a gib, a cotter, a taper key to lock a wheel on a shaft, and needs it to stay put under a day of vibration. Chooses a shallow taper well inside the friction cone — α safely below φ — and accepts the low efficiency and the hard drive as the price of a joint that never shakes loose.

The splitter

wants it to run

Sinks a felling or splitting wedge into timber and wants the crack to open and the halves to part. A steep taper, often past the friction line, so the wood's own spring-back keeps the faces working and the wedge does not seize in a log it half-opened — it must bite, drive, and be freed for the next blow.

The rigger

wants the steepest that still holds

Levels a machine on folding steel wedges, backing one against another under a foot that must sit to a thousandth and not creep. Picks the steepest taper the friction he can trust will still lock — α a hair under φ — so the shim seats with the least drive and holds the setting all the same.

The bench

Set the taper against the friction

A wedge driven under a load, between two faces that rub. Set the taper — how shallow or steep the wedge is — and watch the force it delivers and the travel it costs trade against each other. Choose the faces, from oiled steel through dry metal to a rough, rusted pair, and watch the friction cone φ widen or shrink — the line the taper must stay inside to hold. Set the load the wedge works against: it scales the forces but not the regime, for whether the wedge holds, labours, or springs is pure geometry and friction, set by α against φ alone. The discipline in the word

An angle that stands in for a hand

The wedge is the oldest cutting and lifting tool there is, and the simplest machine by far — nothing but an inclined plane freed from the ground and driven. Everything it does is read off one angle against one other: the taper α it is ground to, and the friction angle φ the faces happen to have. Set them side by side and the whole machine falls out. Shallow against the cone and it holds like a vice and drives like a punishment; steep and inside the cone and it holds lightly and delivers well; steep and past the cone and it will not hold at all. The force-for-distance bargain it strikes is the lever's own — the same coin the fulcrum counts, where the best lever is likewise the least advantage that does the job, never the most. But the self-locking the wedge gets from friction is a thing the lever cannot buy: it is the same one-way grip that makes a worm gear drive its wheel and never be back-driven, and the near-cousin of the pawl, which holds a ratchet one way with a tooth where the wedge holds with an angle. A screw is only a wedge wrapped round a cylinder, and self-locks by the same inequality.

The discipline is to know which of the three accounts you are keeping — force, efficiency, or grip — and to spend the taper on that one, knowing it settles the other two. If you want it to hold, buy the hold with a shallow angle and pay in drive and travel; if you want it to work, steepen it toward the cone and give up the margin; and never forget that the grip you are trusting is only friction, which a drop of oil or a season of wear can take away. The wedge is an angle doing the work of a hand that holds — and like any hand, it can let go.
