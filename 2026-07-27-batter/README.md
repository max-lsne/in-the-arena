# Batter

**A plumb wall meets its load head-on and loses. A battered wall leans back into
what pushes on it — and stands. The lean is not decoration and it is not there to
shed rain. It is where the wall puts its weight so that the one line that decides
everything lands where it must.**

A **batter** is the deliberate backward lean built into the face of a wall. A
gravity retaining wall — a sea wall, a dam, a drystone field boundary, the
masonry holding back a road cut — holds its load by its own weight and nothing
else: no anchors, no reinforcement, no tie into the ground behind it. Whether it
stands has almost nothing to do with how heavy it is and almost everything to do
with **where** that weight sits over the base.

Two forces act on the wall. Its **weight** presses straight down through its
centre of mass. The retained earth (or water) pushes **horizontally** against the
back, and because that push grows with depth, its resultant sits a third of the
way up from the base. Add them — down plus sideways — and you get a single
slanting **line of thrust**. Everything the wall will or won't do is written in
one place: where that line crosses the base.

> A masonry wall can push but it cannot pull. Ask one edge of a bed joint to hold
> the wall down and it can't — it simply lifts. So the whole base must stay in
> compression, and that is a rule about a third.

## The middle third

Divide the base into three.

- **Line of thrust in the middle third** — every point of the bed is pressed
  down. The joint stays shut and the wall behaves as one solid thing. It holds.
- **In the front third** — the heel (the back edge) is pulled into tension it
  cannot carry. The joint **opens** a hair, water walks in, frost widens it, and
  the wall comes apart from a crack no one drew — a winter or two after it was
  built, from the back, out of sight.
- **Off the base entirely** — nothing holds the wall down. It goes over the toe.

**Batter is the answer to the rule.** Lean the front face back and you carry the
wall's weight rearward, toward where the earth is pushing, walking the line of
thrust back into the middle third *without pouring a single extra stone*. A
thinner, cleverer wall outlasts a fat plumb one. That is the whole craft: not
more stone, but stone in the right place.

## Five walls, worst to true

1. **Overturned** — too thin, built plumb; the line of thrust falls off the front
   of the base and the wall tips over its toe.
2. **Slid** — heavy enough not to tip, but the thrust beats the friction under the
   base and shoves the whole wall forward off its footing.
3. **Opened** — stands and passes a glance, but the line sits in the front third;
   the heel joint lifts and hands the weather its seam. The failure is drawn; it
   only has to be found.
4. **Overbuilt** — trued the lazy way, plumb and fat, widened until sheer mass
   drags the line back into the third. It holds — and hauls far more stone than
   the load ever asked. Sound, and wasteful: the batter is the cleverness it
   declines.
5. **True** — face battered just enough that the wall's own weight leans back to
   meet the push, and the line rides the middle third at the least stone. It does
   not need to be strong. It needs to be right.

## What's in this folder

- `index.html` — the landing page and the bench, in the browser.
- `styles.css` — a limewash-and-sandstone palette: pale field, dressed-stone grey
  for the wall, iron-ochre for the retained earth, a decisive iron line for the
  thrust; lichen for a wall that holds true, amber for a joint that opens, and
  brick-red for one that slides or goes over. Fraunces for headings, Inter for
  body, JetBrains Mono for marginalia. Light and dark.
- `script.js` — the statics. A gravity wall in cross-section, per metre run:
  Rankine active thrust against a smooth vertical back, the weight of a trapezoidal
  section through its centroid, the resultant dropped to the base, and the reading
  — overturning and sliding factors, eccentricity, toe and heel pressure, and the
  verdict against the middle third. It opens on a wall a hand too narrow — standing,
  but with its heel joint already open — so the bench is never empty on arrival.

## The bench

Set the wall's height, widen its base, lean the face back with the batter, and
choose what it holds back — loose sand, packed gravel, or standing water (which
pushes harder and rises to the top, which is why dams are the most massive walls
of all). The page draws the wall, the pressure behind it, and the line of thrust
against the lit middle third, and reads whether it holds, opens, slides, or goes
over. *True it* widens the base until the line comes home; *Reset* returns to the
flawed wall.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. No build step, no account, no network once the
page has loaded. The wall you true stays in the page.
