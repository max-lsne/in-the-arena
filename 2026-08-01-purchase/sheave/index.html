sheave · noun · /ʃiːv/ — the grooved wheel inside a block, that a rope runs over

Sheave

A tackle never makes the load lighter. It just hangs it from more parts of the rope at once — and shares the weight out.

A block is a shell of wood or iron with one or more sheaves in it — grooved wheels the rope runs over. Reeve a rope down through a fixed block and up through a moving one, back and forth, and the load hung on the moving block is held not by one part of line but by several at the same time. Each part carries an equal share of the weight, so the last part — the fall, the end in your hands — carries only that share too. Six parts, and you haul a sixth of the load. That is the whole of the trick, and it is not free: to raise the load a foot, every one of those six parts must shorten by a foot, so you must haul six feet of rope through your hands. You buy force with distance, at a fixed rate, and the sheaves only decide how much of the bargain the friction eats.

Where the purchase comes from

Count the parts at the moving block

The rule for a tackle is almost insultingly simple, and it is worth saying in the plainest way: count the parts of rope that run to the moving block. That number is the purchase. Not the number of sheaves, not the number of blocks — the number of falls of rope actually pulling up on the load. Two parts, and a pull of fifty holds a hundred. Six parts, and the same fifty holds three hundred.

Ideal effort = load ÷ parts. The velocity ratio — the rope you haul for each length the load rises — is the same number, the other way up. Force down, distance up, and the product never changes.

What breaks the ideal is the sheaves. Every one the rope bends over steals a little to friction, so a real tackle needs a bit more pull than load ÷ parts — and the more parts you reeve, the more sheaves the rope must turn, so the gain from each extra part is a touch less than the one before. Grease the pins and the loss is small; let the blocks stiffen with salt and rust and it grows, until a great tackle is mostly hauling itself.

The one price

Force is bought with rope, always

There is no arrangement of blocks that beats the bargain — there is only the bargain, paid in different coin. A tackle that lets one hand raise a quarter-tonne is a tackle through which you will haul a great tired heap of rope. This has three consequences worth keeping:

More parts, less effort, more rope, slower lift. Fewer parts, more effort, less rope, quicker lift. The blocks' condition sets how much of the ideal you actually keep.

You can run out of purchase. When the two blocks come together — two-blocked — you can reeve no more parts and the tackle is spent; the load stops there whether it is up or not. Friction has a floor you can raise. Stiff, dry, salted blocks can turn a six-part tackle into the effort of a three-part one; a can of oil is worth more than another sheave. And the fall must be long enough: six parts means six times the rope run out, so a short line two-blocks before the load is home.

Four tackles, dead to rove

From a load that won't start to one hand hauling it home

  1. Won't start. Too few parts for the weight: the fall carries more than a person can hold, and the load simply stays on the ground. Nothing is broken. The tackle is under-rove — it has not been given enough parts to divide the load down to what one pair of hands can pull.
  2. Hard won. It will come, but the fall is over a comfortable steady haul — you can start the load and raise it, but your arms will give out before it is up. One more part, or a hand on the fall beside you, and it comes into reach.
  3. Rove to advantage. The parts have divided the load until the fall sits inside an easy steady pull, and one hand keeps it coming hand over hand. This is the tackle doing its work: the weight unchanged, the effort brought down to a person's measure, the price paid quietly in rope.
  4. Choked with friction. So many parts through such stiff blocks that the sheaves eat most of the gain: the ideal says load ÷ parts, but half of that is going into turning dry pins, and each new part you reeve barely lightens the fall. The cure is not more parts — it is oil, or better blocks.

Three who reeve a line

The same tackle, three trades

The rigger

the parts

Reeves for the weight and the fall he has: enough parts to bring the load down to what the gang can haul, never so many that the blocks two-block before the lift is home. Counts the parts at the moving block, not the sheaves, and knows that is the purchase.

The sailor

the blocks

Keeps the sheaves turning free — greases the pins, rounds the salt off the grooves, swaps a block that binds — because he has learned that a fair three-part tackle beats a foul six. Judges a purchase by how much of the ideal it gives back, not by how grand it looks.

The mover

the rope

Reckons the rope, not just the pull: knows that raising the piano two floors on a six-part tackle means hauling twelve floors of line, and lays the fall out clear so it runs. Trades distance for force on purpose, and counts the cost before he starts.
