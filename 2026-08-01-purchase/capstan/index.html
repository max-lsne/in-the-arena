capstan · noun · /ˈkapst(ə)n/ — a drum a rope is wound round, so friction multiplies the hold

Capstan

Wrap the rope a few turns round the drum, and the friction does the holding — your hand only keeps the tail from paying out.

A capstan is just a drum — a bollard, a windlass barrel, a winch, a bitt. Its magic is not that it is turned but that a rope wound round it grips it, and the grip compounds. Where the rope first touches the drum it carries the load's full pull. As it wraps, the drum's friction bleeds that pull away, so a little further round the rope carries less, and less again, and by the time the rope leaves as the tail in your hand it carries only a whisper of what the load puts on the other end. The astonishing part is the rate: the hold does not fall by a fixed amount per turn but by a fixed fraction, so each turn strips away a slice of what is left. Three turns of hemp on an iron bitt, and one hand holds a berthing ship against the tide.

Where the purchase comes from

Friction that compounds with the wrap

At every point the rope hugs the drum, the drum pushes back, and that push lets it rub — and the rubbing is what saps the tension. Because there is more rope in contact the further round you go, the loss is proportional to the tension already there, which is the signature of a thing that decays by a fixed fraction. Work it out and the tail tension is the load tension shrunk by a power of the wrap:

T_hold = T_load · e−μθ, where θ is the total angle wrapped (2π per turn) and μ is how much the rope and drum grip. The purchase — how many times your hold is multiplied — is eμθ.

Two things set it, and one of them is free. The turns pile up in the exponent: each full turn multiplies your purchase by the same factor again, so going from two turns to three does not add a third — it multiplies. The surface sets μ: greasy or wet rope on a smooth drum grips little; dry hemp on a rough iron bitt grips hard. And nothing here depends on the drum being turned, or on how big the load is — the ratio is fixed by the turns and the grip alone. That is why a child at the tail can hold what a capstan bar-gang put on the other end.

The one catch

The same friction that holds will not let go

Purchase this cheap has a price, and it is not paid in effort — it is paid in control. The friction that lets one hand hold the load is the same friction you must overcome to ease the load out, and it compounds just as fast in that direction:

Too few turns and the load takes charge — the tail overpowers your grip and the rope surges off the drum. Too many, and it will not render — you cannot pay it out at all without throwing a turn off first.

Between those is the working country. Rendering is when the tail sits just above your easy grip: the rope creeps round the drum under strain, slow and controlled — exactly what you want when lowering a load, and a warning when making fast. Holding fast is when the tail is inside an easy one-hand grip and the line will not move until you let it. And the craft is knowing that the cure for a load that surges is not a stronger arm — it is another turn — and the cure for a line you cannot ease is not a harder pull, but a turn taken off.

Four wraps, surging to nipped

From a load that runs away to one you cannot budge

  1. Takes charge. Too few turns: the tail carries more than a hand can grip, so the load overpowers you and the rope surges off the drum, running the line out. Nothing is broken. The wrap has not been given enough turns to strip the load's pull down to a grip.
  2. Rendering. The tail sits just over an easy grip: the rope creeps round the drum under strain, slow and controlled. This is what you want for lowering — a load eased out hand over hand — and what you check when you meant to make fast.
  3. Holding fast. The tail is inside an easy one-hand grip: the drum holds the load and your hand only keeps the tail from paying out. One hand belays tonnes, and the line will not move until you let it. The purchase has come home.
  4. Nipped solid. So many turns, or such a gripping surface, that the tail force is all but nothing: the line will not surge — but it will not render either. A fine make-fast, a poor lowering; to ease the load you must first throw a turn off. Too much purchase is its own kind of trouble.

Three who take a turn

The same drum, three trades

The docker

the turns

Reads the load and throws on turns to match — a couple to check a light line, three or four to hold a ship against the tide — and knows that each turn multiplies rather than adds. Takes a turn off, never fights the tail, when a made-fast line has to be eased.

The climber

the surface

The belay device is a capstan folded small: the same `e^(−μθ)` in the hand. Chooses the friction for the fall he must hold and the lower he must give, and keeps a brake hand on the tail always — because the drum only holds the share the wrap has left it.

The teamster

the render

Snubs a load down a slope with a turn round a tree or a post, and lives in the rendering band on purpose — enough turns that the line creeps and does not run, few enough that he can still pay it out. Knows the friction that saves him going up can trap him coming down.
