handspike · noun · /ˈhandspʌɪk/ — a stout bar used as a lever to move a heavy load

Handspike

You do not lift the rock. You set a bar under it, prop it near the rock, and let your long arm beat its short one.

A handspike is a plain stout bar — oak or iron — and it is the oldest machine there is. Lay it across a fulcrum, put its short end under the load and your hands on its long end, and the two ends are locked in a bargain of arms: the load pushing down on its short arm and you pushing down on your long one, turning about the same point. Whoever has the longer arm wins, in the exact proportion of the arms. Set the fulcrum a hand's breadth from the rock and your grip four feet away, and your four feet overpower its one by four to one — a quarter-tonne answers to the weight of your shoulders. The advantage is nothing more than that ratio. And the price, as always, is on the other side of it: your end must travel four feet to raise the rock one.

Where the purchase comes from

The ratio of the two arms

A lever turns about its fulcrum, and at balance the load's turning-effort equals yours: the load times its arm equals your push times your arm. Rearrange it and the whole machine falls out in one line — your effort is the load scaled by how short its arm is against how long yours is.

Effort = load × (load arm ÷ effort arm). The mechanical advantage is the arms the other way up — effort arm over load arm — and it is all the lever is.

Two things move that ratio, and they are not the same move. Slide the fulcrum toward the load and you shorten its arm and lengthen yours at once, so the advantage climbs fast — this is the strong move, and the one that costs you travel. Lengthen the bar past the fulcrum and you lengthen only your arm, so the advantage grows too, but gently, and a long bar begins to bow and spring under a hard push. The fulcrum is where the power is; the bar is only how far out you can stand.

The one price

Force is bought with travel, always

The lever gives back force in exactly the measure it takes away distance. Raise the advantage to four and your hands must move four times as far as the load; raise it to twenty and you sweep a great arc to lift the rock a finger's width. There is no setting that escapes it — only settings that spend the trade well or badly:

Fulcrum near the load: much force, little travel, a short strong heave. Fulcrum near your hands: little force, much travel — and past the middle, no advantage at all.

The fulcrum can be on the wrong side. Prop it nearer your hands than the load and the arms invert: you are now on the short arm, adding to the load instead of beating it. All force can be no use. Set the fulcrum almost touching the load and the advantage is enormous but the travel is nothing — you strain a boulder that rises a hair, when what you wanted was to roll it clear. And the bar has a limit: a long wooden handspike under a hard push bows, springs, and can snap — iron where it matters.

Four bars, immovable to prised

From a rock that won't stir to one that lifts clean

  1. Immovable. The fulcrum is too far from the load, or the bar too short: the advantage is small, the effort still beyond a hard heave, and the rock does not stir. Nothing is broken. The lever has not been set to divide the load down to what a body can put on it.
  2. Straining. It will lift, but only with a hard burst — you can throw your weight on the bar and start it, but not hold it there. Slide the fulcrum a little toward the load, or stand out further on the bar, and it comes under an easy lean.
  3. Prised. The arms are set so the effort sits inside a comfortable bear-down — you lean your weight on the long end and the load rises to meet it. This is the lever doing its work: the weight unchanged, the effort brought down to a body's measure, the price paid in the sweep of your arc.
  4. All force, no travel. The fulcrum is almost under the load: the advantage is vast and the effort trivial, but your end sweeps a foot to raise the load a hair. You can strain it enormously and move it almost not at all — great for cracking a thing loose, useless for shifting it far.

Three who work a bar

The same lever, three trades

The quarryman

the fulcrum

Sets the fulcrum where the power is — a chip of stone jammed close under the block — and knows that moving it an inch nearer the load does more than another foot of bar. Wants force to crack the block loose from its bed, and spends travel freely to get it.

The wrecker

the bar

Carries a long iron because he cannot always choose his fulcrum, and reaches his advantage by standing further out. Watches for the bow: a bar that springs is storing his heave and giving it back as a jump, and a sprung bar under load is a bar about to fail.

The mover

the travel

Reckons the arc, not just the lift: knows that a twenty-to-one advantage means his hands sweep twenty feet to raise the load one, and sets the fulcrum only as strong as he needs, so the load actually travels and does not merely strain in place.
