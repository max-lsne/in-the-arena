# Handspike

**You do not lift the rock. You set a bar under it, prop it near the rock, and
let your long arm beat its short one — in the exact proportion of the arms.**

A *handspike* is a plain stout bar — oak or iron — and it is the oldest machine
there is. Lay it across a fulcrum, put its short end under the load and your
hands on its long end, and the two ends are locked in a bargain: the load
pushing down on its short arm, you pushing down on your long one, both turning
about the same point. Whoever has the longer arm wins, in the exact ratio of the
arms. Set the fulcrum a hand's breadth from the rock and your grip four feet
away, and four beats one — a quarter-tonne answers to the weight of your
shoulders. And the price is on the other side: your end must travel four feet to
raise the rock one.

## The one price

- **Effort = load × (load arm ÷ effort arm).** The mechanical advantage is the
  arms the other way up, and it is *all* the lever is.
- **The fulcrum is where the power is.** Slide it toward the load and you shorten
  its arm and lengthen yours at once — a fast gain, paid in travel. Lengthen the
  bar and you lengthen only your arm — a gentle gain, until the bar begins to bow.
- **It can turn against you.** Prop the fulcrum past the middle and the arms
  invert: you are on the short arm now, adding to the load, not beating it.

On the bench, with load `W`, load arm `a` and effort arm `b`:

```
effort = W · a / b        MA = b / a        travel ratio = b / a
```

## The bench

One lever in schematic — a bar over a fulcrum, the load on the short arm, your
hands on the long one. Set the **load**, the **bar length**, and how far the
**fulcrum** stands from the load. The page shows the effort at your hands, the
mechanical advantage, the two arms, and the travel — how far your end sweeps for
each length the load rises — and lights the **within-reach band**, the span of
effort a body can bear down with.

It opens on a heavy block with the fulcrum set too far off, immovable under a
hard heave. **Set the fulcrum** slides it toward the load until the lift comes
within reach — using only as much advantage as it needs, so the load actually
travels and does not merely strain in place.
