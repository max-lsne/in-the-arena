glass · noun · /ɡlɑːs/ — a vessel of fused silica, hard and brittle, that rings at a clear pitch when struck and flexes into an oval when driven at it; a thin crystal glass rings long and true, a thick tumbler briefly and dull; from Old English glæs

Glass

Not the loudest voice that breaks it — the one that matches.

Ping a wine glass with a fingernail and it rings at one clear pitch — its natural note, fixed by the shape and thickness of the bowl. Now hold a steady tone at that exact note. The rim answers, flexing from round to oval and back at the pitch of the tone; and because good glass sheds its ringing slowly, each cycle of the tone adds a little more flex than the glass has yet lost, so the oval grows, cycle on cycle, far past what the tone's own loudness could ever push it in a single shove. Grow it far enough and the silica flexes past the strain it can bear, and the glass bursts. Sing a tone even a little off the note and none of this happens — the rim barely stirs, because the pushes no longer land in step. The pitch is the one dial, and it decides everything: whether the glass is silent, sings, strains, or shatters. Loudness only sets how far a matched note carries it.

Where the energy goes

A push kept, cycle on cycle

A glass rim, left alone after a tap, rings at its own pitch and slowly dies away — a little of its ringing lost to the air and to the silica's own faint internal friction each cycle. A thin crystal loses very little and rings for a long time; a thick tumbler loses a lot and stops almost at once. That rate of loss is the glass's own, and the one thing a voice must reckon with.

A matched tone does not have to be loud. It has to be patient, and on the note — then every cycle banks a little more flex than the last one shed, and the small becomes the enormous.

Hold a tone exactly on the note and each cycle's push arrives just as the rim is moving with it, so the whole of the push is added — and because the glass sheds so little between cycles, what is added stays. The flex climbs until the energy poured in by the tone each cycle equals the energy the glass sheds each cycle, and there it settles — but for a good glass that settling point is very high, high enough to break it. Sing off the note and the pushes walk out of step: some cycles help, some fight, and across a held note they cancel, so the rim never builds. The energy is not lost to loudness — it is lost to mismatch. Read the glass and you find a rim keeping its own clear pitch, a tone pouring in energy of your choosing, and the flex standing tiny or fatal entirely on whether the two agree.

The one relation it embodies

The gain, and the note that unlocks it

Let the tone's pitch be f and the glass's own note f₀, and write r = f ⁄ f₀. Let Q be the glass — how many cycles it rings on after a tap. The settled flex of the rim is the flex a slow steady push would give, multiplied by a gain that is the whole of the matter:

  • The gain. gain = 1 ⁄ √((1 − r²)² + (r ⁄ Q)²). It is how many times deeper the rim flexes under a held, matched tone than under the same push held still. Every glass a singer ever broke is in this one curve.
  • On the note. At r = 1 the gain rises to about Q — a crystal that rings for a thousand cycles flexes a thousandfold on its note. This is why a voice no louder than a shout can burst a glass no hand could crush: not force, but match, multiplied by the glass's own long ringing.
  • The width. The peak is narrow — only about 1 ⁄ Q wide in r. The finer the crystal, the taller the flex it will reach and the more exactly the voice must sit on the note. A thick tumbler makes a low, broad hump: easy to hit, impossible to break.

So the very quality that makes a glass sing makes it break, and the note is a target the voice can miss by a hair. A dull tumbler flexes a little under almost any nearby tone and never enough to crack. A fine crystal flexes barely at all until the tone is dead on its note, and then flexes catastrophically. And the flex has no ceiling but the glass's own strain: a crystal held exactly on its note, loudly, does not settle to a safe ring — it settles past breaking.

You do not break a glass by singing louder. You break it by singing truer — and the finer the glass, the truer you must be.

Four states, from a still rim to a burst bowl

From silent to shattered

  1. Silent. The tone is off the note, or too soft to matter. The pushes fall out of step and cancel, or are too small to add to; the rim barely stirs and the glass sits mute. No loudness at the wrong pitch wakes it — a shout a tone away is a shout wasted.
  2. Rings. The tone is near the note and the rim flexes visibly, singing back the pitch it is given — the sympathetic answer, well within the strain the glass can bear. This is the glass a musician plays: it sounds, it holds, it comes to no harm. Beautiful, and safe.
  3. Strains. The tone sits right on the note and loud, and the flex has climbed near the breaking strain — the rim whipping from round to oval so hard the glass hums and blurs. One notch louder, or one cycle longer, and it goes. The state a wary singer backs off from, and a showman holds.
  4. Shatters. The flex has passed the strain the silica can bear, and the bowl bursts. It took no great loudness — only the exact note, held on a glass fine enough to bank it. Matched, and driven past its limit: the same long ringing that made it sing made it break.

Three who read the same note

The same glass, three trades

The singer

the note

Finds the glass's note by ear first — a ping, then matches it in the throat — because loudness spent off the note is loudness wasted. Holds the pitch dead steady, for the peak is a hair wide and a wavering voice slides off it and the flex falls away. Knows a thin crystal will break and a thick tumbler never will, and reaches for the ring before the volume: the trick is match, and the crowd only hears the loudness.

The glassmaker

the ring

Reads a glass by its ring: a long, pure tone means thin, even walls and few flaws — a high Q — and a dull short one means thick or cracked. Prizes the long ring as the mark of quality, and knows it for the same thing that makes the glass fragile to its note. Anneals to take out the locked-in strain that would let a resonance find a flaw and run it into a crack, so the glass rings long and still forgives the odd matched tone.

The engineer

the curve

Sees a lightly-damped oscillator and reads the whole thing off one curve. Writes the flex as the static push times 1 ⁄ √((1 − r²)² + (r ⁄ Q)²), marks the peak near r = 1 of height ≈ Q and width ≈ 1 ⁄ Q, and warns that a high-Q part is a hazard as much as a virtue: near its natural frequency a small steady input becomes a large stress. Detunes it, or damps it, before a stray tone finds it.
