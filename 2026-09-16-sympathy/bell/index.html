bell · noun · /bɛl/ — a hollow cast vessel, hung to swing on its gudgeons, that sounds when a clapper strikes its soundbow; to ring up a tower bell is to swing it, stroke by timed stroke, from hanging still to mouth uppermost; from Old English belle

Bell

A swing too heavy to shove, raised by pushes too small to matter — timed.

A full-circle tower bell weighs more than the ringer, hangs on a headstock high in the tower, and to be rung it must swing a whole turn, mouth-down to mouth-up and back. No arm could throw it. What raises it is a single rope and a fact about swinging things: a bell left to itself swings at one natural stroke, slow and fixed by its weight and its wheel. Pull on the rope at that stroke — a light haul as the bell swings away, and again on the next swing — and each pull lands in step with the last, so the pushes add rather than fight, and the swing climbs a little every stroke until the bell stands on its stay. Pull at any other tempo and the pulls quarrel: one hauls while the bell is still coming back, and it hangs there dead. The tempo of the pull is the one dial, and it decides everything — whether the bell rings up at all, how high it climbs, and whether it climbs so far the stay gives.

Where the energy goes

Small pushes, kept

Set a heavy thing swinging and it keeps a beat of its own — the natural stroke, fixed by the mass and the wheel, not by you. A big bell swings slowly, a small one quickly, and neither cares how hard it was struck; struck harder it swings wider, but at the same unhurried rate. That rate is the bell's, and the one thing the ringer must respect. Everything else follows from a single choice: whether your pull arrives when the bell can take it.

A timed push is not spent; it is banked. Arrive on the beat and every pull is added to the swing already there, and a hundred small pulls become one great one.

Pull at the natural stroke and each haul comes as the bell is moving away from you, so the whole of it goes into the swing — and because a heavy bell keeps most of what it is given, the swings stack up. A pull that would move a still bell a degree, repeated on the beat, raises it degree upon degree until it climbs the whole quarter-turn to the balance. Pull too fast or too slow and the timing walks: sometimes you help, sometimes you haul against the returning bell, and over many strokes the help and the hindrance cancel. The energy is not lost to friction — it is lost to disagreement. Read the tower and you find a bell keeping its own slow time, a rope delivering pulls of your choosing, and the swing standing high or low entirely on whether the two agree.

The one relation it embodies

The gain, and the beat that unlocks it

Let your pull come at frequency f and the bell's natural stroke be f₀, and write their ratio r = f ⁄ f₀. Let Q be the bell's freedom — how many strokes it keeps swinging after you stop. The settled swing is the swing one lone pull would give, multiplied by a gain that is the whole of the matter:

  • The gain. gain = 1 ⁄ √((1 − r²)² + (r ⁄ Q)²). It is how many times larger the timed, repeated swing is than the shove of a single pull. Everything the ringer feels is in this one curve.
  • On the beat. At r = 1 the first term vanishes and the gain rises to about Q — a bell that keeps swinging for fifty strokes will ring up some fifty times higher than one pull could throw it. This is why a child can raise a bell an ox could not: not strength, but agreement, multiplied by the bell's own patience.
  • The width. The peak is narrow — only about 1 ⁄ Q wide in r. The freer the bell, the taller the gain and the finer the tempo you must hold to reach it. Miss the stroke by a little and the gain falls away fast; miss it by much and the pulls fight and the bell hangs dead.

So freedom cuts both ways, and the tempo is a target you can walk straight past. A dry, stiff gudgeon makes a low, forgiving peak — the bell rings up sluggishly but a rough tempo will do. A true, free-swinging bell makes a soaring, needle-thin peak — it will ring up gloriously, but only for hands that hold the exact stroke. And the gain has no ceiling of its own: on a free bell, held on the beat, the swing climbs until something stops it — the ringer easing off, or the stay at the top of the arc taking a blow it was never sized for.

You do not raise a bell by pulling harder. You raise it by pulling in time — and the freer it swings, the truer your time must be.

Four states, from a dead bell to a broken stay

From hanging still to over the top

  1. Rings up. The pull is timed near the bell's own stroke and the bell swings freely enough to bank it, so the swing climbs, stroke on stroke, to a full round peal — high, steady, and short of the stay. This is the ring the tower wants: raised by timing, not by force, and held where the ringer means to hold it.
  2. Labours. The tempo is right, but the bell is hung too stiff — a dry gudgeon, a dragging bearing — so its freedom is low and the peak it can reach is low with it. Every pull is well-timed and still the bell will not climb: the energy you bank each stroke is bled straight back out by the friction. It rings, but heavily, and never comes up to a true swing.
  3. Won't build. The pull is off the beat. Some strokes help, some haul against the returning bell, and across a peal they cancel; the bell rocks a little and hangs there. No amount of strength saves it — a hard pull at the wrong moment is a hard pull wasted. The cure is not muscle but timing: find the stroke.
  4. Over the stay. The bell was rung up on the beat, freely, and the ringer kept pulling — so the swing climbed past the balance and threw its whole weight onto the stay, the slender timber meant only to rest a set bell, not to catch a driven one. The stay breaks; the bell turns full over. Raised too well is its own danger: on the beat, on a free bell, the gain has no limit but the one you keep.

Three who read the same stroke

The same bell, three trades

The ringer

the sally

Raises the bell by feel, not by force. Takes the sally on the down-stroke, gives a check or a pull to match the bell's own time, and lets the swing grow under the hand a little each pull — hauling harder does nothing but blister a palm if the timing is off. Knows a well-hung bell by how little it asks, and a stiff one by how it fights back. Rings up to the balance and no further, and sets the bell against the stay gently, never on the swing.

The founder

the gudgeon

Hangs the bell to swing free: true gudgeons, good bearings, a wheel sized to the tower, so its freedom is high and a modest band of ringers can raise it. Balances headstock and canons so the natural stroke is slow and even, the same each swing. Knows that a free bell is a glorious bell and a demanding one — the truer he hangs it, the finer the stroke the ringers must keep — and sizes the stay for a set bell, warning that it will not catch one driven past the top.

The physicist

the curve

Sees a driven pendulum and reads the whole tower off one curve. Writes the settled swing as the static pull times 1 ⁄ √((1 − r²)² + (r ⁄ Q)²), notes the peak near r = 1 of height ≈ Q and width ≈ 1 ⁄ Q, and the build-up time of about Q ⁄ π strokes before it settles. Warns that on the beat the gain is bounded only by damping or by the stay — a resonance you feed and do not check will find whatever gives first.

The bench

Time it. Find the stroke that rings it up.

The bell's natural stroke is 30 a minute; the tempo is your pull's, and r is the two compared — r = 1 is exactly on the beat. The green band on the gauge is a full round peal, the amber is a high swing near the balance, and the oxblood wall is the stay, where a bell driven past the top breaks the timber meant only to rest it. Slide the tempo across the beat and watch the gain rise to a peak and fall away — a free bell (high freedom) gives a tall, needle-thin peak that rings up high but forgives no mistiming; a stiff bell gives a low, broad one that rings up sluggishly but takes a rough tempo.
