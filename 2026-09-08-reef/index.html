reef · noun & verb · /riːf/ — a band of a sail that can be rolled or folded and tied away to shorten it; to take in such a band and so reduce sail; Old Norse rif, a rib or ridge

Reef

A sail is the one engine you cannot make stronger — only smaller.

The push the wind lays on the cloth grows with the square of the wind: a breeze that doubles hits four times as hard, and there is no throttle to ease it back. The only thing in the sailor's hands is the sail's own area — so when the wind gets up, you do not fight it, you take some sail away. To reef is to gather a band of the sail along its reef points and tie it down, and the boat comes upright as the cloth comes down. Reef in time and she stays in the groove she was drawn for, heeled to a steady angle and driving hard. Leave it too late and the same wind lays the lee rail in the water, the helm goes heavy and sour, and the boat rounds up and stops. The wind did the rising; the answer, the one answer, is to give it less to push on.

Where the pressure comes from

A force that climbs with the square of the wind

Stand a sail up in a wind and the air piles against it. The force it feels is the dynamic pressure of the moving air, ½ρV², laid across the whole area of the cloth: double the wind and the pressure quadruples, treble it and the push is nine times over. This is the term that runs away from a crew. A boat that is comfortable in fifteen knots is carrying a little over half the load it will meet at twenty, and past thirty the same full mainsail is trying to knock her flat.

You cannot turn the wind down, and you cannot make the sail hold more. The only number you own is how much cloth you show it.

Most of that force does not drive the boat forward — it leans her over. The sail's push acts high up, at its centre of effort, and the hull resists low down at the ballast, so the two make a couplet that heels her. A little heel trims the boat and is fast; too much spills the drive, drags the rail, and turns the rudder into a brake. Between the two is a narrow band — the groove — and the whole craft of reefing is keeping the boat in it as the wind comes and goes.

The one relation it embodies

The heel is where two moments agree

Let the wind lay a heeling moment on the rig, and the hull answer with a righting moment. The boat turns until the two are equal, and that steady heel comes out of the balance with almost nothing left in it:

  • The heeling moment — M_h = ½ρ · A · C · V² · h · cos²θ. Air density, sail area A, a force coefficient, the wind squared, and the height h of the centre of effort. It eases as cos²θ when she lies over — but it climbs with V², and that is the runaway.
  • The righting moment — M_r = Δg · GM · sinθ. The boat's weight times the metacentric height GM — how stiffly she stands up — times sinθ. All the boat has to answer with, and past a point it stops growing: that is the rail going under.
  • The balance — set M_h = M_r and, with R = M_h0 ⁄ (Δg·GM) the ratio at upright, the heel is sinθ = (√(1 + 4R²) − 1) ⁄ 2R. Small wind, small heel; as V climbs, θ walks out toward the deck edge.

Reefing is the one move that reaches into M_h and pulls it down. Take a band out of the sail and A drops — and the head of the sail comes with it, so h drops too, and the heeling moment falls faster than the area alone. The wind is untouched. You have simply handed it less cloth on a shorter lever, and the balance settles back to a heel the hull can carry all day.

Nothing here is about strength. It is about giving the wind exactly as much sail as the boat's own stiffness can stand up under, and not a stitch more.

Four states, light air to a knockdown

From a sail that flogs to a rail driven under

  1. Underpressed. Too little sail for the wind, or too little wind for the sail. She sits nearly upright, the cloth soft, making less than she could. In a lull after a reef this is the price you pay for safety — but hold it too long in steadying air and you are leaving speed on the water. Time to shake a reef out.
  2. In the groove. Heeled to the angle she was drawn for. The lee rail runs dry, the helm has a light steady weather bite, and the whole boat drives forward with the wind fully worked. This is the band the sailor steers to hold, easing and trimming and reefing to stay inside it as the wind breathes.
  3. Pressed. More sail up than the wind wants. She heels hard, the helm loads up as the hull tries to round into the wind, and the drive starts spilling off the top of the sail. Still sailing, still safe — but slower than a reef would be, and a gust away from real trouble. The warning to act before the next one.
  4. Rail under. The heeling moment has run past everything the hull can answer. The lee deck goes into the sea, the rudder half lifts out and stalls, and she rounds up and stops — a knockdown in the worst of it. The failure every reef is taken in time to keep off the water.

Three who read the same heel

The same balance, three trades

The helm

the angle

Reads it in the deck underfoot and the weight in the tiller. No one at the helm computes a moment; they feel the boat stand up or lie down, feel the helm go light or heavy, and know to the degree when she has slipped out of the groove. The old rule is the whole of it — the first time you think about reefing is the time to reef — because the thought only arrives once she is already pressed.

The sailmaker

the cloth

Puts the reef where the hand will reach for it. The bands of eyelets across a mainsail, the lines of the reef points, the deep third reef for a gale — all are cut so the sail comes down in known steps, each one lowering the area and the centre of effort together. To reef is not to shrink a sail evenly; it is to fold away its top, where the leverage is worst, first.

The architect

the stiffness

Sets what the hull can answer with. The naval architect draws the ballast, the beam, and the metacentre, and so fixes the righting moment — the whole curve of it, up to the angle where it peaks and falls. A stiff boat carries her sail longer; a tender one reefs early. The reef schedule and the stability curve are the same fact, read from the sail plan and read from the lines.

The bench

Blow it up. Find the sail that keeps her in the groove.

One boat seen bow-on, heeling to the wind: the hull and its ballast, the mast and the sail, the waterline, and the arc of the heel angle she settles to. Pick the boat — a stiff catboat, a modern sloop, a small keelboat, or a tender gaffer, each standing up to the wind differently — set the wind and take in the reef, notch by notch, and watch her come upright as the sail comes down. The page works the balance of the two moments, lights the groove she wants to sit in and the rail-under wall beyond it, and reads the heel, the sail carried, and the wind at which this sail would bury the rail. It opens on a gaffer overpressed in a gale, her rail in the water. Find the reef.

The groove is the band of heel each boat is drawn to sail in — driving hard with the rail still dry. The green band on the gauge is the groove; the oxblood wall past it is where the lee rail goes under. Push the wind up and the heel climbs with its square, running the boat from underpressed, through the groove, to pressed, to the rail in the water. Take in a reef and she comes upright as the sail — and its centre of effort — come down together. Each boat stands up differently: the beamy catboat is stiff and carries sail flat, the tender gaffer heels early and reefs deep.
