The line
draws itself.

Some work doesn't move in straight lines. The book in its third draft. The thesis on its ninth chapter. The renovation. The recovery. The language you've been getting to know for two years. Each day you leave a small mark where the work reached — out past yesterday, level with it, or behind. The graph is the shape of the year.

Slow work is tidal.
Not forward and forward.

The streak app wants a clean staircase up. The to-do app wants today empty. Neither of them knows what to do with a draft that went backward by a chapter, or a week of quiet that the work needed, or the Tuesday when the roof leaked and the page didn't.

Those days belong on the line. Tideline is built for the projects that take a season or three — to mark them honestly, one day at a time, and to let the line be the line.

How it works

  1. 01

    Name one slow thing

    One project. Long. The kind you measure in months, not afternoons. The book. The thesis. The garden. The training plan.

  2. 02

    Leave a mark each day

    Reach, hold, recede, pause, or storm. A word or two for what the work did. Sixty seconds. Closes the day.

  3. 03

    Watch the line

    Each week, the marks join into a strand. Reaches climb. Recedes dip. Pauses leave a soft gap. Storms a darker one.

  4. 04

    Read the year

    On the last day, the strand becomes a small printable map of the project — every mark in its month. That's it. No share card.

The five kinds of mark

  • Reach. The line moved out past yesterday. A new paragraph, a new measurement, a new mile.
  • Hold. The line stayed where it was. You sat with the work and didn't lose ground.
  • Recede. The line slipped. A revision pulled a chapter apart. The tide went back out.
  • Pause. Chosen quiet. A rest day, a Sabbath, a week with the work's blessing.
  • Storm. Life broke in. Not your choice. Not a failure. A mark all the same.

Who keeps a tideline

The novelist in a third draft

The end is somewhere in November. Some weeks the chapter doubles in length. Some it halves. The streak app gave up months ago. Tideline doesn't mind a draft that goes backward.

The PhD student in chapter nine

Eighteen months in. The viva is a hill, not a deadline. The line is mostly hold and reach, with a storm in late February and a long pause for fieldwork. The shape is the work.

The renovator in the bones of a house

Drywall in March. Wiring in May. A summer of recedes when the inspector visited. The strand is a project plan that already happened — and a map of what came next.

The person in a long recovery

The good week. The bad one. The Tuesday that took a Wednesday with it. Tideline is the only place where holding is enough, and storm is a mark, not a fall.
