raise · verb · /reɪz/ — to lift a thing to a higher place, here against gravity and by turning; as the oldest pump there is lifts water not by pushing it up a pipe but by carrying it up a slope, a pocketful at a time

Raise

The same ramp, tilted in a river: turn it, and it carries the water up its slope one pocket at a time.

Lay the thread's triangle down and wind it round a cylinder, as with the bolt — but now tilt the whole screw into the water and hold the casing still while you turn the screw inside it. Each coil of the thread, dipping below the axis, closes off a little pocket — a bucket with no bottom but the tilt. Turn the screw and every pocket walks up the slope, one pitch along the axis per turn, lifting its catch of water a step at a time until it pours out the top. The water is never pushed; it is carried, riding a slope it cannot roll back up. Lay the screw too flat and each turn lifts almost nothing; stand it too steep and the pockets tip their water back down. The water climbs best at a tilt in the middle.

Where the lift comes from

A pocket that cannot be rolled back up

Picture one gap between two wraps of the thread, down on the underside of a tilted screw. Its lowest point is a little sump; water poured into it pools there and sits, because to leave it the water would have to climb — either up the thread to the wrap above, or back over the downhill lip it came in by. While the screw is still, it holds. Now rotate the screw one turn. The whole helix advances along its axis by one pitch, and that sump — carried round and along — arrives where the next wrap's sump used to be, one pitch further up the slope. The water inside has been lifted, and it still cannot climb out. Keep turning and the pocket, and every pocket behind it, marches up the screw, each handing its water to no one, each simply carried.

The screw does not pump water. It makes a staircase of little sumps and walks the whole staircase uphill, so the water rises without ever being pushed.

This is why the screw of Archimedes will lift sand, grain, fish, or sewage as happily as water: it never relies on pressure or suction, only on a pocket that is closed at the bottom by tilt and carried up by turning. It runs slow, it leaks a little back past each lip, it cannot lift higher than its own length allows — and it has been turning in irrigation ditches and ship bilges and treatment plants for two thousand years, because almost nothing can jam a slope.

The one tilt, read three ways

Lift per turn, fill, and the spill

Call the pitch p, the radius R, and the tilt of the whole screw from the horizontal θ. Turn it at some steady rate. The whole machine is these read against one another:

  • Each turn lifts a pocket one step. One full turn moves every pocket one pitch along the axis, which is p·sin θ straight up. Steeper tilt, taller step: the lift per turn climbs with sin θ, and is almost nothing when the screw lies flat.
  • But the pockets empty as you stand it up. A pocket holds water only up to the height of its downhill lip. Tilt the screw steeper and that lip drops relative to the trapped surface, so the pocket keeps less; past a spill tilt θc — set by how the pitch sits against the radius — the lip falls below the water and the bucket empties backward. The fill falls as the lift per turn rises.
  • The most water is raised in the middle. The water delivered per turn is the fill times the lift — a full pocket lifted a tall step. One is falling and the other rising, so their product peaks at a tilt between flat and spilling: the best tilt is θ ≈ arcsin(½ sin θc), a nearly-full bucket carried a useful height. Crowd either extreme and you get little: flat lifts full pockets nowhere, steep lifts empty ones high.

Two thousand years of screws settle around tilts of twenty to forty degrees for exactly this reason — not because a steeper screw could not lift higher per turn, but because its pockets would arrive empty. The optimum is never at the extreme; it is the steepest tilt whose pockets still come up brim-full enough to be worth the carry.

Stand the screw up for a taller step and the pockets tip empty; lay it down to keep them full and the step shrinks to nothing. The art is the tilt between, where a full bucket is carried a useful height.

Four tilts, from a flat carry to a dry one

Around the best tilt

  1. Nearly flat. Every pocket brim-full, but p·sin θ is a sliver — a hundred turns to raise the water a hand's breadth. All fill, no lift. The screw laid almost level in a gentle ditch.
  2. The optimum. Around θ ≈ arcsin(½ sin θc) — pockets still nearly full, each carried a useful step. The most water delivered per turn; where a working screw is set.
  3. Steep, past the best. A tall lift per turn, but the pockets ride half-empty — more height bought at the cost of water. It still raises something, less than the optimum, and strains for it.
  4. Past the spill tilt. θ > θc: the downhill lips have dropped below the water and every pocket empties backward as fast as it fills. The screw turns and turns and raises nothing — a staircase with no steps left to stand on.

The bench · tilt it, and find where the most comes up

Turn the screw and watch the pockets climb

The screw sits tilted in the reservoir below; turn it with Spin and the pockets — the water trapped between its wraps — walk up the slope, one pitch per turn, and pour from the spout. Change the tilt and watch the trade: flatter keeps the pockets full but lifts them barely, steeper lifts them high but empties them, and past the spill tilt they run back down. The gauge marks the tilt that delivers the most; find it, and see the pour run fullest there.

Who reads it which way

One screw, several trades

The irrigator

lift, not pressure

Wants water up a low bank with no power and no priming. Sets the screw at a gentle tilt it can turn by hand or wind, trading a tall lift for pockets that never fail to fill — and that a stick or a fish cannot jam.

The treatment-plant engineer

solids that would clog

Lifts sewage and sludge the one way a pump cannot: by carrying, not pressing. Picks the tilt near the optimum for throughput, because the screw never seizes on what it carries — grit, rag, and all.

The micro-hydro builder

the screw run backward

Lets the water fall down the slope and turn the screw, reading the same pockets as a turbine. The spill tilt becomes a design limit from the other side — too steep and the falling water never fills a pocket to push on.
