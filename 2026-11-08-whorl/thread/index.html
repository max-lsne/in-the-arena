thread · noun · /θrɛd/ — the ridge that winds in a continuous helix round a screw or bolt; from the spun line of a spinner, for the one unbroken coil that runs the length of the shaft — and so, the wound ramp a nut climbs, and the only thing holding it there

Thread

Turn the bolt and it walks up its own slope into a clamp — and stays, held by nothing but an angle.

A bolt is an inclined plane wound round a post. Unwind one coil of its thread and flatten it, and you have a plain ramp: a base as long as the way round the shaft, a rise as tall as one pitch. Hold the nut and turn the bolt, and the nut walks up that ramp — one pitch along the axis for every full turn round — bearing down harder and harder on whatever it meets. Then you let go of the wrench, and it does not spin loose. The whole reason is a single comparison: the slope of the thread against what friction can hold. If the ramp is gentler than friction's grip, the clamp it has built cannot slide it back — the bolt is self-locking, and holds with no catch, no pawl, nothing but its own geometry. Steepen the thread past that line and it backs out under its own squeeze.

Where the hold comes from

A ramp, wound so it never runs out

Lay a right triangle on the bench: a short vertical rise, a long horizontal base, and the sloping hypotenuse between. A block on that slope needs only a gentle push along the base to climb the rise, because it climbs a little for every bit it travels — that is the old bargain of the inclined plane, force traded for distance. Now take the triangle by its base and wrap it round a cylinder so the base becomes one trip around and the rise becomes a step along the axis. The hypotenuse becomes a helix, winding up the post, and it never reaches a top: wrap the next triangle on above it, and the next, and you have a thread that climbs forever in a hand-sized length.

The screw is the inclined plane that someone wound round a post so it would not run out — a long, easy ramp packed into a short, strong turn.

That is the whole of a bolt. Turning the head drags the thread round the base of its triangle; the nut, riding the thread, is carried up the rise — one pitch of axial travel for one turn round, no matter how slowly or quickly you turn. When the nut meets the work it cannot climb further, so instead it presses, and the turning you keep feeding in piles up as clamp force. A fine thread — a shallow slope — multiplies a light wrench into an enormous squeeze, the way a long gentle ramp lets you roll up a weight you could never lift. A coarse thread seats faster but grips with less.

The one triangle, read three ways

Lead angle, friction angle, clamp

Call the pitch p — the step the thread climbs in one turn — and the mean radius of the thread r, so one trip round is 2πr. Those are the two legs of the unwound triangle. Take the friction between the metals, a coefficient μ, and the torque T you put on the head. The whole bolt is these read against each other:

  • The slope is the lead angle. tan λ = p ⁄ 2πr. A fine thread or a fat shaft makes λ small — a shallow ramp, a long way round for a short step. This is the force multiplier: ideally the axial push is 2πr ⁄ p times your turn, so a shallow thread clamps hard.
  • Friction has a slope of its own. tan φ = μ — the friction angle, the steepest ramp a resting block will cling to before it slides. Dry steel is maybe φ ≈ 8–11°; oil it and φ falls toward 6°; grease it less still. It is friction's whole say in the matter, boiled down to one angle.
  • It holds when the thread is gentler than friction. A bolt is self-locking — a clamp on the thread cannot unwind it — exactly when λ < φ. Real fastener threads run λ ≈ 2–4°, far under even oiled steel's φ, which is why a bolt stays tight with no pawl and a jack holds a car on nothing but its screw. Steepen past φ and the load slides the thread back: now it is a thing you must hold, not set.

The torque you need to build a clamp force F is T = F·r·tan(λ+φ) — your slope plus friction's, both fought at once on the way up. To loosen it you fight the difference: T = F·r·tan(φ−λ), and when λ ≥ φ that goes to nothing or below — the bolt pays you to let go. Everything the fastener does lives in whether the lead angle sits under the friction angle or over it.

A bolt is tight not because you cranked it, but because its ramp is too gentle for friction to let the clamp slide back down. The torque builds the squeeze; the angle keeps it.

Four threads, from a holding fastener to a backing-out one

Around the one line

  1. Fine thread, dry steel. A shallow λ ≈ 3° under a dry φ ≈ 11°. Deep inside self-locking: a light wrench builds a vast clamp, and it would take a hard reverse torque to start it back. The everyday bolt.
  2. Standard thread, oiled. Oil drops φ toward 6°, so the margin narrows though the bolt still holds. This is why a lubricated bolt must be torqued to a lower figure — less friction means more of your turn becomes clamp, and more is recoverable too.
  3. Coarse thread, near the line. λ climbs toward φ. It seats fast and grips weakly, and sits on the edge of self-locking — a tap of vibration and it may creep loose. The place for a lock washer, a nylon insert, a drop of thread-lock.
  4. Steep lead, past friction. λ > φ. The clamp drives the thread back the instant you let go — this is a power screw run backward, the ball-screw that spins down under load. Fast and reversible, and it must be held by a brake, not by itself.

Who reads it which way

One thread, several trades

The machinist

fine vs coarse

Picks the pitch for the job: fine threads hold vibration and fine-adjust, coarse threads run down fast and clear dirt. Both self-lock; the choice is grip against speed — the ramp's old trade, in a thread chart.

The structural engineer

torque, not angle

Cannot see the clamp, so specifies the torque that builds it — and halves the figure when the bolt is oiled, because less φ turns more of the wrench into squeeze, and the angle still holds.

The machine designer

hold or reverse

Wants a lead screw that holds its position picks λ < φ; one that must back-drive — a ball-screw, a quick-release — picks λ > φ and adds a brake. The same triangle, chosen to lock or to let go.
