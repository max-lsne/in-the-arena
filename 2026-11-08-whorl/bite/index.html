bite · verb · /bʌɪt/ — of a tool, to catch and hold in the material rather than skate across it; the moment a cutting edge stops riding the surface and starts taking it, the whole difference between a screw that draws itself in and one that spins uselessly

Bite

The same ramp, sharpened: catch the wood with it and it hauls itself in — one pitch a turn, and you never push.

Take the thread's triangle, wind it round a post as before, and now sharpen the leading edge so it cuts. Hold nothing — no nut, no casing — and set the tip to the wood. Turn it, and the lead thread catches the grain and draws the whole tool in, one pitch deeper for every turn, whether you lean on it or barely touch it. This is the screw's strangest trick: a nail you must beat in with a force that grows with every inch, but a screw sets its own feed by its slope and asks only that you keep turning. The pitch is the feed. Too fine a thread skates on hard grain and strips; too coarse a one wedges the wood apart and splits it; and a pilot hole, by removing the core, saves both.

Where the pull comes from

A thread that cuts is a thread that feeds

Drive a nail and you are fighting the wood head-on: every millimetre of depth is crushed and split directly by your hammer, and the deeper it goes the more grain grips its shank, so the force you need climbs the whole way down. The nail has no opinion about how far it travels per blow — that is set by how hard you hit and how much the wood resists, and it fails the moment you stop hitting. Now cut a thread on that shank and sharpen its start. Turn it once, and the thread, biting the wood, cannot stay where it is: the only place the cut groove lets it go is forward, by exactly one pitch, the way a nut walks a bolt. The wood itself becomes the nut.

A nail is driven; a screw is drawn. The nail's depth is bought with force, and climbs; the screw's depth is set by its pitch, and is free — you pay in turns, not in push.

So the feed per turn is the pitch, flatly, and the force that pulls the tool in is your torque turned the corner by the thread — about 2πT ⁄ pitch, which for a fine thread is enormous. A slender gimlet with a fine lead will draw itself through dense oak under a light wrist; that same mechanical advantage is why a wood screw will pull two boards together, crushing the gap shut, long after you could press them by hand. The screw does not care how hard you push. It cares only that it can bite — and that the bite it takes is one the wood can give without bursting.

The one bite, read three ways

Feed, pull, and the two edges

Call the pitch p, the torque you turn with T, and the wood's hardness h. The whole of it is the feed, the force, and the two ways it goes wrong:

  • The feed is the pitch, not the push. One turn, one pitch deeper — feed = p — regardless of how hard you bear on it, so long as the thread bites. This is why a screw countersinks, draws joints, and sets to an exact depth that a nail never can: the depth is counted in turns.
  • The pull is the torque, wound tight. The axial force drawing the tool in is ≈ 2πT ⁄ p — the same slope that clamps a bolt, now hauling the screw into the wood. A fine pitch is a huge pull on a light turn; a coarse pitch a smaller pull but a bigger bite per turn. Force against speed, the ramp's one trade, again.
  • Two edges bound it. Too fine a thread, or wood too hard for your torque, and it cannot cut — it skates, stripping the groove, feeding nothing. Too coarse a bite and the thread wedges the grain apart faster than the wood can bear and it splits. The safe pitch lives between, and a pilot hole — drilling out the core the thread would have to crush — pushes both edges wide, which is why hardwood is always bored first.

It is the same self-locking slope the bolt holds by, turned to a new purpose: there the thread kept a clamp from sliding back; here it keeps the tool from backing out, so a screw stays set under load where a nail works loose. The wound ramp does not know whether it is fastening, lifting, or boring — it only ever trades a turn for a step, and here the step is a bite.

Set the pitch to the wood, not to your strength. The screw brings its own force; what it needs from you is a bite the grain can give — coarse enough to feed, fine enough not to split.

Four bites, from a clean feed to a burst board

Around the feeding pitch

  1. Right pitch, soft wood. A coarse, deep thread in pine draws itself in fast — a few turns to the head, a big bite the soft grain closes around. The lag screw's home: all feed, no fuss, no pilot needed.
  2. Fine pitch, hard wood, pilot bored. Oak, drilled first. The fine thread pulls in hard under a light turn and the pilot hole takes the core, so it feeds slow and clean with no split. Slow, exact, strong.
  3. Too fine, no bite. A fine thread meets hard grain with too little torque: it cannot cut, so it skates — spinning in a stripped groove, feeding nothing, camming its head out. More torque, or a coarser thread.
  4. Too coarse, dry hardwood. A deep bite forced into brittle grain with no pilot: the thread is a wedge, and the bursting force climbs past what the wood can hold. It splits along the grain — the one failure the screw shares with the nail.

Who reads it which way

One bite, several trades

The cabinetmaker

feed to a depth

Counts turns, not force. Picks a fine pitch and bores a pilot in hardwood so the screw feeds slow and exact and draws the joint tight, where a nail would split the rail or stand proud.

The timber framer

pull, not push

Wants a coarse lag that bites deep and fast in soft structural pine, and leans on the self-feed pull — 2πT⁄p — to crush two heavy members together where no clamp could reach.

The driller

the auger's lead

Reads the same thread as the little screw ahead of an auger bit, whose only job is to pull the cutter in at a set feed so the flutes can clear the chip — a screw that feeds a drill, not a fastener at all.
