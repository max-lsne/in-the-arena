Belay · verb · /bɪˈleɪ/ — to secure a climbing rope round a fixed point or through a friction device so that a fall can be held; earlier, to make a running rope fast round a cleat or pin aboard ship; from Old English belecgan, to lay a thing about

Belay

A climber comes off with the whole force of the fall gathering in the rope, and the person below stops it — not by being stronger than a falling body, but by closing one hand. The rope has been run through the bends of a small device, and the bends, or rather the friction of the rope round them, do very nearly all the holding.

It is the same trick a sailor plays on a bollard, brought to the mountain and made small enough to carry. Run a rope round a bend and the tension in it bleeds away exponentially: whatever the fall brings on above, only a fraction survives round the wrap to reach the hand below. The ratio is fixed and simple — the grip you must supply falls by a factor of e^(μθ), where μ is the roughness of rope on metal and θ the total angle the rope is bent through. A little more bend goes a very long way, because it enters through an exponential.

The problem the wrap solves

One hand against a falling body

A falling climber is not a weight to be lifted but an energy to be caught, and caught in a moment. Held straight in the hands the rope would be torn through them, burning and gone, and the fall unchecked. Yet a fall is held, again and again, by a belayer who weighs less than the climber and grips with a fraction of their strength. The task is to turn a hold a hand can actually keep — a firm grip on the brake side, a hundred-odd newtons — into a restraint of thousands, and to do it with something light enough to hang from a harness.

The device does it with a bend and nothing else. The rope enters, turns hard round a bar or through a slot, and leaves; every point of that turn presses the rope into the metal, and the harder the fall pulls, the harder it presses and the more the metal grips back. The grip accumulates round the whole arc of contact, each patch shaving a little more off the tension, so the great force above dies smoothly to a mere pull at the brake hand below. The belayer holds only what is left when the bend is done.

They do not out-pull the fall. They hold the small change the wrap leaves them, and let the bend hold the climber.

The one relation it embodies

Tension dies exponentially round a bend

Follow the rope round the device through a small angle dθ. It presses on the metal with a force set by its own tension, and friction on that patch subtracts a slice of tension proportional to both the tension there and the little angle: dT = −μ·T·dθ. A quantity whose loss is proportional to itself decays exponentially, and integrating round the whole wrap gives the capstan equation:

  • The grip that survives — T_brake = T_fall · e^(−μθ). Whatever the fall, only this fraction reaches the brake hand. The wrap does the rest.
  • The amplification — A = T_fall ⁄ T_brake = e^(μθ). One newton of grip holds A newtons of fall. It depends on only the bend angle and the surface — never on how thick the rope is.
  • The cost of a bend — more wrap multiplies the amplification, it does not add to it; a sharper or extra turn through the device buys a whole factor, which is why a device with two bends brakes so much harder than a bar with one.

Two things set the whole affair. The wrap θ — how hard and how many times the rope is bent — is chosen by the device and how the brake hand holds it: drop the brake hand and the rope straightens toward the pull, the wrap falls away, and the amplification collapses. The surface μ — dry rope on clean alloy, or a wet, iced, muddy rope — is what the day hands you, and a slick rope can quietly halve the exponent and let a hold you trusted begin to run.

Nothing here is about strength. It is about how far the rope is bent before the brake hand catches what is left — and never letting that bend go.

The bench

Wrap it, weight the rope, read the brake

A belay device with the rope run round its bends: the fall comes on at the thick, bright end — the climber's weight in the rope — and what is left runs off thin and faint to the brake hand below. The colour and weight of the rope fade round the wrap exactly as the tension does. Press Weight it to bring the fall force on and watch how little of it ever reaches the brake hand. Why keep it

The hold that lives in the bend

The belay is worth a page because it is the bollard's law carried up a mountain and trusted with a life. Nothing about the device is strong — a scrap of alloy, a rope, a hand — and yet it holds a falling body, because the shape of the path the rope takes turns a grip anyone can keep into a restraint nobody's arm could. It is the same law that lets a bollard hold a ship on a dockhand's tail, and lets a flat belt carry a mill's whole power on a slack side barely tight at all. One equation, three trades.

It keeps company with the other quiet multipliers in this series — the purchase that trades distance for force, the holdfast that grips harder the harder it is pulled, the preload that spends a joint's danger in advance. The belay's trick is the most trusted of them: don't be strong, be wrapped — and never straighten the bend. Weight the rope on the bench, then slick the surface down and watch the brake begin to run — the whole discipline is in one exponent, and one hand kept low.
