Bollard · noun · /ˈbɒləd/ — a short, stout post on a quay or a ship's deck to which a mooring or towing line is made fast, most often by taking a few turns of the line round it; from Middle English, perhaps from bole, a tree-trunk

Bollard

A coaster comes alongside with the whole weight of her drift behind the line, and a single dockhand stops her and eases her in with a light pull on the loose end. He is not stronger than the ship. He has taken the hawser three or four turns round a post, and the post — or rather the friction of the rope on it — does very nearly all the holding.

This is the oldest force-multiplier at sea, and it costs nothing but a bend. Lead a line round a drum and the tension in it bleeds away exponentially as it goes: whatever comes on at the load end, only a small fraction survives round the turns to reach the hand. The ratio is fixed, and beautifully simple — the hold you must supply falls by a factor of e^(μθ), where μ is the roughness of the pair and θ the total angle wrapped. Every extra turn is another whole factor of e^(2πμ). Two turns hold a dinghy; four hold a ship; six would hold the pier.

The problem the wrap solves

A hand against a hull

A vessel adrift does not stop because someone pulls on a rope. Tonnes of steel carrying even a slow drift store more energy than any crew can absorb by grip, and a line held straight in the hands would either tear free or take the crew with it. Yet a ship is held, and stopped, and warped along a wall, by people who could not out-pull a bicycle. The task is to turn a hold a human can actually supply — a steady pull on a tail, a few hundred newtons — into a restraint of tens of thousands.

The bollard does it with a bend and nothing else. Take the hawser round the post and every point of contact leans the rope into the iron; the harder the rope is pulled, the harder it presses, and the more the surface can grip back. That grip is not spent all at once — it accumulates around the whole arc of contact, each little patch shaving a little more off the tension, so the pull dies away smoothly from the great load at one side to a mere tail at the other. The dockhand holds only what is left when the arc is done.

He does not fight the ship. He holds the small change the wrap leaves him, and lets the turns fight the ship.

The one relation it embodies

Tension dies exponentially round a bend

Follow the rope round the post through a small angle dθ. It presses on the drum with a force set by its own tension, and friction on that patch subtracts a slice of tension proportional to both the tension there and the little angle: dT = −μ·T·dθ. A quantity whose loss is proportional to itself decays exponentially, and integrating round the whole wrap gives the capstan equation — Euler's, and Eytelwein's after him:

  • The hold that survives — T_hold = T_load · e^(−μθ). Whatever the load, only this fraction reaches the hand. The wrap does the rest.
  • The amplification — A = T_load ⁄ T_hold = e^(μθ). One newton on the tail holds A newtons of ship. It depends on only the angle and the surface — never on the drum's size, nor on how thick the rope is.
  • The cost of a turn — each full turn adds 2π to θ, so it multiplies the amplification by e^(2πμ). Amplification is not added a turn at a time; it compounds.

Two things, and only two, set the whole affair. The wrap θ — how many turns you take — is the one you choose on the spot, and because it enters through an exponential a little more of it goes a very long way. The surface μ — rope on painted steel, wet or dry, tarred hemp on oak — is the one the world hands you, and a wet or greasy post can quietly halve the exponent and let a line you thought was fast begin to surge.

Nothing here is about strength. It is about how many times you let the load go round the bend before you catch what is left.

Why keep it

The hold that lives in the bend

The bollard is worth a page because it is pure geometry standing in for strength. Nothing about it is strong — a post, a rope, a hand — and yet it holds a ship, because the shape of the path the rope takes turns a hold anyone can manage into a restraint nobody could. It is the same law that lets a belay catch a falling climber on the grip of one hand, and lets a flat belt carry a mill's whole power from shaft to shaft on the strength of a slack side barely tight at all. Euler wrote it down for the capstan; every quay, crag and line-shaft has leaned on it since.

It keeps company with the other quiet multipliers in this series — the purchase that trades distance for force through a tackle, the holdfast that grips harder the harder it is pulled, the preload that spends a joint's danger in advance. The bollard's trick is the plainest of them: don't be strong, be wrapped. Take the strain on the bench, then slick the surface down and watch the tail start to surge — the whole discipline is in one exponent, and one more turn.
