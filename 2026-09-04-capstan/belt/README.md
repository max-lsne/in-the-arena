# Belt

**A small page for the power that lives in a wrap — a flat belt, barely
tight, carries a mill's whole power across a gap and does not slip, because
friction round the pulley lets its tight side stand at `e^(μθ)` times its
slack before it lets go.**

A whole mill once ran from one engine by leather alone — a line-shaft
under the ceiling and, dropping from it to every loom and lathe, a flat
**belt**. Each belt is barely tight, and yet it carries the engine's power
to its machine and never, in the ordinary way, slips. It holds by the same
law as a hawser on a bollard: a band led round a drum can pull far harder
on one side than the other.

## The pitch in one breath

Two shafts turn, feet apart, and the power of one must reach the other. A
belt will carry it — but only if it grips: too slack and the driving
pulley spins inside the belt, all the power lost to heat and squeal; too
tight and belt and bearings are crushed. The way out is not to tighten the
belt but to **wrap the pulley**. Where the belt hugs the rim, friction
lets the two sides stand at different tensions without sliding, and the
allowed difference is not a fixed amount but a fixed **ratio**. Wrap more
of the pulley and the same slack side anchors a far greater tight side —
and so carries far more power — before the belt slips.

## The one relation

Follow the belt round the pulley through a small angle `dθ`. At the point
of slipping, friction can carry a slice of tension proportional to both
the tension there and the little angle: `dT = μ·T·dθ`. Round the whole
wrap that is the **capstan equation** in its belt-drive dress — the
**Eytelwein** ratio:

- **`T_tight / T_slack = e^(μθ)`** — the tight side may stand at most this
  many times the slack before the belt slides. Set the slack side and this
  fixes the greatest tight side the belt will carry.
- **`T_tight − T_slack`** — the *difference* is the pull delivered to the
  shaft; times the pulley radius, the torque. A big ratio lets a small
  slack side deliver a large pull.
- More contact angle **multiplies** the allowed ratio. An idler pressed on
  the slack side, or a bigger pulley, buys wrap and so buys a whole factor
  of carrying power.

Two lessons: the **wrap** `θ` is the one the millwright arranges — half a
turn for an open belt, more with an idler — and a little more transforms
the carrying power; the **surface** `μ` is the one wear hands you, and a
glazed or dusty belt drops it, collapses the ratio, and sets the drive
squealing under a load it carried yesterday.

## The bench

A pulley with a flat belt led round it: the tight side stands at the
thick, bright end and the slack side runs off thin and faint. Set the
**wrap** and the **surface** `μ`, then press **Drive it** to bring the
tight-side pull on and watch how small a slack side anchors it. Below, the
**tension diagram**: the tension decaying round the arc from tight to
