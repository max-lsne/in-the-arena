Belt · noun · /bɛlt/ — an endless band of leather or fabric run round two pulleys to carry rotation and power from one shaft to another; from Old English belt, a girdle, from Latin balteus

Belt

A whole mill once ran from one steam engine by leather alone — a line-shaft under the ceiling and, dropping from it to every loom and lathe, a flat belt. Each belt is barely tight, and yet it carries the engine's power to its machine and never, in the ordinary way, slips. It holds by the same law as a hawser on a bollard: a band led round a drum can pull far harder on one side than the other.

Lead a belt round a pulley and the tension in it changes exponentially round the arc of contact. The two sides are not equal: the side being pulled taut — the tight side — may stand at up to e^(μθ) times the slack side before the belt breaks traction and slides, where μ is the grip of belt on pulley and θ the angle of wrap. The difference between the two sides is the pull the belt delivers to the shaft — so a belt only lightly set up can still hand over a great deal of work, because the wrap lets the tight side climb so far above the slack.

The problem the wrap solves

Carrying power on a band that must not slip

Two shafts turn, feet apart, and the power of one must reach the other. A belt between their pulleys will do it — but only if it grips: too little tension in the band and the driving pulley spins inside the belt, all the power lost to heat and squeal; too much and the belt and its bearings are crushed and worn. The task is to carry as much pull as the machine demands across the gap, on a band tensioned as lightly as will still hold, so nothing is wasted in the grip itself.

The pulley does it with the wrap and nothing else. Where the belt lies against the pulley it presses in, and friction there lets the two sides of the belt stand at different tensions without sliding — the tight side hauling the load, the slack side merely keeping the belt on. The allowed difference is not a fixed amount but a fixed ratio, set by how far the belt wraps the pulley and how well it grips. Wrap more of the pulley, or grip it better, and the same slack side can anchor a far greater tight side — and so carry far more power — before the belt lets go.

You do not hold the load by tightening the belt. You hold it by wrapping the pulley, and let the slack side merely keep station.

The one relation it embodies

The two sides stand in a fixed ratio

Follow the belt round the pulley through a small angle dθ. It presses on the rim with a force set by its own tension, and friction on that patch can carry a slice of tension proportional to both the tension there and the little angle: dT = μ·T·dθ at the point of slipping. Integrating round the wrap gives the capstan equation, here in its belt-drive dress — the Eytelwein ratio:

  • The slipping ratio — T_tight ⁄ T_slack = e^(μθ). The tight side may stand at most this many times the slack before the belt slides. Set the slack side, and this fixes the greatest tight side the belt will carry.
  • The pull delivered — T_tight − T_slack. The difference is what turns the shaft; multiply by the pulley radius for the torque. A big ratio lets a small slack side deliver a large pull.
  • The cost of a wrap — more contact angle multiplies the allowed ratio, it does not add to it. An idler pressed against the slack side, or a bigger pulley, buys wrap and so buys a whole factor of carrying power.

Two things set the whole affair. The wrap θ — how much of the pulley the belt hugs, raised by an idler or by the geometry of the drive — is the one the millwright arranges, and because it enters through an exponential a little more transforms the carrying power. The surface μ — oiled leather on iron, or a belt gone glazed and dusty — is the one wear hands you, and a slick belt drops μ, collapses the ratio, and sets the drive squealing under a load it carried yesterday.

Nothing here is about tightening the belt. It is about how far it wraps the pulley before you ask it to carry the load.

The bench

Wrap it, drive it, read the slip

A pulley with a flat belt led round it: the tight side stands at the thick, bright end — hauling the load — and the slack side runs off thin and faint, merely keeping the belt on. Why keep it

The power that lives in the wrap

The belt drive is worth a page because it is the bollard's law turned from holding to carrying. The band is barely tight, and yet it hands a mill's whole power across a gap, because the shape of the path it takes round the pulley lets one side stand far above the other without sliding. It is the same law that lets a bollard hold a ship on a dockhand's tail, and lets a belay catch a falling climber on one braked hand. Hold, arrest, carry — one equation wears three coats.

It keeps company with the other quiet multipliers in this series — the purchase that trades distance for force, the governor that holds an engine's speed, the fusee that keeps a spring's pull level as it dies. The belt's trick is the most productive of them: don't strain the band, wrap the pulley — and let the slack side merely keep station. Drive it on the bench, then glaze the surface down and watch the ratio collapse and the belt begin to slip — the whole discipline is in one exponent, and one more turn of hug.
