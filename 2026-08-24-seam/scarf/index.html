What carries a span, given a long lap and a peg

Butt two beams and the load falls at the seam.
Scarf them and it never knows the join is there.

Set two timbers end to end and glue them, and you have a beam as strong as one thin line of glue — which is to say not a beam at all: put a load on it and it snaps clean at the butt the instant that line lets go. A scarf refuses to have a seam. You cut both ends to long matching tapers, table them so they hook, drive an iron peg through, and the two timbers run together over four, six, eight times the beam's own depth — so at no one place is much of the load crossing, and the beam carries as if the timber had never been cut. The join disappears into the run of the grain.

A succession done as a butt joint is two clean ends set together: the leaver's last day is the arriver's first, the work touching at a single line. It looks efficient — no wasted weeks of two people doing one job. But a butt is only as strong as the thin seam of glue at the join, and the first real span finds it out. The load that used to run smoothly along the beam arrives at the seam, finds nothing lapped across it, and drops straight through the gap between the one who left and the one who came.

A scarf spends the weeks on purpose. You cut both ends long — the leaver stays on past their last real task, the arriver starts before their first — and you table the two together so they hook, key them so they draw up tight, and peg them so they cannot draw apart. Now the two carry the span together over a long overlap: the leaver still answering while the arriver already decides, the load never resting on one line. When the leaver finally goes, the beam does not even feel it. The grain runs on past the join.

How it works

  1. 01

    Cut the ends long

    A scarf begins by refusing the clean butt. Cut both timbers back to long matching tapers — the leaver stays past the last task, the arriver starts before the first — so there is length to lap. The longer the taper, the stronger the joint. A short lap is barely better than a butt.

  2. 02

    Table it so it hooks

    A plain tapered lap can still slide apart under tension. Cut a step — a table — into both faces so they hook into one another and the joint carries a pull as well as a push. The work handed over with a catch in it, so it cannot quietly come apart while your back is turned.

  3. 03

    Key it up tight

    Drive a pair of folding wedges — the key — into the joint to draw the two tapers hard together, closing every gap so the whole lap beds down and shares the load evenly. The weeks of overlap are not enough on their own; you have to cinch them, make the two actually carry together, not just sit alongside.

  4. 04

    Peg it and plane it fair

    Bore through the lap and drive an iron peg so the tapers can never draw apart, then plane the joint flush until the grain runs on unbroken. A finished scarf you cannot pick out by eye — the beam simply carries, and no one asks any more which end was cut and which was grown.

The four joints

  • Butt. Two ends set together, glued at one thin line. No overlap at all — as strong as the seam and no stronger. It drops the load the moment the line lets go.
  • Lapped. The ends cut to long tapers and laid over one another. Real overlap now, carrying a push well — but a plain lap can still slide apart under a pull.
  • Keyed. Tabled so it hooks and drawn up tight with folding wedges. The lap beds down and shares the load evenly. The overlap made to actually carry together.
  • Pegged. Bored and pegged through, planed fair, the grain running on unbroken. Carries as if the timber were never cut. A handover you can no longer find the seam of.

Who cuts the same joint

The shipwright at the yard

Forty years scarfing oak for wooden hulls, where a butt joint in a keel is a drowning waiting to happen. He cuts his laps eight times the depth and pegs them with the yard's own oak trenails. "A scarf's not a repair," he says. "It's how you make a thing longer than the tree it came from." His joints outlast the boats.

The head who overlapped the succession

The trustees wanted a clean handover — old head gone Friday, new head Monday. She insisted on a two-term overlap instead: she stayed on the hard governance while the new head already ran the school day, the two carrying together. When she finally left, no parent could say what week it changed. The load never dropped.

The surgeon teaching the registrar

He does not hand over a list, he laps a season of theatre: the registrar cutting while he still holds the retractor, then holding while the registrar cuts, the responsibility tabled and keyed so it cannot slide back to him too soon or forward too fast. By the time he steps out, the join carries. The patients never met a seam.

The two who merged their firms

They could have butted the two companies together on a signing date and watched clients fall through the gap. Instead they cut long tapers — six months where both names answered every call, the accounts tabled across both books, the culture keyed slowly tight. The join is pegged now and planed fair. It reads as one firm that was always one firm.
