# Scarf

**A small page for the handovers that matter — done as a long overlap,
not a clean cut and a dropped load.**

Butt two timbers end to end and glue them, and the beam is as strong as
one thin line of glue: put a real span on it and it snaps at the seam
the instant that line lets go. A scarf refuses to have a seam. You cut
both ends to long matching tapers, table them so they hook into each
other, drive folding wedges to draw them tight, and peg them through
with iron, so the two timbers run together over four, six, eight times
the beam's own depth. At no single place is much of the load crossing,
and the beam carries as if it had never been cut — the grain runs on
past the join and no one can find it. The strength is entirely in the
length of the overlap. A butt joins at a line; a scarf joins over a run.

## The pitch in one breath

Most successions are butt joints. The leaver's last day is the arriver's
first, the work touching at a single line — efficient, no wasted weeks
of two people on one job. But a butt is only as strong as that seam, and
the first real load finds it out: the decision that needed the reason,
the client who only trusted the one who left. The load reaches the seam,
finds nothing lapped across it, and drops through the gap. A scarf
spends the weeks on purpose — the leaver stays past the last task, the
arriver starts before the first — and tables and keys and pegs the two
together, so they carry the span side by side over a long overlap. When
the leaver finally goes, the beam does not even feel it.

## The four joints

- **Butt** — two ends set together, glued at one thin line. No overlap;
  as strong as the seam and no stronger. It drops the load the moment
  the line lets go.
- **Lapped** — the ends cut to long tapers and laid over one another.
  Real overlap, carrying a push well, but a plain lap can still slide
  apart under a pull.
- **Keyed** — tabled so it hooks and drawn up tight with folding wedges.
  The lap beds down and shares the load evenly; the overlap made to
  actually carry together.
- **Pegged** — bored and pegged through, planed fair, the grain running
  on unbroken. Carries as if the timber were never cut.

## The four conditions

- **Dry** — just offered up, nothing drawing yet. The overlap cut but
  not closed.
- **Glued** — the faces bedded and taking, starting to act as one.
- **Cramped** — wedged and pegged hard together, the whole lap sharing
  the load.
- **Faired** — planed flush and proven under load, the join gone into
  the run of the grain.

## What's in this folder

- `index.html` — the landing page, with a boatyard scene: two oak
  timbers spanning between posts, cut to long tapers and lapped over a
  tabled scarf, an oak key driven to draw them up and two iron pegs
  through the joint, a load coming down carried across the whole lap,
  curled plane-shavings and a chisel on the bench.
- `styles.css` — an oak-and-iron palette: honey oak and amber
  heartwood, pale sapwood, blue-black wrought iron for the pegs, a
  shaving-cream ground and a seasoned green for the soundest joint.
  Fraunces for the headings, Inter for the body, JetBrains Mono for the
  marks.
- `script.js` — twelve joints on the trestles across a fictional day in
  the boatyard at Ferrymead in late August 2026: a keel scarf pegged
  thirty years and never worked loose, two ends butted on a signing date
  to save six months, a head lapping a two-term succession, a surgeon
  lapping a season of theatre with a registrar.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser. No build step.
