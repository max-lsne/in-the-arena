What holds a wall, given an offset and a plumb line

A stack of bricks is not a wall.
The wall is in the offset.

Lay bricks straight, one directly over another, and you have not built a wall — you have built a row of separate columns, and every vertical joint runs unbroken from the footing to the coping. A crack goes looking for the weakest path and finds a joint that runs the whole height, and the wall splits top to bottom along a line that was never bonded. The bond is the cure, and it is only an offset: lay each brick to bridge the two beneath it, so no joint ever lines up with the one below, and a load pressing down at one point has to travel out sideways across a dozen bricks before it reaches the ground.

A plan where every part rests squarely on one other part is a stack, not a wall. The launch depends on the one hire, who depends on the one contract, which depends on the one client — a single column of joints all lined up. It looks tidy and it stands in still weather. But the ground always settles somewhere, and when it does the crack runs straight down the aligned joint, because nothing was laid across it. One brick slips and takes the whole course with it.

A bond is the same bricks, offset. You lay each commitment to bridge the two beneath it, so no dependency sits directly on one other — the hire's work overlaps two projects, the client is really three smaller ones, the revenue crosses two quarters. No joint runs unbroken, so a load that lands hard in one place is carried out sideways across a dozen bricks before it reaches the footing. The wall can lose a brick and not a course. That is the whole art of the bricklayer, and it is only an offset.

How it works

  1. 01

    Never let a joint run

    The one rule under all the bonds: no vertical joint lines up with the one below it. Lay each brick to break the joint under it by a good lap — a quarter or a half. A joint that runs is a road for a crack; a broken joint sends the load sideways into solid brick.

  2. 02

    Lap each brick over two

    Every brick should bear on two below it, not one. Then no single brick can fail on its own — to move it you must move its neighbours too. Depend on two things for anything that matters, lapped, so losing one only shifts the load, it does not drop it.

  3. 03

    Send headers through the wall

    A wall two skins thick will split into two thin walls unless bricks are turned end-on — headers — to tie the skins together. The hard blue headers do the tying. Turn some work sideways on purpose, across the grain of the plan, to bind the faces into one wall.

  4. 04

    Point it and let it weather

    Rake out the joints and point them with a weather struck finish so the rain runs off and the mortar sets hard over years. A bond does not prove itself the day it is laid; it proves itself in the twentieth winter, still standing where the stack came down.

The four bonds

  • Stack. Bricks straight over one another, every joint aligned. No lap at all — a row of columns, not a wall. A crack runs the whole height along the first joint it meets.
  • Stretcher. Each brick lapped half over the one below. The simplest real bond — no joint runs — but only one skin thick, easily pushed over sideways.
  • English. Whole courses of headers between courses of stretchers, tying two skins into one thick wall. Strong, plain, and slow. The joints break and the skins bind.
  • Flemish. Header and stretcher alternating in every course, the hard blue headers threaded through the face. The strongest common bond, and the one that shrugs off a settling footing.

Who lays the same course

The bricklayer on the old canal

Thirty years laying Staffordshire blue into lock walls that take the frost and the barge-wash and never move. He lays nothing straight-jointed, ever, even a garden wall no load will ever find. "A joint that runs is a crack you paid to build in," he says. His work outlasts the men who commissioned it.

The founder who un-stacked the plan

The board loved the tidy org chart: one head of sales on one anchor client on one renewal. She saw a column of aligned joints and pulled it apart — three mid clients instead of one whale, the sales work lapped across two people, revenue crossing quarters. When the anchor would have walked, the wall only shifted its load.

The ops lead who broke the single point

Every incident review found the same aligned joint: one service, one on-call engineer, one runbook only she had read. So she bonded it — paired the on-call, split the service, wrote the runbook so two could carry it. Nothing now runs unbroken from the top of the stack to the bottom. The three-a.m. page stopped being fatal.

The family holding the smallholding

They never let the farm rest on one crop, one grant, one good summer. Sheep lapped over hay lapped over the campsite lapped over the winter logs, each bridging the others, none load-bearing alone. Bad years come through like settling ground. The wall loses a brick and holds its course, the way their grandparents laid it.
