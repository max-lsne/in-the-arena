# Seam

**Three small pages, made the same morning, for the one law that runs
through three trades: a join carries load only across a long overlap,
never at a single line. Lap it, don't butt it.**

Most days in this folder are one page. Some mornings the idea comes in
threes. A seam is where two things are brought together and asked to act
as one — a seam of cloth, a seam of coal, a welded seam of steel — and
whether it holds has almost nothing to do with the materials and almost
everything to do with how far the two run alongside each other before
they part. Butt two things end to end and the join is a line, and a line
is where things fail. Lap them — let each carry a length of the other —
and the load has nowhere to drop. These three started in different
trades — a rope, a wall, a beam — and they meet here, because they are
all saying the one thing in three materials.

## The three

- **[Splice](./splice/)** — *the join in a rope.* A knot is quick and a
  knot is a lie: it bites the line so hard it keeps barely half its
  strength, and under a shock it can spill and run. A splice takes the
  slower way — you unlay the strands and tuck each one back through the
  standing part, over and under, three tucks and more, so the join is a
  hand-span of woven overlap that keeps very nearly the full strength of
  the rope and cannot spill. A page for the handovers you make by
  weaving the leaver's work through the stayer's, not by tying a quick
  knot and walking away. Knot → Seizing → Short → Eye. Slack → Tucked →
  Set → Fair.

- **[Bond](./bond/)** — *the join in a wall.* Stack bricks straight, one
  directly over another, and every vertical joint runs unbroken from
  footing to coping — a stack of columns pretending to be a wall, and a
  single crack finds the straight joint and splits it top to bottom. The
  bond is the offset: lay each brick to bridge the two beneath it, so no
  joint ever lines up with the one below, and a load pushing down at one
  point is carried sideways across a dozen bricks before it reaches the
  ground. A page for arranging your commitments so the fault lines never
  run straight through. Stack → Stretcher → English → Flemish. Green →
  Set → Pointed → Weathered.

- **[Scarf](./scarf/)** — *the join in a beam.* Two timbers butted end
  to end make a beam that is only as strong as the glue in one thin
  line, and it drops its load the moment that line lets go. A scarf cuts
  both ends to long matching tapers and laps them, tables and keys them,
  drives a peg through — so the two run together over many times the
  beam's own depth, and the join carries as if the timber were never cut
  at all. A page for succession done as a long overlap: the one leaving
  and the one arriving bearing the load together across the joint, so it
  never falls in the gap. Butt → Lapped → Keyed → Pegged. Dry → Glued →
  Cramped → Faired.

## The one law

All three trades learned the same thing, and none of them from each
other. A join fails at a line and holds across an area. The knot, the
straight joint, the butt — each concentrates the whole load onto one
seam, and asks that seam to be as strong as everything around it, which
it never is. The splice, the bond, the scarf — each *spreads* the join:
runs the two parts alongside one another over a long lap, so that at any
one section only a little of the load is crossing, and the seam
disappears into the body of the work. You cannot make a join strong by
making the line stronger. You make it strong by refusing to have a line —
by lapping the two things far enough that there is nowhere for the load
to fall through.

It is the same thing the [plait](../2026-06-28-plait/) knows about a few
ordered strands, and the [purchase](../2026-08-01-purchase/) knows about
turning a small steady pull into a large one, and the
[confluence](../2026-06-20-confluence/) knows about streams that only
become a river by running together. Lap it, don't butt it.

## What's here

Three folders, each a self-contained page — one HTML file, one
stylesheet, one script, no build and no network once it has loaded.
Each carries a small interactive bench seeded with a fictional day's
work in its trade: a rigger's afternoon of splices, a bricklayer's
courses, a shipwright's scarfs. Open any `index.html` in a browser.

*One of a series — a page a day, each built on one old working word and
the discipline hidden inside it. Some mornings, three. 2026-08-24.*
