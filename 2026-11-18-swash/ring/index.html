ring · here — the set of followers placed evenly round one swashplate, each a share of a turn behind the last, so their strokes overlap into a steady delivery; the working heart of the axial-piston pump

Ring

One stroke pulses. Many strokes, staggered, nearly pour.

A single follower on a swashplate draws a clean sine — but a sine spends half its time giving and half taking back, and alone it pulses. Set a ring of followers round the one plate — N of them, each at the same radius but a different azimuth, each a full 1/N of a turn behind its neighbour — and you have a pump. At every instant some followers are driving out while others draw back; connect the out-strokers to a port and their flows add. The sum is almost flat. Almost: a small ripple rides on it, at N times a turn, and here is the quiet rule the pump-builders live by — an odd N ripples far less than an even one. The same lopsided-sum lesson the Cardan joint teaches, run the other way: here many wrongs are arranged to nearly cancel.

The bench

Many staggered strokes, one near-steady sum

On the left, the tilted plate with its ring of followers — each riding the face at its own azimuth, a wave of lift running round the disc. The ones driving outward (delivering) glow; the ones drawing back sit quiet. On the right, the summed delivery against the turn: a band that would be dead flat if the strokes filled each other perfectly, carrying instead a small ripple. The faint curves beneath are the individual contributions that add up to it. Change the count and watch the ripple breathe — deep and slow for an even ring, shallow and quick for an odd one.

How many rods ride the one plate. More is smoother — but parity matters more than count up close: an odd ring ripples far less than the even one just below it. Pumps pick 7 or 9.

Why odd beats even

The one relation, read for its sum

  • Each follower draws the same sine, shifted: follower k is at z_k = r·tan α·sin(θ − 2πk/N). Its velocity, the flow it gives, is the cosine of the same. The ones with velocity outward are on the discharge port; their velocities add to the delivery Q(θ) = Σ max(0, cos(θ − 2πk/N)).
  • That sum of rectified cosines is flat to first order — the mean is N/π times the single-stroke peak — but not perfectly. Where one follower's velocity crosses zero, it drops out of the sum and another picks up; the hand-off leaves a small cusp, and the cusps repeat at N per turn.
  • The parity is the whole trick. With an even ring the followers come in opposing pairs — as one hands off, its opposite hands off too, so the cusps coincide and deepen, ripple at N per turn. With an odd ring the hand-offs interleave, falling halfway between, so they partly fill each other and the ripple runs at 2N per turn and far shallower.
  • In numbers: a three-follower ring ripples near 14%, five near 5%, seven near 2.5%, nine near 1.5% — while an even ring ripples about as much as the odd one two below it. That is why the catalogue is full of 7- and 9-piston pumps and nearly empty of 6 and 8.
  • The tilt sets how much it pours; the count sets how smoothly. Smooth it further and you reach for a flywheel on the shaft or an accumulator on the line — but the ring has already done most of the smoothing for free, at the source. The same plate, split instead into a lift and a lean, becomes a control.

Six pistons and seven differ by one part in seven of iron — and by a factor of five in how quietly the pump runs. The whole saving is in the parity.
