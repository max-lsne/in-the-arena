control · here — the swashplate read backwards: a stationary plate whose height and tilt are carried into the spinning rotor as a pitch for every blade, the two commands a helicopter is flown by

Control

Raise the plate and it lifts. Lean the plate and it flies.

Run the swashplate the other way and it stops being a pump and becomes a command. Under a helicopter's rotor sits a plate that does not spin; above it, the blades do. Push the whole plate up — collective — and every blade, wherever it is in its sweep, takes the same extra pitch: more bite, more lift, everywhere at once. Tilt the plate — cyclic — and now a blade takes more pitch on one side of the circle and less on the other, a single swing per turn. The rotor disc tips toward the low side and the craft slides that way. A blade's pitch as it comes round is just the sum: A₀ + A₁·cos(ψ − φ) — a flat offset with one wave laid over it, a constant plus a first harmonic. One plate, two knobs, read by the turning head as two separate orders.

The bench

A flat lift, a single wave, laid one on the other

On the left, the plate under the rotor: raise it with collective and the whole ring of pitch climbs; tilt it with cyclic and the ring takes on a lean, high on one bearing and low on the opposite. The blades sweep round, each taking the pitch the plate hands it at that azimuth. On the right, that pitch unrolled against azimuth — the flat collective line, the cyclic cosine, and their sum, the schedule a blade actually flies. Set cyclic to zero and the sum is flat: pure lift. Open it and the wave appears, peaking where the plate is highest.

Lift and a forward lean turning

Drag the rotor to turn it, or let it run. space runs or pauses; ← → step the sweep. Set cyclic to 0 for pure lift; open it to steer.

How far the whole plate is raised — the pitch added to every blade equally. It sets lift: more collective, more bite all the way round, the craft climbs.

How far the plate is tilted — the amplitude of the once-a-turn swing. It sets how hard the disc tips, and so how hard the craft is pushed. At 0 the plate is level and lift is even.

Which way the plate leans — the azimuth φ where pitch peaks. It aims the lean; turn it and the high side of the disc walks round the compass.

Why one plate gives two commands

The one relation, read for its parts

  • A non-rotating plate has exactly three freedoms a pilot can set: a height and a tilt (two axes of tilt, read as a magnitude and a direction). The rotating head samples the plate at each blade's azimuth ψ, and height-plus-tilt sampled round a circle is precisely A₀ + A₁·cos(ψ − φ) — a constant and a first harmonic, nothing more and nothing less.
  • The constant A₀ is collective: it lifts every blade's pitch the same, so it cannot steer — it can only change how hard the whole disc pulls. It is the swashplate's stroke offset, applied to all azimuths at once.
  • The first harmonic A₁·cos(ψ − φ) is cyclic: its amplitude A₁ is how hard, its phase φ is which way. One blade, coming round, pitches up through half the circle and down through the other half — exactly the single stroke of stroke, now carried as a command instead of a displacement.
  • The two are orthogonal: a constant and a cosine share no overlap over a turn, so collective and cyclic do not bleed into each other. Raise the plate and the lean is untouched; tilt it and the mean lift is untouched. That independence is why a pilot can hold height and change heading at the same time, on one plate.
  • One honest complication the rotor adds: a blade is a spinning mass, so its flap response to a pitch input lags about a quarter turn — gyroscopic precession, the timing problem again. The linkage is simply clocked 90° ahead to pay for it, so the disc tips where the pilot points. The algebra here — constant plus first harmonic — is untouched; only its phase is pre-rotated.

Lift is the part of the pitch that is the same everywhere; steering is the part that is different somewhere. One plate holds both because a height and a tilt are all a circle can carry.
