swash · noun · /swɒʃ/ — a disc fixed aslant to a turning shaft, so that its spin is read by a follower as a back-and-forth; the swashplate or wobble-plate, heart of the axial-piston pump and the helicopter rotor head

Stroke

Tilt a disc on a spinning shaft, and a turn becomes a stroke.

Set a disc square to a shaft and it is just a flywheel; set it aslant — tilted off square by an angle α — and its rim rides high on one side and low on the other. Hold a follower against its face — a rod kept from turning, free only to slide along the shaft — and as the shaft spins the high side comes round, lifts the rod, and the low side lets it fall. One turn, one full stroke. And the travel is the cleanest thing in the whole catalogue: z = r·tan α·sin θ, a pure sine. Its amplitude is just r·tan α — the stand-off radius times the tilt — and nothing distorts it. Where a slider-crank bends its sine with the geometry of a finite rod, the swash has no rod to bend; it writes the plainest rise and fall a rotation can be turned into.

A faint curve marks the bent sine a short-rodded crank would draw for the same stroke; the swash's own curve sits clean on top of the true sine, never leaning. Why the stroke is clean

The one relation, read for its purity

  • Drop a line from the follower's tip to the plane the plate would fill if it were square. The tilted face lifts that plane by its slope times the across-shaft reach of the tip: z = r·tan α·sin θ. There is no second term — the follower slides straight along the shaft, so nothing foreshortens its travel.
  • That is the whole difference from a crank. A crank's piston hangs off a finite rod, and the rod's swing adds a cos 2θ bulge — the second harmonic — that hurries the near dead end and eases the far one. The swash has no rod; its follower is rigid along the axis, so the bulge is zero and the sine stays true.
  • The stroke — the full travel between dead ends — is 2·r·tan α. Two knobs set it and nothing else: the tilt, through tan α, and the radius, straight. Double either and the stroke doubles; lay the plate flat and it falls to nothing.
  • The follower is fastest where it crosses the middle — θ = 0, 180°, speed r·tan α·ω — and dead stopped at the ends — θ = 90, 270°, where it turns around. Those two dead ends are the swash's version of a crank's top and bottom, but reached on a clean cosine of speed, symmetric fore and aft.
  • Run the plate the other way and the trade reverses: push the follower and the plate must turn — an axial-piston pump drives fluid exactly so. Set a ring of followers round the one plate and their strokes stagger into a steady delivery — the subject of ring; split the plate's job into a lift and a lean and you can steer a helicopter with it — control.

A crank fakes a sine and misses by a harmonic. The swash doesn't fake it — the slant is the sine, drawn once per turn with a straightedge.
