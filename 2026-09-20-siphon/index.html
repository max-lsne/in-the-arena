siphon · noun · /ˈsʌɪf(ə)n/ — a tube that carries a liquid up over a crest and down to a lower outlet, running on its own once filled; the fall from the source surface to the outlet sets the flow, and the atmosphere pressing on the two open surfaces holds the column over the bend up to a fixed height and no more; from Greek síphon, a pipe or tube

Siphon

A tube that carries a liquid uphill, paid for only by the farther fall on the other side.

Fill a tube, hang it over the rim of a full vessel with one end down in the liquid and the other reaching below the surface outside, and it empties the vessel by itself — lifting the liquid up over the crest and letting it fall down the far side, no pump and no hand. It looks like something for nothing, and it is not: it is a falling column routed over a rise, held together at the top not by suction but by the weight of the atmosphere on the two open surfaces. Two heights run it, and they do different jobs. The fall — how far the outlet drops below the source — sets how hard it flows. The lift — how high the crest stands above the source — sets whether it runs at all, because the pressure at the crest falls below the atmosphere by the whole height of liquid it holds up, and when that pressure reaches the liquid's vapour point the column boils, parts, and the siphon breaks.

Where the flow comes from

Uphill, and paid for by the fall

Read a running siphon as one continuous column of liquid with two open ends. At the source the atmosphere presses down on the surface; at the outlet, lower down, it presses up. The liquid in the long falling leg weighs more than the liquid in the short rising leg, and that difference in weight — nothing more — is what pulls the whole column along, up over the crest and out. The crest does not lift the liquid; the fall on the far side drags it over.

A siphon is not a pump. It is a way of spending a fall you already have — the drop from the source down to the outlet — and taking a detour up over a rise on the way, at no cost to the flow but a strict cost to the pressure at the top.

So the outlet's depth below the source is the whole engine. Lower the outlet and the column runs faster; raise it toward the source and the flow slackens; bring it level and the two legs balance and nothing moves. The crest's height changes none of this — a siphon over a tall bend and a siphon over a low one, dropping to the same outlet, deliver the same flow. What the crest changes is subtler and harsher: whether the atmosphere can hold the column together over the top at all.

For at the crest the liquid is at its highest and its pressure at its lowest. The atmosphere at the source has to push the column all the way up to the crest and supply the pressure that drives it on; the higher the crest, and the faster the flow, the less pressure is left at the top. Push the crest too high and the pressure there falls to the point where the liquid boils at room temperature — it cavitates, a gap of vapour opens at the bend, and the column, no longer continuous, simply falls back down both legs. The siphon has not run out of fall; it has run out of atmosphere.

The one relation it embodies

Flow from the fall, lift under the sky

Let the source surface stand a height H above the outlet — the fall — and the crest stand a height L above the source — the lift — with the atmosphere P₀ on both open surfaces and a liquid of density ρ. Take it ideal and frictionless, and the whole of it is three short lines:

  • The flow. Read from the source surface to the outlet: the speed there is v = √(2·g·H), so the discharge is Q = A·v. It is set by the fall alone — the crest does not appear. Raise the outlet toward the source and it slackens; level it and the flow dies.
  • The lift. Read from the source surface to the crest: the pressure there is P_crest = P₀ − ρ·g·(L + H) — below the atmosphere by the whole height from crest to outlet. The siphon runs only while that stays above the vapour pressure P_v, so the crest may stand at most L_max = (P₀ − P_v)⁄ρg − H. The first term is the barometric ceiling H̄ — about 10.3 m for water, 0.76 m for mercury — and the fall draws it down.
  • The prime. Nothing starts a siphon; the tube must be filled first. Once the falling leg outweighs the rising one the atmosphere holds it charged on its own — but let air into the crest, or let the source fall below the tube's mouth, and the column parts and it stops.

So two heights do two different jobs. The fall sets how hard it runs, and may be as large as the drop allows — more fall, more flow, always. The lift sets whether it runs at all, and spends against a fixed budget, the barometric ceiling, which the fall is already drawing down: the faster the flow, the lower the crest it can clear. A siphon cannot be made to lift over any bend you like by pulling harder; there is no pulling, only the atmosphere's fixed push, and past its budget the column simply comes apart.

The siphon's whole gift, and its whole limit, is the same fact: the atmosphere holds the column up, and the atmosphere is finite. It will give you all the flow the fall can buy, and break the moment the crest asks more lift than the sky can lend.

Four states, from a steady run to a parted column

From running to broken

  1. Runs. A real fall, and the crest well under the ceiling: the column is continuous, the pressure at the bend sits comfortably above the vapour point, and the siphon flows steadily and holds its own prime. This is the rig a fitter sets — moving liquid, with margin over the top.
  2. Slack. The outlet sits nearly level with the source. The fall is small, so the flow is a feeble trickle — and levelled exactly, it stops, the two legs in balance. Safe, and barely working: a siphon here is only an awkward filled tube. Lower the outlet to give it a fall.
  3. Strained. The crest is near the barometric ceiling. It still runs, but the pressure at the top of the bend is close to the liquid's vapour point, and a little more lift — or a faster fall, which eats the ceiling — will tip it over. Treacherous: it works on the bench and fails on a warm day or a long draw. Lower the crest, or take a slower fall.
  4. Broken. The crest is over the ceiling. The pressure at the bend has fallen to the vapour pressure, the liquid has boiled, a gap of vapour has opened at the top, and the column has parted and fallen back. No fall relieves it — the fall makes it worse. To save it you must lower the crest, or change to a liquid the atmosphere can lift higher.

Three who read the same tube

The same siphon, three trades

The gardener

the furrow

Empties a rain butt onto the beds and floods a furrow from the ditch, and reads a siphon by its drop. Sets the delivery end well below the water it is drawing so the flow is brisk, and keeps the tube's crest just over the rim, never up a ladder — knowing the flow is the gardener's to set by the fall, but the height over the rim is the sky's to grant. Primes the tube by filling it at the tap, thumbs both ends, and lets it go.

The vintner

the rack

Racks clear wine off the lees without stirring them, and wants the gentlest fall: the carboy set just above the receiving jar so the draw is slow and the sediment stays put. Keeps the crest a bare hand over the shoulder of the vessel, and knows a warm cellar racks worse than a cold one — the warmer the wine, the higher its vapour pressure and the lower the bend it will carry. Never lets the mouth of the tube reach the lees.

The engineer

the spillway

Draws the crest pressure as P₀ − ρg(L+H) and sizes the bend under the barometric ceiling with margin against cavitation — in a siphon spillway, a roof drain, a fuel transfer. Knows mercury lifts three-quarters of a metre and a hot liquid barely at all, so chooses the liquid and the crest together, keeps the velocity head in the budget, and primes and vents the leg before trusting it to hold.
