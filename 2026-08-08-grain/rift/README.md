# Rift

**A rive-r never saws a chair leg or a shingle — they split it, because a split
follows the fibres and leaves them long and strong, where a saw cuts them off
short. But grain slopes, and a split left to itself runs off toward the weaker
side and loses half the billet.**

Wood is a bundle of long fibres, immensely strong along their length and weak
between. A saw makes a straight line by severing every fibre it crosses; a split
makes no line of its own at all — it parts the fibres and runs wherever they run,
in the weak glue between, leaving every one of them whole. That is why cleft oak
outlasts sawn oak in a gate and cleft ash makes a handle that will not break: the
strength is in the unbroken fibre. The catch is the same fact turned against you.
Because the split obeys the grain and not you, and grain is never straight — it
slopes, spirals, dives around a knot — a split left to itself follows the slope,
curving toward the weaker side until it *runs out* the edge and one half of the
billet is firewood.

## The one control

You cannot cut the split straight; there is no line on a froe. You *bend* it
straight. The split runs toward the weaker side, so you weaken the side you want
it to leave — you lever the thick half, putting its fibres in tension, and the
running split swings back toward the middle. So the control has a wall on each
side:

- **Too little steer — it runs out.** The grain's slope wins, the split walks off
  the centre, and it leaves through the edge. Half the billet lost.
- **The true band — rives true.** The bend just cancels the slope, the split runs
  down the centre, and both halves come away long-fibred and equal.
- **Too much steer — it tears.** Bent past what the fibres take, the split stops
  parting and shears across the grain; the face comes up furred and weak.

And the width of the band is the wood's to give. Straight oak rives like a dream
and answers the smallest steer; interlocked elm will not run true at any steer,
which is exactly why it was chosen for wheel hubs that must never split. The
cure for a rift that runs out is not more force — it is more steer, or a shorter
billet, so the slope has no room to run.

The bench uses the geometry directly:

```
run-off = L · tan θ           exit = run-off − steer · pull
```

where `θ` is the grain slope, `L` the billet length, and the steer's pull is set
by the stock's willingness to be led. Run-out when the exit passes the edge;
torn when the steer strains the fibres past their limit.

## The bench

One froe part-way down a billet, from the side: the grain running at a slope, the
started split, and the line it will take. Set the **grain slope**, the **billet
length**, the **steer**, and the **stock**. The page shows the run-off the grain
demands, how far the steer pulls it back, where the split will exit, and the
strain the steer asks of the wood, and lights the **true band**.

It opens on a long billet of sloping grain with no steer — the split running out.
**Take up the steer** — and watch the exit walk back to the centre before it
leaves.
