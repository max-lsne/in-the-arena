# Grain

**Three small pages, made the same morning, on one idea seen from three trades:
every worked material hides a *direction*. Wood, stone, and cloth are not the
same in every line you take through them — they are strong and clean along one
axis and weak and treacherous across it. The craft is not force. It is reading
which way the grain runs and setting your angle to it, because the same material
that carries a cathedral along its grain will split, spall, or sag across it.**

Most days in this folder are one page. Some mornings the idea arrives already in
threes. We say *going against the grain* as a figure of speech, as if grain were
a stubbornness to be overcome by pushing harder. It is the opposite. Grain is
information — a record, laid down while the material was made, of the one
direction it will consent to be worked. A log remembers the way it grew. A block
of stone remembers the way it settled, bed by bed, on an ancient floor. A bolt of
cloth remembers the loom, warp one way and weft the other. Ignore that memory and
no amount of strength saves you: the froe runs out the side, the ashlar sheds its
face in the first hard frost, the beautiful skirt sags into a bag. Read it, set
your angle, and the material does the work for you.

There are three grains here, and each of these pages is one:

- **[Rift](./rift/)** — *the grain of wood.* A log rives clean along its grain
  and tears across it, so a rive-r never saws a fence rail — they split it, and
  the split follows the fibres. But real grain slopes, and a split left to itself
  runs off toward the weaker side and loses half the billet. Read as **the run of
  the fibres**, and its wall is the run-out.

- **[Bed](./bed/)** — *the grain of stone.* Sedimentary stone was laid down in
  beds, and it must go back into the wall the way it lay in the ground — *on its
  natural bed*, load across the layers. Turn it face-bedded, layers parallel to
  the weather, and frost gets between them and sheds the face off in sheets. Read
  as **the lie of the beds**, and its wall is the spall.

- **[Bias](./bias/)** — *the grain of cloth.* Woven cloth is stiff along the warp
  and weft and stretchy at 45° — *on the bias* — so a cutter chooses the angle for
  the drape a garment needs: near the grain to hold a line, on the bias to round a
  curve. Read as **the angle to the weave**, and its walls are a stiff piece that
  will not sit and a bias one that grows and sags.

## The one shape

Fibre, bed, weave — three memories, three trades, and the same discipline in
every one: the material is not the same in every direction, and it has told you
which direction it wants. Take the line it offers and the work goes clean with
almost no force — the split falls open, the stone weathers a thousand years, the
cloth flows to the body. Take a line across it and the same material turns on
you, and strength does not help, because what fails is not the material but the
angle you asked of it. Each page lets you feel where the good line runs and how
narrow the tolerance is on either side of it — and lights the moment the grain
stops being an obstacle and starts being the plan.

Each page is one HTML file, one stylesheet, one script. No build, no account, no
network once it has loaded. The numbers are the real ones: the run-off of a split
against grain slope, the freeze-thaw life of a stone against the lie of its beds,
the give of a woven cloth as `sin(2α)` against the angle you cut it.
