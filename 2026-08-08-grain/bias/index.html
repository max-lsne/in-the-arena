bias · noun · /ˈbʌɪəs/ — the line at 45° to the weave, along which woven cloth stretches and drapes

Bias

Cut near the grain and the cloth holds a line; cut on the bias and it rounds a curve — but cut too much bias and it grows and sags.

Woven cloth is a lattice: warp threads running its length, weft threads crossing them at a right angle, locked over-and-under. Pull it along either thread and almost nothing happens — the threads are already straight and strong, and they simply take the load. But pull it at 45° to both, on the bias, and the whole lattice shears: the little squares rack over into diamonds, the cloth gives, stretches, and — released — drapes into soft folds and clings to a curve no straight-cut cloth could ever follow. This is the dressmaker's deepest lever, and it is pure grain. A waistband, a collar-stand, a straight seam that must hold its shape is cut on grain, near the thread, so it stays put and does not wander. A cowl, a bias-cut gown, a length of binding that must roll smoothly around a tight neckline is cut on the bias, so it flows. And the price of the bias is written into the same shear that gives it life: cloth cut on the bias grows. It stretches under its own hanging weight, the hem drops long and uneven over the days, the seams pucker, and every metre wants a third more cloth to lay out. Choose the angle to the weave, and you choose between a piece that will not move and a piece that will not hold still.

Why the bias gives

The threads do not stretch — the angles do

The threads themselves barely extend; a woven cloth's give does not come from the fibre stretching but from the weave changing shape. On grain, a pull runs straight down threads that are already taut, and there is nowhere for it to go — the cloth is stiff. Off grain, the pull falls across the crossings, and the square cells are free to rack into diamonds, narrowing one way as they lengthen the other. That racking is greatest exactly at 45°, halfway between warp and weft, and falls to nothing as you turn toward either thread — so the give follows sin(2α), zero on grain, full on the true bias.

Give peaks at the bias and vanishes on the grain: give = maxGive · sin(2α). Turn the piece toward 45° for all the drape the cloth can offer; turn it toward the thread for none. The angle is the whole control, and the cloth sets how much it has to give.

Two things set whether that give is right. The curve is the demand: a straight seam asks for no give at all, a gentle drape asks for a little, a tight collar-roll or a bound curved edge asks for a great deal, and the cloth must be turned toward the bias until it can meet that ask. The drop is the cost: the longer a bias piece hangs, the more its own weight stretches it, so a short collar can take full bias for free while a floor-length skirt cut on the bias will grow at the hem for weeks and must be hung to settle before it is ever levelled.

The one choice

Enough bias to round it, no more than will hold still

So the angle to the weave is a quantity with a wall on each side, and the dressmaker sets it deliberately for the piece in hand. Too near the grain and a cloth asked to curve simply refuses: it stands away from the body in stiff planes, wrinkles across itself where it is forced round, and will not lie to the shape. Too near the bias, on a piece that hangs, and the same give that let it flow now betrays it: it grows under gravity, the hem sags long and wavers, the seams draw up and pucker, and the cloth is wasted three ways in the cutting.

Too little bias and the piece will not sit — it stands off the curve in stiff folds. Too much bias and it grows — it sags, wavers, and puckers. Between them it hangs true: enough give to take the curve, little enough that it keeps its length.

And the safe country between the walls is the cloth's to give. A crisp cotton poplin has little give at any angle — wonderful for a shirt that must hold a line, hopeless asked to round a tight curve — while a fluid silk crepe has give to spare and drapes beautifully, but grows the fastest and must be handled with the longest patience. The cure for a piece that will not sit is not to force it — it is more bias, or a softer cloth; and the cure for a piece that grows is less bias, a shorter drop, or a cloth with more backbone.

Four cuts, stiff to sagging

From a collar that stands off the neck to a hem that will not stop dropping

  1. Stands stiff. Cut on the grain and asked to curve: the cloth has no give to rack into the shape, so it stands away in flat planes and wrinkles where it is forced round. Strong and stable — exactly wrong for a curve. A collar that will not roll, a facing that gapes.
  2. Holds its line. On or near the grain, on a piece that must not move: a waistband, a straight seam, a placket. The stiffness is the point — it stays where it is set and carries the load down the thread without wandering. Right, when nothing has to curve.
  3. Hangs true. Turned toward the bias just enough to take the curve, and no further: the cloth racks into the shape, drapes to the body, and rolls round the edge — yet keeps its length, because the give it has is the give the curve asked for and not a scrap more. The cut you set for.
  4. Grows and sags. Full bias on a piece that hangs long: it flows, but it will not hold still — the hem drops uneven over days, the seams pucker, and the lay wastes cloth. Beautiful and untrustworthy; it must be hung to settle for a week before it can be levelled, and even then it creeps.

Three who cut to the grain

The same weave, three trades

The tailor

the roll

Cuts a collar and its undercollar with the grain turned so the stand rolls softly round the neck and the fall lies flat — reading the weave against the curve of the throat — and cuts the canvas beneath on the true bias so it hugs the chest without a wrinkle.

The gown-maker

the drape

Lays a bias-cut gown so the 45° runs down the body, and the cloth clings and flows over every curve — then hangs the made piece for days to let it grow to the end of its stretch before levelling the hem, because a bias hem cut level today is uneven tomorrow.

The binder

the edge

Cuts binding strips on the true bias so a single narrow band will round an inside curve, an armhole, a scalloped edge, lying flat where a grain-cut strip would ripple and refuse — the whole reason bias tape is bias and not straight.
