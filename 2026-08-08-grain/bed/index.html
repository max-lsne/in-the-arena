bed · noun · /bɛd/ — the plane along which a sedimentary stone was laid down, and the way it must sit in the wall

Bed

Set the stone the way it lay in the ground — or frost gets between the beds and sheds its face off in sheets.

A block of sandstone or limestone is not one solid thing. It is a stack of beds — layers of sediment pressed and cemented over ages, strong across their thickness and weak between one layer and the next, exactly like the grain of wood. A mason cutting it into ashlar can turn that block any way they please, and only one way is right: the stone must go into the wall the way it lay in the ground — on its natural bed, beds level, the weight of the wall pressing across the layers and holding them shut. Turn it on edge or, worst of all, face-bedded — beds standing vertical and parallel to the wall face — and you have laid the weak planes open to the weather. Rain and meltwater seep into the exposed bed-ends; a hard frost turns that water to ice, ice is larger than water, and it jacks the layers apart from within. The face does not crumble evenly; it spalls, shedding whole sheets a bed thick, and the carving with them, until the ashlar is a ruin while the stone beside it, the same stone laid right, stands another five hundred years.

Where the beds come from

A stone remembers the floor it settled on

Sedimentary stone was made by falling still: grain after grain of sand or shell settling out of ancient water, season on season, each layer pressed flat by the weight of the next until the whole was cemented to rock. That history is printed into the block as bedding planes — faint, closely spaced surfaces where one season's deposit meets the next — and along them the stone parts far more easily than across them. Split a slab of flagstone and it cleaves along its beds into paving; that same weakness, stood the wrong way in a wall, is the line the frost will find.

Naturally bedded, the beds lie flat and the wall's weight clamps them shut, and rain runs off the top of each course without ever entering a layer. Face-bedded, the beds stand open to the sky along the whole face, and every one is a mouth for water — and where water can sit and freeze, it will lift the layer in front of it clean off the stone.

Two things decide whether an open bed survives. The weather is the count of freeze-thaw cycles — not how cold it gets, but how many times a year the water in the stone crosses zero and back, because each crossing is one jacking blow. The wet is how much water reaches the stone in the first place: a sheltered panel high on a wall stays nearly dry and can forgive a poor setting for a long time, while a plinth in the splash, a cill, or a coping takes the run-off of everything above it and is soaked for the freeze every time.

The one reading

The right way up depends on where the stone must stand

Nearly always the rule is simple: lay it on its natural bed, beds level, and the frost never gets a purchase. But there is a real subtlety that separates a mason from a stone-cutter, and it is the same grain-reading turned around. A cornice, a cill, a coping — any stone that must throw water off a horizontal top — cannot be laid on its natural bed, because then the beds lie parallel to that weathered top surface and the frost peels it from above, delaminating the sill layer by layer. Such a stone is set edge-bedded instead: beds turned on end and running front-to-back, so that water crosses the beds and sheds, and no single layer is left lying flat under the weather.

In the field of a wall, the beds want to lie flat — face-bedding is the sin. On a coping that throws the weather, the beds want to stand on edge — natural bedding is the sin. Same stone, same grain, opposite answer, because the weathered face has moved from the front to the top.

So there is a working country and a wall on each side of it. Weathers true is the setting where the exposed beds meet the weather square-on, water crosses them and runs off, and the stone frets slowly and evenly like every good stone does. Spalling is the near wall: the beds lie open and parallel to the weather, water sits in them, and the face sheets off. And the width of the safe country is the stone's own to give — a dense granite drinks almost no water and forgives a careless setting for ages; a soft, thirsty sandstone drinks deep along its beds and will spall in a few winters if they are laid wrong and wet.

Four stones, spalling to sound

From a face shed in sheets to a stone that weathers a thousand years

  1. Spalling. Beds laid open and parallel to the weather, in a wet and freezing place. Water fills the exposed layers, freezes, and jacks whole sheets off the face — carving and all — a bed at a time. The stone is not weak; it was set to fail, and it fails fast.
  2. Fretting. Beds oblique to the weather, or a stone that drinks less: water gets a partial hold and the face decays faster than it should — pitting, crumbling, losing its arrises — but it does not sheet off. It will stand, worn and softening, and want re-facing within a lifetime rather than never.
  3. Weathers true. Beds set square to the weather so water crosses and sheds, and the wall's weight holds them shut. The stone frets only at the surface, slowly and evenly, gathering lichen and a patina but keeping its line for centuries. The setting you build for.
  4. Sound as the quarry. A dense, unthirsty stone laid right in a sheltered place: it takes on almost no water, the frost finds nothing to lift, and it weathers no faster in the wall than it did in the ground. As near to permanent as building gets — and the reason old work still stands.

Three who read the bed

The same beds, three trades

The banker mason

the ashlar

Marks the natural bed on every block at the bench before it is worked, so it goes up the way it lay in the quarry, and never cuts a face across the beds where the weather will reach it. Reads the block's grain the way a joiner reads a plank.

The quarryman

the split

Splits the bed open along its natural planes with plug and feathers, working with the grain of the rock to free clean blocks, and stacks them marked bed-up so no stone is ever set on its side by mistake in the wall.

The conservator

the coping

Reads a spalling face and names its cause — a stone face-bedded a century ago, now shedding — and replaces it edge-bedded where it throws the weather, so the new stone sheds water across its beds instead of peeling from the top.

The bench

It opens on a thirsty sandstone set nearly face-bedded on a soaked, hard-freezing coping — the face shedding in a handful of winters. Turn the bed to shed — and watch the years run out to centuries.

The sound band is where the beds meet the weather square enough that water crosses and sheds rather than sitting to freeze in an open layer. Turning the bed toward square lifts the years; a harder weather, more wet, or a thirstier stone all cut them. Turn the bed to shed takes the least turn that carries the stone into the sound band for its place; Reset returns to the spalling set it opens on. The lie of the beds is the mason's to choose at the bench; the weather is the wall's to endure.
