The cut costs — plan the kerf in

You cannot cut a board into pieces
that add up to the whole board.

A kerf is the ribbon of wood a saw turns to sawdust with every pass — the width of the blade's own bite. Cut a stick into five pieces and the five pieces never sum back to the stick: five cuts have eaten five kerfs, gone for good. That is the whole discipline of a cut list. Pay the kerf up front, or measure the pieces to the last millimetre and watch the final one come up short.

Two things come off every cut

A piece you keep, and a kerf you spend.

The saw does not divide the board for free. Each pass yields two things at once — one you carry to the bench, one that falls to the floor as dust — and the second is the one every beginner forgets.

The piece — what you keep

The length you meant to make, sawn clean off the stock. This is the part you measured, the part on the cut list, the part that ends up in the work. It is also the only part a hopeful count adds up — which is exactly why the count runs out early.

The kerf — what you spend

The blade's own width, turned to sawdust and gone. It is not waste you were careless with; it is the fixed toll of making a cut at all. Count it once per cut and the board comes out honest. Ignore it, and the toll comes due at the far end, on the piece you had no slack for.

The kerf is a choice, not a constant

A finer blade spends less — and cuts slower.

Kerf is set by the tool. A ripping blade tears a wide road through the wood and wastes it fast; a fine-tooth blade leaves a hair of a line but crawls. Which overhead you pay is something you get to pick — as long as you pick before you lay out the cuts, not after.

  1. Rip blade — ~4 mm. A wide, fast kerf for tearing down the length of a board. Cheap on time, dear on material; ten cuts and you have lost a whole piece to dust.
  2. Crosscut — ~3 mm. The everyday kerf. Wide enough to clear, narrow enough to plan around. Most cut lists are reckoned at this width.
  3. Fine-tooth — ~2 mm. A slower blade that leaves a cleaner line and spends less stock. Worth it when the board is short and every millimetre is spoken for.
  4. Hand saw — ~1 mm. A thin, patient kerf. You pay in effort what you save in wood; good for one careful cut, punishing for forty.
  5. Near-zero — a hair. The kerf you wish you had, and never quite do. Even a laser leaves a scorch; there is no cut that costs nothing.

Width here stands for whatever overhead your own cuts carry — the setup, the switch, the fee, the sawdust. Pick the blade that fits the board. There is no blade with no kerf.

The whole of the method

Set the stock. Lay out the pieces. Read the offcut.

  1. 1

    Set your stock and your blade

    Start from what you actually have — one board of a fixed length — and the kerf your saw will cut. The board does not grow to fit the plan; the plan has to fit the board.

  2. 2

    Lay out the pieces you need

    Name each length and set it down on the stock in order. As you add pieces the bench sums them with a kerf between each — so what you see is the real reach of the cut, not the hopeful one.

  3. 3

    Read the offcut — and mind the kerf

    What is left over is the offcut, and it is the truth. While it holds, the board is enough. The moment it goes red you have laid out more than the stock will give, and the last piece will not come off.

The bench

Who keeps one

For anyone dividing a fixed thing into named pieces.

The maker

A cut list that adds up on paper still comes up short on the saw. Reckon every board with a kerf per cut, and you buy the right length the first time instead of one board too few.

The planner

A day is a board and every task is a piece — but the switch between them is a kerf. Block a day to the minute with no kerf between the blocks and the last one never happens. Leave the sawdust in.

The budgeter

Split a fixed sum into line items and each transfer takes its fee off the top. The stock is the balance, the fee is the kerf; plan the pieces to the whole and the last one bounces.

Kerf — the slot a saw cuts, and the width of material it removes in cutting; the stock a blade turns to dust on every pass, which a cut list must be reckoned against or fall short.
