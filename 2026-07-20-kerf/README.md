# Kerf

**You cannot cut a board into pieces that add up to the whole board. The blade
eats a little of every cut — count the kerf, or the last piece falls short.**

A **kerf** is the slot a saw leaves, and the width of material it removes to
leave it: the ribbon of wood the blade turns to sawdust on every pass. It is
easy to forget because it is not there to see — it left as dust. But it is real,
and it is fixed: make a cut, spend a kerf. Cut one board into five pieces and you
have made five cuts and spent five kerfs, and the five pieces will never, ever
sum back to the board you started with. That gap is not sloppiness. It is the
toll of cutting at all.

Kerf is a small page built around that toll. It is a bench for one board of a
fixed length and a cut list you lay out on it — named pieces, set end to end,
with a kerf reckoned into every cut. The board does not stretch to fit the plan.
The plan has to fit the board.

> A cut list that adds up perfectly on paper is the classic way to come up one
> piece short at the saw. The pieces were measured; the cuts were not. The kerf
> is what you did not measure, and it always collects at the far end.

## Piece, or kerf

Every pass of the saw yields two things at once, and only one of them ends up in
the work:

- **The piece — what you keep.** The length you measured and meant to make, sawn
  clean off the stock. This is the only part a hopeful count adds up, which is
  exactly why a hopeful count runs out early.
- **The kerf — what you spend.** The blade's own width, turned to sawdust and
  gone. Not carelessness — the fixed cost of making a cut. Count it once per cut
  and the board comes out honest; ignore it and the toll comes due at the far
  end, on the piece you left no slack for.

## The blades

The kerf is set by the tool, so it is a choice — as long as you make it before
you lay out the cuts. Coarsest and fastest on top, finest and slowest at the
bottom:

- **Rip blade — ~4 mm.** A wide, fast kerf for tearing down the length of a
  board. Cheap on time, dear on material.
- **Crosscut — ~3 mm.** The everyday kerf most cut lists are reckoned at.
- **Fine-tooth — ~2 mm.** Slower, cleaner, and easier on a short board where
  every millimetre is spoken for.
- **Hand saw — ~1 mm.** A thin, patient kerf; you pay in effort what you save in
  wood.
- **Near-zero — a hair.** The kerf you wish you had and never quite do. Even a
  laser leaves a scorch. There is no cut that costs nothing.

Width here stands for whatever overhead your own cuts carry — the setup, the
switch, the fee, the sawdust. Pick the blade that fits the board.

## The bench holds a board

The bench is one board. Add pieces and it lays them out end to end, a thin band
of sawdust for every kerf, and the offcut at the tail. The offcut is the truth:
while it holds, the board is enough; the moment it goes red, the cut list has run
past the end of the stock and the last piece will not come off. There is no
measuring your way out of that — the board is as long as it is.

## The sawdust

Cutting raises dust, and the dust is the fastest read on the page. The air over
the bench hangs **clear** on a clean run with the kerf paid for and the offcut
holding, **thickens to a tan haze** as the cuts pile up and the board runs short,
and turns **grey-red** the moment the list overruns the stock. You do not have to
read the numbers to know the run is in trouble — the air over the bench tells you.

## The shape of the page

Hero, the two things a cut leaves, the ladder of blades, three steps, and then
the bench itself: a board with a stock length and a kerf, a cut list you lay out
on it, and a reading that sums the pieces *with* a kerf per cut. Set the stock,
add pieces, size each one, and read the offcut. No account, no cloud, no build
step. Open `index.html` in a browser; that is the whole of it.
