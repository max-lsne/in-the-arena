Scarf

A joint is only as strong as the way it turns a pull into a slide.

Stand two timbers end to end and glue them — a butt joint — and you have asked the glue to do the one thing it does worst: hold in tension, straight across the grain, over nothing more than the bare cross-section. Pull, and it lets go almost at once. A scarf refuses the question. It cuts a long shallow taper in each end and laps them, so the break runs at a slant and the same pull no longer pries the faces apart — it tries to slide them past each other, as shear, spread over a glued face many times the area of the cut. And shear is what glue is for. Make the taper shallow enough and the joint quietly disappears: the member breaks in clear timber, well away from the scarf, as if it had never been cut at all.

Where the load goes

Glue is weak in tension and strong in shear

An adhesive line has two ways to fail, and they are not close. Pull the two faces apart — tension, or its worse cousin peel, where the parting starts at one edge and unzips — and even a good glue gives at a low stress. Slide the faces along each other — shear — and the same glue holds several times harder, because now the whole bonded area is working at once instead of a crack front eating along an edge. Every joint that lasts is built on that gap: it arranges the geometry so the load arrives as shear and almost never as tension.

You cannot make the glue stronger. You can only decide, by the angle of a cut, whether it is asked to hold the load it is good at or the load it is bad at.

The butt joint gets it exactly wrong — pure tension on the smallest possible face. The scarf gets it right twice over. Slanting the cut resolves the axial pull into a large shear component along the face and only a small tension across it; and slanting it stretches the face itself, so both of those stresses are divided over more glue. Shallow the taper and the tension term collapses while the shear stays well within reach. There is no new material and no stronger glue anywhere in this — only a pull, redirected until it lands where the joint can carry it.

The one relation it embodies

A shallow cut trades length for strength

Take a member of cross-section A under an axial pull F, so the timber carries a stress σ = F ⁄ A. Cut the scarf at a slope of 1 : N — one across the thickness for N along the length — and let φ be the angle the cut makes with the axis, so tanφ = 1 ⁄ N. Then the whole joint falls out of one resolution of that stress onto the slanted face:

  • The face — a plane at angle φ across the bar has area A ⁄ sinφ = A·√(1 + N²). A 1:8 scarf glues eight times the cross-section; that alone is most of the win.
  • The two stresses — resolve σ onto the face and it splits into a shear τ = σ·sinφ·cosφ = ½σ·sin2φ that slides the faces, and a peel σ_n = σ·sin²φ that pries them apart. Shallow the scarf — small φ — and the peel term dies as sin²φ while the shear falls too; steepen it to the butt (φ = 90°) and all of it lands as pure tension, σ_n = σ.
  • The balance — the joint reaches the timber's own strength when the glue outlasts the wood: when the shear at the wood's failing stress is still under the glue's, σ_wood·N ⁄ (1 + N²) ≤ τ_glue. The steepest — shortest — scarf that clears this is the answer the trades reach for, and for real wood and glue it lands between 1:8 and 1:12.

So the one dial is the length of the taper. A steep, short scarf saves timber and time and fails in the glue; a long, shallow one wastes a hand's width of good wood and develops the full strength of the piece. The cut does not add anything. It simply keeps handing the glue a gentler and gentler version of the same pull, until the weakest thing left in the member is the timber itself.

Nothing here is about a better adhesive. It is about giving the glue exactly the kind of load it can hold, and dividing that load over exactly as much face as the piece can spare.

Four states, a clean break to a joint that lets go

From timber-strong to sheared clean off

  1. Timber-strong. The taper is shallow enough that the glue outlasts the wood. Pull to failure and the member breaks in clear timber, off in the grain somewhere, not on the joint — the scarf has vanished into the piece. This is the whole aim, and the shortest scarf that reaches it is the one the trade cuts.
  2. Glue-limited. Holding, and holding well, but the glue is now the weakest link. The joint carries the load in front of it, yet pull harder and it will let go before the wood does — its failing load sits below the timber's. Sound for the duty it was cut for, but not the full strength of the piece.
  3. Peeled. The scarf is too steep, or square to the pull. The tension across the face has climbed past what the glue holds, and the faces are prised apart — the low, cheap failure the butt joint hands you almost for free. Unzips from an edge and runs.
  4. Shorn. Steeper still, or simply overloaded. The shear along the face has run past the glue line and the two tapers slide clean past each other. The joint parts along the cut and the timber on either side is left whole and useless. The failure a longer taper is cut to keep off.

Three who cut the same slope

The same taper, three trades

The boatbuilder

the length

Scarfs a plank or a spar and needs it to bend round the hull as if it were one length of timber. Cuts long — 1:8 for a plank, 1:12 for a mast — because a spar in a seaway is worked in bending as well as pull, and only a shallow taper carries that without a hard spot at the joint. Planes the two bevels together on a jig so they mate over their whole face, and the finished scarf is invisible under paint.

The joiner

the face

Lengthens a moulding or a run of skirting and cares most about the glue face itself — that it is flat, clean, and fully wetted, because a shallow scarf is nearly all glue area and a starved or dusty patch shows up as the one place it eventually opens. Reaches for the long taper and the slow glue, clamps it the length of the lap, and lets the joint borrow the strength of the whole run.

The engineer

the numbers

Reads the same joint as σ, τ, and a ratio. Knows the peel term dies as sin²φ and the shear as sinφcosφ, sets the scarf so the glue's shear allowable is never reached before the timber's tension one, and adds nothing for luck that the geometry has not already earned. To draw the slope is to choose, in one number, whether the wood or the glue is the weakest thing in the piece.
